# Moving and Measuring

Movement Module

You can measure Movement distances immediately after declaring any Skill with the Movement Label and before determining where the Trooper ends his Movement, always measuring from the same point on the base's outer edge and underside.

The sequence of events is:

1. The player declares a Skill with the Movement Label.

2. Measure to find out which locations the Trooper could reach with that Skill.

3. Declare the final location, and the exact route that the Trooper is taking to reach it.

4. Move the Trooper to the final location.

### Horizontal Measurement (Complex Paths)

The player measures to see how far the Trooper can reach and establishes the route they will take. After determining the end point of the route, the player places the Model or Marker there. If necessary, the player can mark the exact location of the end point and use the Model or Marker at other points along the route to check LoF and AROs. When everything is decided, the Model or Marker must be placed at the end of the route.

Route and final position of the Model/Marker

The Model/Marker is placed along the route to check LoF and AROs.

Some players, instead of establishing the full route of the Trooper from the get-go, prefer to make several measurements from one point on the game board to the next, placing the Trooper each time at each of these points in their route. For example, they would move the Trooper up to a corner, make a new measurement, and then move around it and go to the next corner and place the Model there. This is not the standard way of measuring Movement for Infinity, but it is acceptable as long as the player places the Trooper right behind the point where they are pausing the movement, so they do not gain extra Movement distance.

### Vertical Measurement (Ladders and Vertical Surfaces)

Those pieces of scenery representing stairs and ladders allow Troopers to treat those vertical or diagonal surfaces as a horizontal surface. Therefore, a Trooper may use any Skill or ARO with the Movement Label without declaring Jump or Climb, and without the restrictions of Jump or Climb. ## FAQs

Q: If a Trooper enters Prone State at the end of their move, how does this affect the distance they can move?

A: It doesn’t. They move normally and then finish the end of the move prone.


**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**

Distances are measured between the *closest points of silhouettes*, not bases, even though using the base is recommended for speed. This matters most when:
- Models are on different elevations
- Partial silhouette exposure matters for LoF
- Engaged State or base contact is in question

The base is a convenience tool, not a replacement for the silhouette rules.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
Movement does not “snap” to surfaces or edges. If a Trooper’s declared movement path ends with their silhouette not fully supported (for example, overhanging a ledge), the placement is illegal and must be corrected to the last legal point along the declared route.

This is often missed when players try to gain extreme corner cover or vertical positioning.

---

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
Troopers with a Skill using the Movement Label effectively gain 360° LoF *along their route*. This has two commonly missed implications:
- A Trooper may generate AROs from enemies behind them during movement
- A Trooper may draw LoF to targets they could not see at the start or end position

This makes movement inherently reactive and dangerous, even if the final position appears safe.

---

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
ARO timing and movement interaction is one of the most misunderstood areas. Reactive players may declare AROs:
- As soon as the first Short Skill is declared
- Or later, if the second Short Skill creates a new valid ARO opportunity

Because of this, the *exact declared route* matters more than the final position. A poorly planned movement path can generate AROs even if the Trooper “ends safe.”

---

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
When a Trooper moves, enemy Troopers may choose *any point along the movement path* as the reference point for their ARO (for range, cover, and LoF), as long as that point is valid. This allows reactive players to:
- Choose optimal range bands
- Deny cover by selecting an exposed moment
- Avoid negative MODs that would exist at the final position

Movement is therefore a negotiation of exposure, not just displacement.


**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
You cannot measure movement to “test” whether a Skill is legal. If a Skill’s Requirements are not met when checked, the Trooper performs Idle instead. This commonly affects:
- Movement that would require Climb or Jump
- Attempted base contact that fails
- Movement paths that illegally cross impassable terrain

The Order is still spent.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
Vertical movement is fully real movement. Horizontal distance does not replace true path length. Moving up, down, or diagonally across elevations consumes real inches along the declared route. This makes:
- Rooftop hopping expensive
- Vertical board design tactically meaningful
- Climb and Jump risk-heavy but powerful


**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
Strategically, good movement in Infinity is about *controlling opponent choices*, not just positioning. A strong movement declaration:
- Forces suboptimal ARO range choices
- Limits ARO options to Dodge or Reset
- Exploits 360° movement LoF to clear corners safely
- Baits AROs that can later be punished

Bad movement declarations hand your opponent perfect information and optimal reactions.


**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
Tactically, experienced players often declare movement paths that are intentionally conservative, even if longer in Orders. Preserving uncertainty and denying clean AROs is frequently worth more than raw distance efficiency.

Infinity rewards precision, but it punishes greed.
--

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
Finally, fair play matters. The rules explicitly encourage opponents to help verify LoF and measurements *after declaration*. This is not a weakness of the system; it is a social contract that keeps Infinity fast, lethal, and tactical instead of adversarial and slow.

Clear declarations and mutual verification make movement one of the deepest mechanics in the game.
