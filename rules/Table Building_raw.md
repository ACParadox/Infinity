# Infinity N5 Table Design Knowledge - PART 1
Balanced, Fun, and Tactically Diverse Game Tables
300 points, 4x4 table, standard deployment (no diagonal layouts)
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 1. Design Goals

A good Infinity table should:
- Support all weapon ranges and unit types
- Encourage movement, problem solving, and counterplay
- Avoid dominant firelanes or single correct strategies
- Reward order efficiency and positioning
- Make narrative sense while remaining mechanically fair

Infinity tables are urban combat puzzles, not symmetrical battlefields.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 2. Core Assumptions

- Table size is 48 by 48 inches (smaller/bigger tables will modifiy these tips a bit, but core ideas are retained)
- Game size is 300 points
- Standard opposing deployment zones
- Up to 15 troopers per side
- TAGs are expected and supported

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 3. The 9-4-Scatter Baseline Layout

Divide the table into a 3 by 3 grid.
Each square is 18 by 18 inches.

Place 9 main buildings:
- One building or building clump per square
- Footprints between 8x8 and 12x12 inches
- No playable interiors
- Vary roof heights between 1 and 3 stories
- Bias taller buildings toward the center

Optional:
- One central objective room if required by the mission

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 4. Central Structure Bias
- Three large buildings should dominate the midfield
- Objectives should be placed on or next to terrain
- Objectives should never be in open ground
- No long-range firelane should directly cover an objective

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 5. Firelane Philosophy

General rules:
- No deployment zone to deployment zone firelanes
- Longest straight firelane should be about 65 to 75 percent of table length
- Mix short, medium, and long engagement distances

Corridors:
- Aim for 2 to 3 main corridors across the table
- Corridors should be contestable and have multiple entry points

Snipers:
- Provide a good but not dominant sniper position
- Only one deployment zone should have a strong sniper perch
- Strong AROs without cover are acceptable

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 6. Deployment Zones
Each deployment zone should:
- Fit 15 models comfortably
- Include multiple pieces of solid cover
- Contain scatter to block strafing runs
- Include space for S7 models

Avoid:
- DZ to DZ firelanes
- Entire DZ exposed to a single flank

Deployment zones must be planned deliberately.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 7. Verticality and Asymmetry

Height:
- Prefer a maximum of 3 stories
- About 25 percent of first-level footprint should have higher levels
- Include ladders, stairs, catwalks, and vaulting access

Asymmetry:
- Equal terrain volume, not equal shapes
- Different distributions in each table quarter
- Asymmetry increases replayability

There must be multiple ways to interact with higher levels.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 8. TAG and Large Model Considerations

- Leave 2 to 3 inch gaps between building clumps
- TAGs should move through streets and vault obstacles
- Avoid TAGs on rooftops

Include 4 to 6 TAG-scale scatter pieces:
- Vehicles
- Cargo
- Large barricades

TAGs should project control, not dominate automatically.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 9. Secondary Scenery Pieces

Add 4 medium scenery pieces:
- Size around 4x4 to 5x5 inches
- Place them at the intersection lines between main buildings
- Purpose is to break firelanes and anchor midfield fights

Examples:
- Parked or crashed vehicles
- Machinery
- Small plazas
- Checkpoints

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 10. Scatter Terrain Density

Ground-level scatter:
- Provide cover every 4 to 6 inches
- Allow models to move cover to cover
- Favor active play without removing AROs

Rooftop scatter:
- Essential for balance
- Pull scatter back from roof edges
- Allow sniper versus sniper play

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 11. Board Edges
- At least 2 or 3 table edges should include solid terrain
- Prevent open flank firelanes
- Support AD troops and infiltrators

Avoid always-blocked or always-open edges.
Edges should present risk, not certainty.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 12. No Dead Ground
Every part of the table should:
- Offer cover
- Offer line of fire
- Or act as a transition zone

If players never go somewhere, redesign it.
---

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 13. Narrative vs Gameplay Tables
Narrative tables:
- Emphasize story
- May intentionally limit some tactics
- Useful for campaigns and asymmetric scenarios

Gameplay tables:
- Emphasize mechanical balance
- Ensure all weapons and unit types can matter

Both approaches are valid.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 14. Terrain Rules and Optional Mechanics

Use sparingly:
- Low visibility zones for bushes or heavy foliage
- Saturation zones for debris or clutter
- Difficult terrain for rubble or mud
- Strong ARO positions without cover

Terrain rules enhance terrain but never replace it.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 15. House Rules and Iteration

Players should:
- Review tables regularly
- Adjust terrain or rules if one playstyle dominates

Examples:
- Line of fire only into, not through, windows
- Restricted line of fire through railings
- Base contact required for certain openings

Revisit house rules as terrain collections evolve.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## 16. Final Checklist

Before playing, confirm:
- No DZ to DZ firelanes
- 2 to 3 usable corridors
- Cover every 4 to 6 inches
- Vertical access is contestable
- TAGs can move meaningfully
- Objectives are protected
- Board edges are interactive



# Infinity N5 Table Design Knowledge - PART 2
------------------
## Core Philosophy

- The table exists to maximize fun and interaction.
- Terrain should reward engagement, not punish mistakes.
- A good table balances gameplay clarity with narrative feel.
- Think of the table as halfway between a chessboard and a movie set.
- Tables should not lock players out of the game after deployment or Turn 1.

## First Principles for Beginners

- Place large buildings first.
- Decide desired firelane lengths before adding scatter.
- Medium firelanes are preferred; long firelanes must be breakable.
- Always check sightlines from multiple angles, not just one direction.
- Avoid horizontal firelanes that cut through deployment zones.

## Building Count and Density

- For standard CB-style terrain:
  - 6–7 large buildings
  - 4 medium buildings
  - 4 small buildings
- Too many buildings without scatter creates a grid and plays poorly.
- Gaps between buildings should generally allow a hand to fit for ease of play.

## Verticality

- Infinity benefits strongly from height variation.
- Three levels of height is ideal.
- Levels do not need to be full floors; partial height variation is enough.
- Avoid all rooftops being the same height.
- Avoid two-plane tables (everyone on ground vs everyone on roof).

## Deployment Zones (DZ)

- DZs must be planned deliberately.
- Each DZ must:
  - Fit 15 models comfortably
  - Accommodate TAGs without blocking S2 placement
  - Have at least one building touching the back table edge
- At least one, preferably two, terrain pieces should touch the DZ backline.
- DZs should integrate naturally into the table, not feel pasted on.
- Avoid dominant sniper positions inside DZs.
- DZs should allow defense but not total safety.

## Objectives and HVT Placement

- Objectives should be placed before buildings when possible.
- Objectives must not be in open ground.
- Objectives must not be visible from across the table.
- Missions like Unmasking require:
  - Multiple valid HVT placements per player
  - Defensive options that are not identical on both sides
- Slight imbalance between DZs is desirable to make turn choice meaningful.

## Firelanes

- No deployment zone to deployment zone firelanes.
- Long firelanes should be segmented into slices.
- Sniper lanes should always have:
  - A close-range access path
  - A way to engage within 16 inches
- If a sniper dominates beyond 32 inches uncontested, the table has failed.
- Every strong ARO should have an order-intensive but viable counterplay.

## Scatter Terrain

- Scatter is essential and often underused.
- Scatter allows odds manipulation, cautious movement, and problem solving.
- Scatter spacing guideline:
  - One piece every 4–6 inches
- Scatter must allow models to chain cover across the table.
- Scatter should:
  - Touch buildings
  - Create nooks and irregular shapes
  - Break both horizontal and vertical sightlines
- Lack of scatter cannot be fixed by adding more buildings.

## Rooftop Scatter

- Rooftop scatter is mandatory for balance.
- Scatter should be pulled back from roof edges.
- Allows rooftop firefights without total dominance.
- Prevents TAGs or snipers from seeing entire tables.

## TAG Considerations

- TAGs should be powerful but never table-locking.
- Avoid allowing TAGs to:
  - Sit on the highest point with cover
  - See the entire table from one position
- Test the table with large silhouettes during setup.
- Ensure there are safe advance paths under TAG fire.
- Large bases should be challenged, not made impossible to move.
- Always provide total cover deployment spots for S7+ models.

## Bridges, Stairs, and Connectivity

- Bridges turn rooftops from islands into pathways.
- Bridges allow safe forward movement over walls.
- Stairs should face forward or sideways, not backward.
- Poor stair orientation increases order tax unnecessarily.
- Multiple access routes to upper levels are required.


## Asymmetry

- Asymmetry is critical for replayability.
- Terrain volume should be equal, distribution should not.
- Slightly better AROs, objectives, or TAG positions on one side is good.
- Asymmetry makes first vs second turn a real choice.

---

## Board Edges

- Board edges must contain terrain.
- Clear edges create unfair flanking and AD abuse.
- At least part of 2–3 table edges should be blocked.
- Avoid predictable “always blocked” or “always open” edges.

---

## Cylinders and Shape Design

- Cylinders are not Infinity-friendly.
- Circular terrain increases cognitive load.
- Flat surfaces reduce arguments and speed up play.
- Cylinders often create ambiguous cover and LOF interactions.
- Use angular shapes or irregular blocks instead.

---

## Interiors

- Interiors are generally discouraged.
- Moving terrain during play is a failure state.
- Fully modeled interiors are often unplayable.
- Templates trivialize interior combat.
- Use terrain rules instead of modeling clutter.

## Terrain Rules Usage

- Use rules to represent complexity:
  - Low Visibility for foliage
  - Saturation for dense clutter
  - Difficult terrain for rubble
- Terrain rules enhance terrain; they do not replace it.
- Avoid true line-of-sight trees and bushes.

## Do Not List

- Do not rely on massive singular centerpiece terrain.
- Do not create unavoidable chokepoints.
- Do not allow dominant rooftops with cover.
- Do not force all engagement through one alley.
- Do not leave large portions of DZ uncovered.
- Do not create tables that require constant clarification mid-game.

## Pre-Game Terrain Check

- Always clarify before deployment:
  - See into vs see through
  - Objective room rules
  - Ladders vs decoration
  - Vertical limits
  - Cover boundaries
- Ideally provide a printed terrain guide per table.

## Iteration and Improvement

- Take photos of good layouts.
- Test tables solo with silhouettes.
- Get feedback from different factions and playstyles.
- Tables evolve; first versions are rarely correct.
- A good table enables play even after a bad Turn 1.


## Overwatch and High Ground Balance

- Strong overwatch positions are acceptable.
- They must not be oppressive.
- A Hollow Man Spitfire or similar piece on high ground is fine if:
  - There are multiple ways to approach it
  - There is shadow cover underneath
  - The position is order-intensive to maintain
- No high position should dominate all objectives.
- Any strong ARO must have at least one viable, if costly, counterplay.

High ground should be an advantage, not a win condition.

---

## Terrain Layers and Accessibility

- Ideal tables have 3 usable vertical layers.
- A 4th layer is acceptable if limited to one building.
- Upper levels must be accessible without requiring movement skills.
- Super-Jump and Climbing Plus should be advantages, not requirements.
- Never require multiple jump or climb orders just to reach objectives.

Vertical access must be:
- Obvious
- Clearly marked
- Visually distinct

---

## Visual Language and Clarity

Terrain should communicate how it is used:
- Ladders, stairs, and vault points should stand out in color or shape.
- Access points should be obvious at a glance.
- Use strong contrasting colors for:
  - Ladders
  - Vaultable objects
  - Elevation transitions

Good tables teach players how to use them without explanation.

---

## Bridges and Connectivity

- Bridges prevent rooftops becoming isolated islands.
- Upper levels should form:
  - Partial networks
  - Safe but limited traversal routes
- Bridges should:
  - Break long sightlines
  - Enable lateral movement
  - Be risky but rewarding

Avoid creating a single dominant rooftop highway.

---

## Firelanes and Shadows

- No deployment zone to deployment zone firelanes.
- Long firelanes should be broken by:
  - Scatter
  - Height changes
  - Angled buildings
- Shadow zones are critical.
- Players should be able to advance once they leave the DZ.

If leaving the DZ feels impossible, the table has failed.

---

## Terrain Density and Flow

- Tables should have distinct areas with different play styles:
  - Dense close-range zones
  - Medium mixed zones
  - More open long-range zones
- These zones should blend naturally, not feel segmented.
- Three-lane structures are acceptable if lanes interweave and reconnect.

Good flow means:
- Multiple routes
- No forced chokepoints
- No dead ends without payoff

---

## Deployment Zones (Advanced)

Deployment zones must:
- Contain significant terrain
- Break internal sightlines
- Provide Lieutenant-safe positions
- Support weaker Lieutenant options

Certain factions rely on defensive LT placement.
Tables should account for this.

---

## Objectives and Control

- Objectives should be deliberately visible or hidden.
- Control over objectives should vary by table side.
- Slight asymmetry is good.
- First vs second turn should be a real decision.

Avoid:
- All objectives visible from the same rooftop
- All objectives equally accessible from both sides

---

## Terrain Rules and Special Zones

Use terrain mechanics sparingly and intentionally:
- Low Visibility zones for foliage or clutter
- Saturation zones for dense areas
- Difficult terrain in small, avoidable patches

Guidelines:
- Never cover more than ~25–30% of the table with terrain zones
- Avoid stacking multiple penalties
- Avoid rules that punish the losing player further

Terrain friction benefits the alpha striker if overused.

---

## Terrain Skills and Fairness

- Terrain skills are unevenly distributed across factions.
- Avoid designing tables that heavily reward or punish specific skills.
- Terrain Total should not trivialize large portions of the table.

Terrain zones should be:
- Optional
- Avoidable
- Interesting, not mandatory

---

## Shape Design

- Angular, flat-sided terrain is preferred.
- Cylinders and organic shapes increase ambiguity.
- Irregular shapes must have clearly defined bases and heights.

Ambiguity causes arguments.
Arguments ruin games.

---

## Interiors and Line of Fire

- Fully modeled interiors are discouraged.
- Prefer:
  - Open-top buildings
  - Abstract interiors
  - Zone-based rulings
- Moving terrain during play is a failure state.

Always clarify:
- See into vs see through
- Window behavior
- Doors and gates
- Vaulting permissions

---

## Table Guides and Communication

Every table should have:
- A brief terrain guide or verbal briefing
- Clear rulings on:
  - Windows
  - Doors
  - Bushes
  - Irregular shapes
  - Special zones

Players should never be surprised mid-game by terrain behavior.

---

## Asymmetry and Replayability

- Equal terrain volume, unequal distribution.
- Asymmetry creates:
  - Replay value
  - Meaningful deployment choices
- Some positions should be better for:
  - Snipers
  - Links
  - Infiltrators
  - TAGs

Let players deny terrain their opponent wants.

---

## Layout Orientation

- Square-aligned layouts are easier to balance.
- Angled layouts:
  - Reduce DZ lock
  - Increase dynamic movement
- If using angles:
  - Use one or two consistent orientations
  - Avoid random angles across the table

Purposeful variation beats chaos.

---

## Evolution and Iteration

Tables should evolve:
- Take feedback seriously
- Test with competitive players
- Adjust after negative experiences
- Photograph successful layouts

A table is never finished.
It is refined.

---

## Transport and Practicality

- Terrain should nest where possible.
- Scatter should pack inside buildings.
- Expect wear and damage over time.
- Touch-ups are normal.
- Function matters more than perfection.

Terrain that never leaves the shelf helps no one.

---

## Aesthetic Principles

- Neutral ground and roofs
- Color on vertical surfaces
- Desaturated environments
- Let models stand out

The table is the stage.
The miniatures are the actors.

# Infinity N5 Table Design Knowledge
Terrain Density, Fire Corridors, and Balance

---

## Core Concept: Terrain Density Is Balance

Terrain density directly controls which troop types dominate the game.

- Too dense favors short-range troops and warbands.
- Too sparse favors long-range guns and reactive play.
- Balanced tables allow every range band to matter.

If terrain density heavily favors units that are much cheaper in points,
the table is likely denser than Infinity’s core balance expects.

---

## The Two Extremes to Avoid

### Overly Dense Tables
Characteristics:
- Few or no long fire corridors
- Heavy reliance on corner cover
- Constant short-range engagements

Consequences:
- Warbands and template weapons dominate
- Snipers, HMGs, and Missile Launchers lose value
- Cheap close-combat units outperform their points cost

Rule of thumb:
If a 6-point Chain Rifle warband feels as valuable as a 16+ point Sniper,
the table is too dense.

---

### Overly Sparse Tables
Characteristics:
- Long, wide fire corridors across the table
- Limited scatter and shadow zones
- Large open gaps wider than 8 inches

Consequences:
- Long-range weapons dominate
- Advancing becomes extremely order-inefficient
- Reactive turn becomes overwhelming

Infinity heuristic:
Any gap wider than 8 inches is a potential death zone.

---

## Target Density Philosophy

A good Infinity table should:
- Contain some fire corridors that cross the table
- Avoid positions that oversee most of the table
- Support firefights across all range bands:
  - 0–8 inches
  - 8–16 inches
  - 16–32 inches
  - 32+ inches

The goal is not equality, but coexistence.

---

## Rulebook Guidance (Practical Interpretation)

- Use the rulebook diagram as a density reference, not the text scale
- Favor larger blocking pieces over many tiny ones
- Ensure troops can advance without invalidating long-range weapons

Balance is achieved through terrain variety, not uniformity.

---

## Terrain Types in Infinity

Infinity terrain broadly falls into three categories.

### Sight-Blocking Terrain
- Completely blocks Line of Fire
- Examples:
  - Buildings
  - Large rock formations
  - Hills
- Purpose:
  - Enables safe movement
  - Breaks long fire corridors

---

### Low Cover Terrain
- Provides cover without blocking Line of Fire
- Examples:
  - Walls
  - Crates
  - Barriers
  - Small vehicles
- Purpose:
  - Modifies firefights
  - Favors reactive play when overused

---

### Visibility Zones
- Can be seen through but impose penalties
- Examples:
  - Vegetation
  - Smoke-like environmental features
  - Storm effects
- Purpose:
  - Add texture to firelanes
  - Reward positioning and equipment choices

All three types should be present.
No single type should dominate the table.

---

## Common Density Failure Modes

### Too Much Sight-Blocking Only
- Creates a “corner cover” table
- Strongly favors the active player
- Allows easy target selection
- Often seen on overly dense urban tables

Solution:
Add scatter and low cover to create contested spaces.

---

### Too Much Low Cover
- Reactive turn becomes oppressive
- Active troopers provoke many AROs at once
- Movement becomes extremely punishing

Solution:
Add more total Line of Fire blockers.

---

### Too Much Visibility Terrain
- Multispectral Visor troops become dominant
- Long-range MSV platforms gain excessive value
- Penalties feel one-sided

Solution:
Add solid blockers such as rocks, hills, or fallen structures.

---

## Practical Density Indicators

Your table is likely well-balanced if:
- There are multiple safe advance paths
- Long-range guns can matter without dominating
- Close-range fights occur near objectives
- Rooftops are relevant but not all-seeing
- Players regularly fight at different ranges during the same game

---

## Rooftops and Density

- Rooftops must include scatter or parapets
- Rooftop-to-rooftop lanes should be restricted
- Vertical fire corridors should not dominate ground play

Rooftop scatter is density control, not decoration.

---

## Deployment Zone Considerations

- Terrain density must extend into deployment zones
- Avoid cramped DZs caused by large blockers just outside the 12-inch line
- DZs should not force immediate exposure to long corridors

Deployment should be survivable, not comfortable.

---

## Lessons from Tournament Tables

Open tables:
- Favor experienced long-range lists
- Punish inexperienced players harshly

Dense tables:
- Favor aggressive warbands
- Can feel chaotic and oppressive

The best tables:
- Look denser than they play
- Offer hidden long lanes
- Allow early long-range fights and later close-range brawls

---

## Final Density Heuristic

A good Infinity table:
- Allows advance without safety
- Allows overwatch without certainty
- Rewards range control without invalidating movement

If every troop type in both lists finds at least one moment to shine,
the density is correct.


# Infinity N5 Table Design Knowledge
Distribution of Terrain and Why Placement Matters

---

## Core Concept: Placement Matters as Much as Quantity

Having the right amount of terrain is only half the problem.
Where that terrain is placed determines how the game actually plays.

Poor distribution can be just as damaging as poor density.

---

## Distribution Extremes to Avoid

### Terrain All on One Side
Example:
- One deployment zone full of terrain
- The opposing half mostly empty

Consequences:
- One player advances safely
- The other is exposed immediately
- The game becomes one-sided and uninteractive

This is the equivalent of shooting fish in a barrel.

---

### Perfectly Symmetrical Distribution
Example:
- Mirror-image deployment zones
- Identical fire corridors on both sides

Consequences:
- Deployment choice becomes meaningless
- Firefights happen at the same ranges every game
- Tactical variety is reduced
- Games feel repetitive and flat

Symmetry looks fair, but often plays dull.

---

## Ideal Distribution Philosophy

A well-distributed table should:
- Offer distinct deployment zone choices
- Create varied fire corridor lengths
- Encourage different tactical approaches per side
- Support multiple plans without forcing one

Asymmetry is desirable when it creates interesting decisions.

---

## Uniform Tables and Their Problems

### Uniform Height Urban Tables
Common issues:
- Rooftop fire corridors stretch across the entire table
- Ground level lacks low cover
- Snipers dominate from above
- Advancing infantry is punished excessively

Fixes:
- Add rooftop scatter to break long lines
- Add low cover at ground level
- Vary building heights deliberately

---

## Breaking Uniformity Effectively

### Enclosures and Terrain Clumps
Corner enclosures and clustered terrain:
- Allow safe ground-level advance
- Create staging areas
- Introduce vulnerability to airborne troops
- Prevent constant long-range engagement

Clumping terrain creates rhythm in movement and combat.

---

### Vertical Interaction
Good tables mix:
- Tall towers for overwatch
- Medium-height blockers to interrupt lanes
- Ground-level obstacles to shape movement

The interaction between these elements matters more than any single piece.

---

## Distinct Deployment Zones

Strong terrain distribution creates deployment zones that feel different.

Benefits:
- Meaningful choice between sides
- Different risks and rewards
- Encourages list adaptation and table reading

A good table asks:
"Do you want safety, or control?"

---

## Mixed Engagement Ranges on One Table

The best tables allow:
- Long-range engagements on one flank
- Mid-range firefights in the center
- Close-range brawls near objectives

A single table should support multiple range bands simultaneously.

---

## Terrain That Controls Flow

Certain pieces define how the game unfolds:
- Tall walls that block movement but allow hacking
- Pipes and industrial elements taller than infantry
- Large blockers that force lateral movement

These pieces don’t just block fire.
They shape decisions.

---

## Key Insight from Play Experience

Even on tables with restricted ground-level LoF:
- Long-range shots should still be possible with effort
- Close-range fights should emerge naturally later
- The same piece of terrain can support different fights at different times

Good terrain evolves over the course of the game.

---

## Practical Distribution Guidelines

Your terrain distribution is likely good if:
- Deployment zones feel meaningfully different
- Players fight at multiple ranges in one game
- Some areas feel safe early but dangerous later
- The table rewards movement, not static play

---

## Final Heuristic

Good terrain distribution:
- Creates choices, not answers
- Encourages adaptation, not scripts
- Feels asymmetric without being unfair

If players remember where the fights happened and why,
the distribution worked.

# Infinity N5 Table Design Knowledge
Worked Table Setup Method, Playability Checks, and Advanced Principles

---

## Purpose of Table Setup Guidance

Infinity terrain is one of the game’s greatest strengths and greatest risks.
Good tables create memorable games.
Bad tables create horror stories.

Table setup is an opportunity to create:
- Interesting decisions
- Tactical challenges
- Novel play experiences

But mistakes in setup can easily create negative play experiences.
These principles exist to reduce that risk.

---

## Core Goal of Table Design

A table should provide:
- Opportunities for players
- Obstacles to overcome
- Novelty between games

A table is successful when:
- Players can maneuver
- No single position dominates the game
- Multiple playstyles are viable
- The table feels different each time it is set up

---

## Fundamental Setup Principle: Order of Placement

Always place terrain in order of size:
- Largest pieces first
- Medium pieces next
- Small pieces and scatter last

Reasons:
- Defines the core structure early
- Prevents accidental dominance
- Makes firelanes easier to evaluate
- Reduces rework later

If in doubt, place the largest terrain first.

---

## Combining Terrain Into Shapes

Do not disperse terrain evenly.
Combine pieces to create complex shapes.

Benefits:
- Creates nooks and crannies
- Produces safer movement paths
- Increases tactical options
- Multiplies the usefulness of limited terrain collections

Simple geometric pieces become interesting when combined.

Avoid grid layouts.
Grids create death zones and boring play.

---

## Playability Over Aesthetics

Always consider how models will move and fight:
- Ensure ladders, stairs, doors, and access points exist
- Face access routes toward likely movement directions
- Prioritize routes between deployment zones and midfield

Terrain that looks good but cannot be used is a failure.

---

## Step 1: Place the Largest Terrain Piece

- Identify the single largest terrain piece
- Place it between the two deployment zones
- Avoid placing the tallest terrain inside deployment zones

Why:
- Prevents plunging fire dominating the midfield
- Creates a central objective or contest area
- Ensures firelanes become lanes, not open fields

If the highest terrain is in the deployment zones, maneuver dies.

---

## Step 2: Place All Other Large Terrain

- Place all remaining large structures next
- Use them to sketch the table’s main shapes
- Observe how firelanes naturally form

At this stage:
- You should already see lanes of fire
- You should already see areas of interaction
- The table should be playable in a minimal sense

This step defines the table’s skeleton.

---

## Step 3: Add Medium Terrain to Increase Complexity

- Fill gaps between large terrain
- Attach smaller pieces to larger ones
- Create irregular outlines and recesses

Key ideas:
- Face access points toward the center where appropriate
- Combine pieces to create defensible positions
- Avoid straight, uninterrupted edges

Straight lines are dangerous in Infinity.
Complex shapes create survivability.

---

## Force Multiplication Through Combination

Combining terrain:
- Increases usable cover
- Creates fallback positions
- Enables guts movement
- Allows layered defense

Spreading terrain evenly does the opposite.

---

## Step 4: Add Scatter Terrain With Intent

Scatter should never be randomly scattered.

Guidelines:
- Place scatter next to existing terrain
- Use it to create safe approaches
- Use it to create prone hiding spots
- Use it to break long lines of fire

Avoid isolated scatter in open ground.
Total cover makes models safe, not token cover.

---

## Open Spaces Are Still Necessary

Do not eliminate all open ground.

Reasons:
- Variation in engagement
- Space for scenario zones
- Prevents visual clutter
- Maintains table rhythm

Open space should exist between complex areas, not replace them.

---

## Sanity Check: Playability Review

After setup, step back and inspect the table.

Look for:
- Overly dominant firelanes
- Positions that lock down deployment zones
- Unavoidable lines of fire
- Missing terrain between DZ and midfield

This inspection is mandatory.

---

## The Phoenix Test

A table fails if:
- A single model can move along a flank
- Maintain cover
- And dominate large portions of the table

Named after a real tournament outcome where this happened repeatedly.

To pass the Phoenix test:
- Break continuous lines of fire
- Add lateral complexity
- Avoid straight, uninterrupted corridors

---

## Large Model Considerations

Tables must support:
- TAGs
- Motorcycles
- Large-base models

Guidelines:
- Provide total cover deployment options
- Allow guts movement choices
- Accept some asymmetry between sides

Large models should be challenged, not excluded.

---

## Fireteam Deployment Support

Tables should allow:
- Fireteams to deploy spread out
- Models on different elevations
- Multiple exit routes from the DZ

This helps counter:
- Guided missiles
- Template saturation
- Alpha strike clustering

If the table forces clumping, it has failed.

---

## Avoiding Dead Zones

Dead zones often appear:
- Between DZ and midfield
- When focus is placed only on center and edges

Ensure:
- Forward Deployment models have usable terrain
- No large empty strips exist unintentionally

Use the full depth of the 48-inch table.

---

## Deployment Zone Depth

Terrain must extend to the back edge of deployment zones.

Avoid:
- Placing all terrain in the front 8 inches
- Forcing players to bunch up

Depth enables:
- Defense against alpha strikes
- Better Lieutenant protection
- Safer deployment choices

---

## Iteration and Adjustment

Do not be afraid to move terrain slightly.

Small changes can:
- Pinch off dominant lines of fire
- Open new movement paths
- Improve fairness without flattening the table

Perfect tables are built through adjustment.

---

## Advanced Principle: Avoid Uniformity

Uniform density is boring.

Better approach:
- Dense areas
- Open areas
- Transitional zones

As long as both players can access them, variation is good.

---

## Angled Terrain Placement

Placing terrain at angles:
- Maximizes blocked space
- Breaks horizontal firelanes
- Creates diagonal interactions

Use angles intentionally.
Avoid random jumbles.

---

## Visual and Physical Accessibility

Terrain must be usable by real people.

Avoid:
- Extremely tall buildings
- Excessive stacking
- Hard-to-reach areas

Height beyond 2–3 stories adds little tactically and harms accessibility.

---

## Visual Clarity and Contrast

Players must understand terrain at a glance.

Best practices:
- High contrast for ladders and doors
- Clear access points
- Consistent color language

Accessible tables are better tables.

---

## Avoid Swiss Cheese Tables

Swiss cheese tables:
- Look dense
- Play open
- Allow LoF through many gaps

Fix:
- Fewer, larger blockers
- More combined shapes
- Less isolated scatter

Density comes from blocking, not clutter.

---

## Rooftop Parking Lot Effect

Higher elevation always has less density.

Consequences:
- Rooftops naturally create wide firefields
- High ground favors long-range pieces

Solutions:
- Add rooftop scatter
- Increase density at ground level
- Accept open high ground but compensate below

Vertical density contrast is acceptable when intentional.

---

## Varying Base Elevation

Raising parts of the table itself:
- Adds natural variation
- Improves visual interest
- Enhances gameplay depth

Even small elevation changes go a long way.

---

## Final Principle: Rules Are Guidelines

These principles are not laws.

Terrain should provide:
- Opportunities
- Obstacles
- Novelty

Break rules intentionally to create:
- Unique tables
- Memorable experiences
- Variety across events

Just remember to still pass:
- Safe deployment
- Phoenix test
- Basic playability checks

That is the foundation of a great Infinity table.

# Infinity N5 Table Design Knowledge
Ambiguous Terrain, Shared Assumptions, and Pre-Game Clarity

---

## Episode Context

This discussion focuses on **ambiguous terrain** in Infinity and why unresolved assumptions about terrain are one of the fastest ways to ruin an otherwise good game.

The topic arose from a tournament game between two newer players from different metas, each making perfectly reasonable but *different* assumptions about how the table worked.  
The disagreement surfaced late in the game, at a point where no ruling could restore the fun.

Key takeaway:
If two players are unknowingly playing on *different tables*, everyone loses.

---

## Why Ambiguous Terrain Matters in Infinity

Terrain is not background decoration in Infinity.
It is a core system.

In Infinity:
- You play your opponent
- You play the mission
- You play the table

A bad table or misunderstood table can decide a game before Turn 1.

Infinity is one of the few games where:
- Deployment can lose you the game
- Terrain layout can invalidate skill
- A good table can elevate weaker play
- A bad table almost always creates a bad experience

---

## Terrain vs “Scenery”

Infinity distinguishes between:
- **Game-relevant terrain** (blocks LoF, provides cover, zones, access limits)
- **Purely aesthetic scenery**

The problem arises when something *looks* like one but is *played* as the other.

Ambiguity happens when this is not agreed on in advance.

---

## The Core Terrain Interactions

Terrain primarily affects:
- Line of Fire
- Cover
- Movement and access
- Special terrain zones

If any of these are unclear, the terrain is ambiguous.

---

## The Meta Problem

Inside a local meta:
- Everyone knows how the tables work
- Assumptions are shared
- Ambiguity rarely surfaces

Outside your meta:
- Different cities
- Different stores
- Different conventions
- Different habits

Assumptions diverge immediately.

Tournaments magnify this problem because:
- Neither player owns the table
- Neither player built it
- Neither player may have seen it before
- There may be no cheat sheet

---

## The Golden Rule

**Terrain must be discussed before the game.**

This should be part of standard pre-game procedure, alongside:
- Deployment zones
- Objective rules
- Classifies

Five minutes of discussion prevents forty minutes of frustration.

---

## Measuring and Silhouettes Pre-Game

Common sense usually wins here.

Typical allowances:
- Measuring deployment depth
- Checking midfield distance
- Placing silhouettes to confirm height interactions
- Verifying whether terrain blocks silhouettes (especially S6–S8)

Strict enforcement of “no pre-measuring” before initiative is chosen tends to create more problems than it solves.

---

## Special Terrain Rules (Overview)

Common terrain effects include:
- Difficult terrain (movement penalties)
- Saturation zones (Burst reduction)
- Visibility conditions (Low, Poor, Zero visibility)
- Access width (narrow vs wide gates)

These rules are functional but often secondary to the main ambiguity problems.

---

## Primary Sources of Ambiguity

### 1. Gaps Between Terrain Pieces

Common causes:
- Assembly tabs
- Bases not fitting flush
- Terrain being bumped mid-event

Questions to resolve:
- Are these pieces intended to be touching?
- Is the gap intentional?
- Can you see through it?
- Can you move through it?

Rule of thumb:
If a gap is too small to move through, it probably should not be used to shoot through.

---

### 2. Open Stairs

Problems:
- Slatted stairs block nothing by True LoF
- Undersides may or may not be shootable
- Bases slot into stairs in ways that create false cover questions

Common solutions:
- Treat stairs as solid
- Treat stairs as ramps for movement
- Decide once and apply consistently

If it is ambiguous, defaulting to “solid” usually improves gameplay.

---

### 3. Railings and Walkways

Decorative railings:
- Look like cover
- Provide no cover under True LoF

Almost universally:
- Railings are played as solid
- Walkway floors are played as solid

Mixed-material railings are especially dangerous:
- Solid panels mixed with mesh
- Aesthetic variation mistaken for gameplay intent

If material changes mid-piece, clarify whether it is aesthetic or functional.

---

### 4. Fences

Fences vary wildly by table:
- Solid walls
- Saturation zones
- Movement blockers
- Climbable obstacles

Two fences that look similar may play completely differently.

This is a textbook case for cheat sheets.

---

### 5. Vehicles

Common consensus:
- Do not shoot under vehicles
- Doing so is miserable

Questions to resolve:
- Are windows open or solid?
- Is the vehicle treated as a solid block?
- Is hovering purely aesthetic?

Floating vehicles especially require clarification.

---

### 6. Industrial Equipment (Forklifts, Cranes)

Ambiguity sources:
- Open frames
- Scaffolding
- Partial structures

Clarify:
- Is this a solid volume?
- Can you shoot through it?
- Can you climb it?
- What silhouette height does it represent?

If it causes arguments, simplify it.

---

### 7. Windows

Common window interpretations:
- Open (true LoF)
- Muddy (see in and out, not through)
- Blocked (solid wall)

Problems arise when:
- Decorative lattice is mistaken for openness
- Players assume different conventions

Windows must be explicitly defined.

---

### 8. Open Buildings

Issues:
- Buildings with missing walls
- Non-playable interiors
- Corner cutouts that allow diagonal LoF

Questions:
- Is this a solid block?
- Can you see through it?
- Can you move through it?
- Is this “inside” or “outside” for see-in/out rules?

These structures look intuitive until they aren’t.

---

## Cheat Sheets: The Best Solution

For custom tables:
- Cheat sheets are ideal
- They eliminate guesswork
- They reduce mental load
- They prevent arguments

If a cheat sheet exists:
- Follow it by default

If both players agree to override it:
- That is fine
- Agree clearly
- Apply consistently

---

## Philosophy of Resolution

When in doubt:
- Favor clarity over cleverness
- Favor cover over exposure
- Favor consistency over realism

Most thematic value comes from visuals, not rules complexity.

---

## Final Principle

Ambiguous terrain is not a rules problem.
It is a communication problem.

Infinity tables are amazing because they are custom.
That strength requires shared understanding.

Talk about the table.
Agree on how it works.
Then play the game.

Everyone will have more fun.

# Infinity N5 Table Design
Ambiguous Terrain and Mobility (Part 2)

---

## Line of Fire Through Complex Buildings

Some buildings create large, ambiguous fire lanes depending on interpretation.

A building with multiple openings can be read in several ways:
- See in and out, but not through
- A single continuous opening
- Never entering the building’s silhouette at all

Each interpretation changes the amount of Line of Fire dramatically.  
All of them are pedantic.  
All of them are legal interpretations unless clarified.

Grated rooftops introduce further ambiguity:
- Does the grating allow LoF through the floor?
- Is the absence of parapets intentional?
- Is the roof usable at all?

Buildings without parapets are almost always a deliberate design choice.
They allow deployment but punish it.

---

## Objective Rooms With External Geometry

Modern objective rooms often have flared walls, buttresses, or external detail.

Core problem:
Objective rooms are infinitely tall.
Do external flares also count as infinite?

There is no universal answer.

If the interior is an 8x8 but the exterior footprint is larger:
- Is the extra geometry decorative?
- Does standing in a flare count as being “in the room”?

Custom objective rooms that deviate from the standard cube must be explained.
Assumptions will fail.

---

## Clear Acrylic and Decorative Smoke

Clear acrylic walls:
- Must not be see-through for Line of Fire
- Seeing through something you cannot shoot through breaks the game

Smoke terrain:
- Must have a physical reference
- Solid anchor points allow fallback solutions like going Prone
- Floating smoke with no structure creates MSV-based arguments

Good smoke terrain allows interaction even when players disagree.

---

## Ambiguous Mobility: Vertical Movement

### Door-to-Hatch Teleportation

Many buildings include doors and roof hatches with no physical connection.

Common house rules exist:
- Short Skill to move between door and hatch
- Fixed movement distance
- Abstract “internal stairs”

Key rule:
If it is not on a cheat sheet or explicitly discussed, it does not exist.

Teleporting mid-game without agreement is unacceptable.

Best practice:
- Matched colors for linked doors and hatches
- Explicit movement rules

---

## Alcoves and Doorways

Even when interiors are not playable:
- Doorways often exist as recesses

Questions:
- Can you partially enter the doorway?
- Can you claim cover from it?
- Does entering the doorway count as entering the building?

Most players allow toeing in for cover.
This breaks up long walls and improves gameplay.

Again, intent must be shared.

---

## Ladders

Printed or sculpted ladders introduce expectations.

Problems:
- Ladders that do not line up vertically
- Ladders that stop short of their destination
- Ladders that are decorative but read as functional

If a ladder exists:
Players will assume it works.

If it does not work:
It must be stated clearly.

Cheat sheets prevent accidental rooftop access.

---

## “Can I Stand On It?”

One of the most common ambiguity categories.

Examples:
- Slanted dumpsters
- Angled rooftops
- Air conditioning units
- Decorative greebles
- Geodesic domes with flat panels

Questions:
- Does it support a base?
- Which base sizes fit?
- Is balance skill relevant?

Infinity is not a dexterity game.

If standing there would:
- Require balancing
- Risk damaging terrain
- Create a dominant sniper perch

The answer should almost always be no.

---

## Decorative Layers vs Functional Surface

Highly detailed terrain introduces false platforms.

Issues:
- Decorative ridges that restrict base size
- Height changes that limit placement
- Central cutouts that prevent large bases

Tags and large silhouettes are the stress test.
If a Tag “almost” fits, that is a problem.

If it was not clearly designed for that base size:
It should not be allowed.

---

## Capital-T Terrain vs Scenery

Some terrain looks like it should have rules but should not.

Example:
A shallow fountain large enough to hold models.

Questions that should not exist:
- Is it Aquatic?
- Does terrain skill apply?
- Does movement stop?

Rule of thumb:
If asking the question sounds ridiculous, the answer is no.

Terrain rules are thematic, not literal.

---

## Stairs vs Ramps

Stairs that cannot physically hold a model introduce issues:
- Can you stop midway?
- Do you need to clear them in one move?
- Are they vaultable?

Most players treat stairs as ramps:
- Measure diagonally
- Allow stopping anywhere
- Ignore step geometry

This is cleaner and avoids vaulting edge cases.

---

## Awkward Scatter Terrain

Some scatter:
- Blocks nothing
- Provides no usable cover
- Cannot safely hold a model

These pieces often become dead space.

Better solutions:
- Declare part of the base solid
- Assign a silhouette height
- Or accept that it is purely decorative

If interacting with it risks breaking it:
Do not interact with it.

---

## Amorphous Terrain (Rocks, Worms, Blobs)

Organic shapes create ambiguity:
- Cover?
- Standable?
- LoF between protrusions?

Best practice:
- Use the base footprint
- True Line of Fire for blocking
- Partial cover when touching base
- No standing unless clearly designed for it

Rocks should be:
- Tall
- Chunky
- Unambiguous

Short, lumpy shapes cause problems.

---

## Sniper Pillars and “Win Buttons”

Extremely tall terrain with flat tops is a failure state.

If a model can deploy there:
- One player wins
- The other stops having fun

Solutions:
- Declare it non-deployable
- Add scatter to block placement
- Make it structurally unsound
- Agree pre-game that nothing can go there

If deploying there would end friendships:
Do not allow it.

---

## Reasonable Height Limits

Practical considerations:
- Table height
- Player reach
- Readability
- Balance

General consensus:
- Two stories is normal
- Three stories is the upper limit
- Anything higher must be carefully controlled

Infinity tables are dense for a reason.

---

## Forests (The Worst Offender)

Forests are the most complicated terrain type in Infinity.

Common rules seen:
- Jungle terrain
- Low or Poor Visibility
- Eclipse zones
- Saturation zones
- Solid LoF blockers
- See in/out but not through
- Movable trees
- Infinite height

You cannot infer forest rules reliably from appearance.

Design cues help:
- Fixed trees imply no movement
- Magnetized trees imply movement
- Clear trunks imply passage
- Dense foliage implies blocking

None of these are guarantees.

Forests require cheat sheets.
Always.

---

## Planter Boxes

Planter boxes are forests with:
- Clear edges
- Solid bases
- Fallback prone options

If foliage is short:
Ignore it.
Play the planter.

Otherwise:
Treat like forests.
Use a cheat sheet.

---

## Final Takeaways

- Discuss terrain before the game
- Never assume your meta is universal
- Ambiguity discovered mid-game ruins games
- Cheat sheets are mandatory for custom tables
- Visual clarity beats clever rules
- If it feels like a “gotcha,” it probably is

If you build a table:
Explain it to someone who has never seen it.

Your intentions are never as obvious as you think.
