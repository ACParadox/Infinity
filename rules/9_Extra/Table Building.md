# Infinity N5 Table Design Knowledge - PART 1
Balanced, Fun, and Tactically Diverse Game Tables
300 points, 4x4 table, standard deployment (no diagonal layouts)

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Design Goals
- The table exists to maximize fun and interaction.
- Terrain should reward engagement, not punish mistakes.
- A good table balances gameplay clarity with narrative feel.
- Tables should not lock players out of the game after deployment or Turn 1.

A good Infinity table should:
- Support all weapon ranges and unit types
- Encourage movement, problem solving, and counterplay
- Avoid dominant firelanes or single correct strategies
- Reward order efficiency and positioning
- Make narrative sense while remaining mechanically fair

Infinity tables are urban combat puzzles, not symmetrical battlefields.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Core Assumptions

- Table size is 48 by 48 inches (smaller/bigger tables will modifiy these tips a bit, but core ideas are retained)
- Game size is 300 points
- Standard opposing deployment zones
- Up to 15 troopers per side
- TAGs are expected and supported

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## The 9-4-Scatter Baseline Layout

Divide the table into a 3 by 3 grid.
Each square is 18 by 18 inches.
1) 9 Main building in each of these grid sections
2) 4 Secondary buildings or cover/obstacles in the grid intersections
3) Scatter dispersed everywhere to always provide cover-to-cover moves

Also:
- Place large buildings first.
- Decide desired firelane lengths before adding scatter.
- Medium firelanes are preferred; long firelanes must be breakable.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Place 9 main buildings:
- One building or building clump per square
- Footprints between 8x8 and 12x12 inches
- No playable interiors
- Vary roof heights between 1 and 3 stories
- Bias taller buildings toward the center
- Gaps between buildings should generally allow a hand to fit for ease of play.


Optional:
- One central objective room if required by the mission

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Central Structure Bias
- Three large buildings should dominate the midfield
- Objectives should be placed on or next to terrain
- Objectives should never be in open ground
- No long-range firelane should directly cover an objective

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Secondary Scenery Pieces

Add 4 medium scenery pieces:
- Size around 4x4 to 5x5 inches
- Place them at the intersection lines between main buildings
- Purpose is to break firelanes and anchor midfield fights

Examples:
- Parked or crashed vehicles
- Machinery
- Small plazas
- Checkpoints

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Scatter Terrain 
Scatter spacing guideline:
- One piece every 4–6 inches
- Scatter must allow models to chain cover across the table.

Scatter should:
- Often touch buildings
- Create nooks and irregular shapes
- Break both horizontal and vertical sightlines

Ground-level scatter:
- Provide cover every 4 to 6 inches
- Allow models to move cover to cover
- Favor active play without removing AROs

Rooftop scatter:
- Essential for balance
- Pull scatter back from roof edges
- Allow sniper versus sniper play
- Prevents TAGs or snipers from seeing entire tables.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Terrain Density and Flow

- Tables should have distinct areas with different play styles:
  - Dense close-range zones
  - Medium mixed zones
  - More open long-range zones
- These zones should blend naturally, not feel segmented.
- Three-lane structures are acceptable if lanes interweave and reconnect.

Good flow means:
- Multiple routes
- No forced chokepoints
- No dead ends without payoff

Uniform density is boring.

Better approach:
- Dense areas
- Open areas
- Transitional zones

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Avoid Swiss Cheese Tables

Swiss cheese tables:
- Look dense
- Play open
- Allow LoF through many gaps

Fix:
- Fewer, larger blockers
- More combined shapes
- Less isolated scatter

Density comes from blocking, not clutter.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Firelane Philosophy

General rules:
- No deployment zone to deployment zone firelanes
- Longest straight firelane should be about 65 to 75 percent of table length
- Mix short, medium, and long engagement distances
- Avoid horizontal firelanes that cut through deployment zones.
- Don't just check DZ-to-DZ firelane length, also check horizontal firelanes.
- Sniper lanes should always have:
  - A close-range access path
  - A way to engage within 16 inches
- If a sniper dominates beyond 32 inches uncontested, the table has failed.
- Every strong ARO should have an order-intensive but viable counterplay.
- If leaving the DZ feels impossible, the table has failed.

- Support firefights across all range bands:
  - 0–8 inches
  - 8–16 inches
  - 16–32 inches
  - 32+ inches

The goal is not equality, but coexistence.

Corridors:
- Aim for 2 to 3 main corridors across the table
- Corridors should be contestable and have multiple entry points

Snipers:
- Provide a good but not dominant sniper position
- Only one deployment zone should have a strong sniper perch
- Strong AROs without cover are acceptable

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Deployment Zones
Each deployment zone should:
- Fit 15 models comfortably
- Include multiple pieces of solid cover
- Contain scatter to block strafing runs
- Include space for S7 models without blocking S2 placement
- At least one, preferably two, terrain pieces should touch the DZ backline.
- Slight imbalance between DZs is desirable to make turn choice meaningful.


Avoid:
- DZ to DZ firelanes
- Entire DZ exposed to a single flank
- Horizontal firelanes that cut through deployment zones.
- Super dominant sniper positions inside DZs.
- Placing all terrain in the front 8 inches
- Forcing players to bunch up

Deployment zones must be planned deliberately.
DZs should allow defense but not total safety.


### Deployment Zones (Advanced)

Deployment zones must:
- Contain significant terrain
- Break internal sightlines
- Provide Lieutenant-safe positions
- Support weaker Lieutenant options

Certain factions rely on defensive LT placement.
Tables should account for this.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Midfield
Ensure:
- Forward Deployment models have usable terrain
- No large empty strips exist unintentionally

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Verticality and Asymmetry

Height:
- Prefer a maximum of 3 stories
- About 25 percent of first-level footprint should have higher levels
- Include ladders, stairs, catwalks, and vaulting access
- Levels do not need to be full floors; partial height variation is enough.
- Avoid two-plane tables (everyone on ground vs everyone on roof).

- Upper levels must be accessible without requiring movement skills.
- Super-Jump and Climbing Plus should be advantages, not requirements.
- Never require multiple jump or climb orders just to reach objectives.
- There must be multiple ways to interact with higher levels.

Vertical access must be:
- Obvious
- Clearly marked
- Visually distinct

Terrain should communicate how it is used:
- Ladders, stairs, and vault points should stand out in color or shape.
- Access points should be obvious at a glance.
- Use strong contrasting colors for:
  - Ladders
  - Vaultable objects
  - Elevation transitions

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Rooftop Parking Lot Effect

Higher elevation always has less density.

Consequences:
- Rooftops naturally create wide firefields
- High ground favors long-range pieces

Solutions:
- Add rooftop scatter
- Increase density at ground level
- Accept open high ground but compensate below

Vertical density contrast is acceptable when intentional.  

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Bridges, Stairs, and Connectivity

- Bridges turn rooftops from islands into pathways.
- Bridges allow safe forward movement over walls.
- Stairs should face forward or sideways, not backward.
- Poor stair orientation increases order tax unnecessarily.
- Multiple access routes to upper levels are required.

- Upper levels should form:
  - Partial networks
  - Safe but limited traversal routes
- Bridges should:
  - Break long sightlines
  - Enable lateral movement
  - Be risky but rewarding

Avoid creating a single dominant rooftop highway.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## TAG and Large Model Considerations

- Leave 2 to 3 inch gaps between building clumps
- TAGs should move through streets and vault obstacles
- Avoid TAGs on rooftops

- Avoid allowing TAGs to:
  - Sit on the highest point with cover
  - See the entire table from one position

- Ensure there are safe advance paths under TAG fire.
- Large bases should be challenged, not made impossible to move.
- Always provide total cover deployment spots for S7+ models.

Include 4 to 6 TAG-scale scatter pieces:
- Vehicles
- Cargo
- Large barricades

TAGs should project control, not dominate automatically.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Board Edges
- At least 2 or 3 table edges should include solid terrain
- Prevent open flank firelanes
- Support AD troops and infiltrators

Avoid always-blocked or always-open edges.
Edges should present risk, not certainty.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## No Dead Ground
Every part of the table should:
- Offer cover
- Offer line of fire
- Or act as a transition zone

If players never go somewhere, redesign it.
---


**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Narrative vs Gameplay Tables
Narrative tables:
- Emphasize story
- May intentionally limit some tactics
- Useful for campaigns and asymmetric scenarios

Gameplay tables:
- Emphasize mechanical balance
- Ensure all weapons and unit types can matter

Both approaches are valid.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Cylinders and Shape Design

- Cylinders are not Infinity-friendly.
- Circular terrain increases cognitive load.
- Flat surfaces reduce arguments and speed up play.
- Cylinders often create ambiguous cover and LOF interactions.
- Use angular shapes or irregular blocks instead.

---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Terrain Rules and Optional Mechanics

Use sparingly:
- Low visibility zones for bushes or heavy foliage
- Saturation zones for debris or clutter
- Difficult terrain for rubble or mud
- Strong ARO positions without cover

Terrain rules enhance terrain but never replace it.

Use terrain mechanics sparingly and intentionally:
- Low Visibility zones for foliage or clutter
- Saturation zones for dense areas
- Difficult terrain in small, avoidable patches

Guidelines:
- Never cover more than ~25–30% of the table with terrain zones
- Avoid stacking multiple penalties
- Avoid rules that punish the losing player further

Terrain zones should be:
- Optional
- Avoidable
- Interesting, not mandatory
---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## House Rules and Iteration

Players should:
- Review tables regularly
- Adjust terrain or rules if one playstyle dominates

Examples:
- Line of fire only into, not through, windows
- Restricted line of fire through railings
- Base contact required for certain openings

Revisit house rules as terrain collections evolve.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Objectives and HVT Placement
- Objectives should be placed before buildings when possible.
- Objectives must not be in open ground.
- Objectives must not be visible from across the table.
- Missions like Unmasking require:
  - Multiple valid HVT placements per player
  - Defensive options that are not identical on both sides


**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Do Not List

- Do not rely on massive singular centerpiece terrain.
- Do not create unavoidable chokepoints.
- Do not allow dominant rooftops with cover.
- Do not force all engagement through one alley.
- Do not leave large portions of DZ uncovered.
- Do not create tables that require constant clarification mid-game.
---
**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Final Checklist

Before playing, confirm:
- No DZ to DZ firelanes
- 2 to 3 usable corridors
- Cover every 4 to 6 inches
- Vertical access is contestable
- TAGs can move meaningfully
- Objectives are protected
- Board edges are interactive

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Table Guides and Communication

Every table should have:
- A brief terrain guide or verbal briefing
- Clear rulings on:
  - Windows
  - Doors
  - Bushes
  - Irregular shapes
  - Special zones

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
### Primary Sources of Terrain Ambiguity
These need to be handled in a Table Guide:
#### Gaps Between Terrain Pieces

Common causes:
- Assembly tabs
- Bases not fitting flush
- Terrain being bumped mid-event
Rule of thumb:
If a gap is too small to move through, it probably should not be used to shoot through.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Open Stairs

Problems:
- Slatted stairs block nothing by True LoF
- Undersides may or may not be shootable
- Bases slot into stairs in ways that create false cover questions
Common solutions:
- Treat stairs as solid
- Treat stairs as ramps for movement

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Railings and Walkways

Decorative railings:
- Look like cover
- Provide no cover under True LoF

Almost universally:
- Railings are played as solid
- Walkway floors are played as solid

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Fences

Fences vary wildly by table:
- Solid walls
- Saturation zones
- Movement blockers
- Climbable obstacles

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Vehicles

Common consensus:
- Do not shoot under vehicles
- Doing so is miserable

Questions to resolve:
- Are windows open or solid?
- Is the vehicle treated as a solid block?

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Industrial Equipment (Forklifts, Cranes)

Ambiguity sources:
- Open frames
- Scaffolding
- Partial structures

Clarify:
- Is this a solid volume?
- Can you shoot through it?
- Can you climb it?
- What silhouette height does it represent?

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Windows

Common window interpretations:
- Open (true LoF)
- Muddy (see in and out, not through)
- Blocked (solid wall)

Problems arise when:
- Decorative lattice is mistaken for openness
- Players assume different conventions

Windows must be explicitly defined.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Open Buildings

Issues:
- Buildings with missing walls
- Non-playable interiors
- Corner cutouts that allow diagonal LoF

Questions:
- Is this a solid block?
- Can you see through it?
- Can you move through it?
- Is this “inside” or “outside” for see-in/out rules?

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Objective Rooms With External Geometry

Modern objective rooms often have flared walls, buttresses, or external detail.

Core problem:
Objective rooms are infinitely tall.
Do external flares also count as infinite?

There is no universal answer.

If the interior is an 8x8 but the exterior footprint is larger:
- Is the extra geometry decorative?

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Door-to-Hatch Teleportation

Many buildings include doors and roof hatches with no physical connection.

Common house rules exist:
- Short Skill to move between door and hatch
- Fixed movement distance
- Abstract “internal stairs”

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Alcoves and Doorways

Even when interiors are not playable:
- Doorways often exist as recesses

Questions:
- Can you partially enter the doorway?
- Can you claim cover from it?
- Does entering the doorway count as entering the building?

Most players allow toeing in for cover.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Ladders

Printed or sculpted ladders introduce expectations.

Problems:
- Ladders that do not line up vertically
- Ladders that stop short of their destination
- Ladders that are decorative but read as functional

If a ladder exists:
Players will assume it works.

If it does not work:
It must be stated clearly.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### “Can I Stand On It?”

One of the most common ambiguity categories.

Examples:
- Slanted dumpsters
- Angled rooftops
- Air conditioning units
- Decorative greebles
- Geodesic domes with flat panels

Questions:
- Does it support a base?
- Which base sizes fit?
- Is balance skill relevant?

Infinity is not a dexterity game.

If standing there would:
- Require balancing
- Risk damaging terrain
- Create a dominant sniper perch

The answer should almost always be no.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Stairs vs Ramps

Stairs that cannot physically hold a model introduce issues:
- Can you stop midway?
- Do you need to clear them in one move?
- Are they vaultable?

Most players treat stairs as ramps:
- Measure diagonally
- Allow stopping anywhere
- Ignore step geometry

This is cleaner and avoids vaulting edge cases.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Amorphous Terrain (Rocks, Worms, Blobs)

Organic shapes create ambiguity:
- Cover?
- Standable?
- LoF between protrusions?

Best practice:
- Use the base footprint
- True Line of Fire for blocking
- Partial cover when touching base
- No standing unless clearly designed for it

Rocks should be:
- Tall
- Chunky
- Unambiguous

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
####  Sniper Pillars and “Win Buttons”

Extremely tall terrain with flat tops is a failure state.

If a model can deploy there:
- One player wins
- The other stops having fun

Solutions:
- Declare it non-deployable
- Add scatter to block placement
- Make it structurally unsound
- Agree pre-game that nothing can go there

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Forests 
You cannot infer forest rules reliably from appearance.
Forests require cheat sheets.
Design cues help:
- Fixed trees imply no movement
- Clear trunks imply passage
- Dense foliage implies blocking

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
#### Planter Boxes

Planter boxes are forests with:
- Clear edges
- Solid bases
- Fallback prone options

If foliage is short:
Ignore it.
Play the planter.

Otherwise:
Treat like forests.
Use a cheat sheet.

**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Pre-Game Terrain Check

- Always clarify before deployment:
  - See into vs see through
  - Objective room rules
  - Ladders vs decoration
  - Vertical limits
  - Cover boundaries
- Ideally provide a printed terrain guide per table.


**[UNOFFICIAL COMMENT]**
## Transport and Practicality

- Terrain should nest where possible.
- Scatter should pack inside buildings.
- Expect wear and damage over time.
- Touch-ups are normal.
- Function matters more than perfection.

Terrain that never leaves the shelf helps no one.
