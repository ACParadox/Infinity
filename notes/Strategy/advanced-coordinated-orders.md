# List-Building Strategy — Advanced Coordinated Orders

Coordinated Orders are best understood as **ARO compression and activation compression**, not simply as “four models for one Order.”

The basic exchange is:

> **Spend 1 Regular Order + 1 Command Token to activate up to four eligible troopers, while forcing each reactive trooper to choose only one of those active troopers as its target.**

This creates value in three different ways:

1. **movement efficiency** — several independent models reposition with one Order;
2. **state/action efficiency** — several models Discover, enter Suppressive Fire, re-Camouflage, place deployables, or perform another shared action together;
3. **ARO-choice pressure** — one reactive model cannot simply make a separate shooting/hacking ARO against every participant.

The third point is the most tactically important, but it has a major caveat: **Dodge and Reset can oppose the appropriate coordinated attacks collectively.** Coordinated attacks therefore do not automatically create three unopposed attacks. They create a declaration fork: the defender may shoot or otherwise react against one chosen participant, or may choose a defensive ARO such as Dodge/Reset that can oppose the relevant attacks at the cost of giving up a different reactive action.

## 1. Rules frame that matters strategically

For tactical evaluation, remember the following restrictions before building a plan around coordination:

- up to **four troopers** may participate;
- all participants must be in the **same Combat Group**;
- all must have the same Training: Regular with Regular, or Irregular with Irregular;
- they must declare the **same sequence of Skills**;
- if a declared Skill requires a target, they must normally choose the **same single target**;
- a participant that cannot satisfy the Requirements of a declared Skill performs **Idle** while the others continue normally;
- Fireteam members cannot participate;
- Controllers and their Peripherals cannot participate;
- the **Spearhead** uses half the indicated Burst of its weapon, Equipment, or Skill, including Burst bonuses, **rounded up**;
- every other participant is reduced to **B1**;
- each reactive trooper chooses only **one** participating trooper as its target;
- Targetless and Deployable weapons/equipment are exceptions to the common-target restriction and need not use the same point on the table.

The Burst rule is important enough to state explicitly:

> **The Spearhead is not automatically B1.**

A B5 weapon becomes B3, B4 becomes B2, B3 becomes B2, and B2 becomes B1 for the Spearhead. The other participants are B1 regardless of their normal Burst.

The September 2026 N5 FAQ adds another important interaction: **Special Dice modifiers such as +1 SD still apply during a Coordinated Order because they do not modify Burst.** This makes +SD profiles unusually coordination-friendly compared with profiles whose offensive value is concentrated in +B.

## 2. The central tactic: force an ARO-choice fork

The defining offensive use of a Coordinated Order is to present one reactive model with several simultaneous threats.

Suppose four shooters attack one target.

If the target declares BS Attack against the Spearhead:

- the Spearhead resolves Face to Face against that ARO;
- the other eligible shooters are not opposed by that BS Attack and normally make Normal Rolls.

If the target instead shoots one of the secondary participants:

- that secondary attack is opposed;
- the more dangerous Spearhead may now be making its reduced-Burst attack as Normal Rolls.

That creates the basic target-choice problem:

> **Which attacker is dangerous enough that the defender is willing to let the others roll normally?**

However, never forget the defensive escape valve:

- **Dodge** can oppose appropriate coordinated physical attacks;
- **Reset** can oppose appropriate coordinated Comms Attacks.

The strongest coordinated attacks therefore do not merely present “four guns.” They make every available ARO choice costly.

Examples:

- shoot the elite Spearhead and accept several B1 Normal Rolls;
- Dodge and give up the chance to shoot an exposed attacker;
- Reset and give up another useful ARO while several hackers pressure the target;
- react to a dangerous disposable weapon and allow the primary gunfighter to roll normally.

## 3. Strong Spearhead + three efficient B1 threats

The most natural coordinated attack package is **one model that still performs well at reduced Burst plus three models that lose very little by being reduced to B1**.

Good Spearheads tend to have:

- high BS or WIP;
- strong positive MODs;
- useful Mimetism/MSV interactions;
- favorable rangebands;
- a high native Burst that remains meaningful after halving;
- strong ammunition or high Damage/PS;
- +SD or other effects that survive the coordination Burst restriction.

Good secondary participants tend to have:

- native B1 weapons;
- Disposable weapons;
- high-impact ammunition;
- strong one-die utility attacks;
- acceptable BS/WIP even without Burst superiority.

This creates an important list-building principle:

> **Do not fill a coordinated attack with four models that all depend on high Burst. Pair a strong Spearhead with models whose one die is already dangerous.**

A B1 Panzerfaust, Blitzen, E/M weapon, Adhesive/PARA-style threat, Flash Pulse, or other high-impact single-die attack often loses little or nothing as a secondary coordinated participant.

By contrast, turning three premium B4 attack pieces into B1 contributors can throw away much of what made those profiles expensive.

## 4. Disposable-weapon volleys

Disposable weapons are among the cleanest offensive uses of coordination because many already operate at B1.

A coordinated volley can combine different weapons as long as all participants are declaring the same Skill, such as BS Attack, and are attacking the same required target.

This lets one target simultaneously face different failure modes:

- raw damage;
- AP or multi-Save ammunition;
- E/M or Isolation/IMM pressure;
- PARA/Adhesive-style disabling effects;
- Stun or other state application.

The objective is often not simply “cause four Wounds.” It is:

> **Make every reactive choice leave the target exposed to a strategically unacceptable Normal Roll.**

This is particularly attractive against expensive HI, TAGs, REMs, or elite pieces whose continued operation depends on avoiding one specific disabling ammunition type.

Do not waste Disposable shots merely because they coordinate efficiently. The target still needs to justify both the ammunition expenditure and the Command Token.

## 5. Coordinated Move: activation compression without a Burst tax

Coordinated movement is often the safest and most consistently valuable use because no shooting Burst is being sacrificed.

Good uses include:

- advancing several independent midfield pieces;
- moving several specialists toward future objectives;
- evacuating multiple exposed Order generators;
- moving several zone-scoring bodies late in the game;
- redeploying a loose defensive screen;
- shifting multiple models toward a flank that has become important;
- crossing a lane watched by one isolated reactive piece.

The last case is especially important.

If one sniper sees four coordinated models cross a lane, that sniper still chooses one participating trooper as the target of its ARO. Coordination can therefore let several models move through a **single reactive gate** more efficiently than activating them one at a time.

But this does not make the movement safe against the whole table.

Before coordinating movement, count:

- how many different enemy pieces can ARO;
- whether different reactive troops can each choose different participants;
- whether templates can catch multiple models;
- whether Mines/Perimeter weapons will trigger;
- whether hostile hacking areas cover the route;
- whether the models are unnecessarily clustered.

Coordinated movement is strongest when it compresses exposure to **one or a few reactive models**, not when it moves four troops into an entire layered defence at once.

## 6. Coordinated Suppressive Fire

Coordinated Suppressive Fire is one of the strongest end-of-turn conversion plays for independent models.

Instead of spending several Orders placing models into Suppressive Fire separately, one Coordinated Order can establish several defensive pieces at once.

The tactical goal should be to create **separate defensive problems**, not one crowded cluster.

Useful placements include:

- one suppressor covering an objective approach;
- one covering a flank or corner;
- one protecting a retreat route;
- one guarding a specialist or scoring zone.

Strong candidates include:

- Mimetism troops;
- durable HI;
- midfield specialists that have already completed their active-turn task;
- cheap troops in annoying positions;
- models whose weapon and rangeband become threatening in Suppressive Fire.

This is especially attractive on Turn 2 or Turn 3 when a Command Token's future opportunity cost is lower and the board position is more important than preserving resources for later restructuring.

## 7. Coordinated Discover

Discover can be an expensive tax when one difficult Camouflage Marker must be revealed immediately.

A Coordinated Discover allows several participating troopers to make independent attempts against the same Marker in one activation. This is useful when:

- one Marker blocks an essential route;
- a hidden specialist is contesting an objective;
- an enemy attack piece must be revealed before the main attack begins;
- range/Mimetism makes any individual Discover unreliable;
- the active player cannot afford several sequential failed Discover Orders.

This is brute-force reliability rather than finesse.

If Sensor can solve the problem more cheaply or affect several markers at once, use Sensor. Coordinated Discover is valuable when the list lacks that better tool or when the target is so important that multiple simultaneous WIP attempts justify a Command Token.

## 8. Coordinated Camouflage-marker movement

Coordinating several Camouflage Markers creates an information-allocation problem for the Reactive Player.

A reactive trooper still chooses only one participating trooper as the target of its ARO. If it chooses one Camouflage Marker, it may Delay against that Marker when permitted—but it has committed its attention to that particular participant.

This makes coordinated marker movement useful for:

- pushing several Camo troops deeper into midfield;
- advancing a real attacker among less important markers;
- moving several specialists while hiding which one matters most;
- forcing the opponent to guess which Marker deserves the ARO;
- preserving marker-state pressure without feeding sequential Discover attempts.

If the selected Marker does not reveal after a Delay—for example because the second Skill is another Move—the delayed ARO can be lost.

The limitation is that multiple reactive models may each choose a different Marker. Coordination overloads **individual reactive pieces**, not the entire enemy army.

## 9. Coordinated re-Camouflage and state activation

The Coordinated Order rules explicitly support activating the same Game State with several participants.

That means multiple eligible Camouflage troops can potentially re-enter the Camouflaged State together when each satisfies the Requirements.

This can be an excellent post-attack reset:

1. several midfield troops reveal during the active turn;
2. they finish outside enemy LoF and otherwise satisfy the requirements;
3. one Coordinated Order returns several of them to Camouflaged State;
4. the opponent must pay the Discover/information tax again next turn.

The broader principle is:

> **When several models independently want the same state-changing action, check whether coordination can buy all of them at once.**

Suppressive Fire is the most obvious example, but Camouflage can be equally valuable in marker-heavy lists.

## 10. Coordinated hacking

Coordinated hacking follows the same core logic as coordinated shooting, but with stricter sameness requirements:

- every participating Hacker must use the **same Hacking Program**;
- if the Program requires a target, all must choose the **same target**;
- the Spearhead receives the normal Coordinated Order Burst treatment;
- the other Hackers are B1.

This is useful when several Hackers can access one important target through the same network and the target has an uncomfortable ARO decision.

Potential goals include:

- Immobilizing a TAG/HI;
- Isolating a critical piece;
- applying Targeted;
- attacking a Hacker through a shared Repeater network;
- forcing a target to choose between Reset and another valuable reactive action.

Do not treat this as a way to bypass Reset. If the target Resets, that Roll is Face to Face against the appropriate coordinated hacking attacks.

The better question is:

> **Does forcing Reset itself create value?**

For example, if Reset means the target is not shooting, not Dodging into a better position, or not using another important ARO, the hacking coordination may have accomplished something even before a state lands.

Also audit the hostile hacking network. Activating several Hackers inside overlapping enemy Repeaters can expose several participants to several different reactive Hackers. Coordination can multiply risk if the opponent has enough separate ARO sources.

## 11. Coordinated Close Combat

Coordinated CC works differently from coordinated shooting.

Only the **Spearhead** performs the CC Attack. The Spearhead gains **+1 Burst for each participating allied trooper in Silhouette contact with the target**.

This suggests a clear construction:

> **One excellent melee Spearhead + cheap bodies that can reach Silhouette contact.**

The supporting models do not need elite CC stats if their main function is to add Burst to the real fighter.

This can be strong against:

- TAGs;
- elite shooters with weak melee defense;
- already-Immobilized or Isolated heavy targets;
- models whose continued existence matters more than the supporting troops' survival.

The hard part is usually delivery. If three extra bodies require several Orders to reach contact, the coordinated CC payoff may not justify the setup.

Coordinated CC is strongest when multiple models are already close enough to engage the target or when movement tools let them arrive together efficiently.

## 12. Coordinated Airborne Deployment

Off-table troopers with Airborne Deployment-labelled Skills may be selected to deploy as part of a Coordinated Order.

The important cost is easy to overlook:

> **Those troopers lose their own Orders for that Turn.**

Therefore coordinated AD is usually **not Order economy**. Sequential arrival can often use each airborne model's personal Order; coordinated arrival instead spends a pool Order and Command Token while giving up those personal Orders.

What coordination buys is **simultaneity and reactive pressure**.

This can matter when several airborne troops must arrive around the same defensive position and sequential arrivals would let the same reactive piece answer them one after another.

Use coordinated AD when the simultaneous board-state change is worth more than the personal Orders being sacrificed.

Typical reasons include:

- creating several threats in one flank at once;
- overwhelming one isolated reactive defender's target selection;
- deploying several scoring bodies late in the game;
- establishing several attack angles before the opponent can respond to them sequentially.

## 13. Coordinated deployables and area control

Weapons and Equipment with the **Targetless** or **Deployable** Traits are exempt from the usual rule requiring all participants to use the same target point.

This creates a valuable area-control application: several models can use the same declared Skill while placing their deployables in different useful locations.

Potential uses include:

- spreading Mines across several approach lanes;
- extending a Repeater network;
- creating several separate disposable threat zones;
- protecting multiple retreat routes or objective approaches.

This is especially efficient after several midfield models have already reached useful positions and the active player wants to convert one remaining Order into a wider defensive network.

Do not cluster deployables merely because they were placed together. The strategic value is usually in **covering different geometry simultaneously**.

## 14. Multi-angle attacks and crossfire geometry

All participants attacking a required target must attack the same target, but they do not have to attack it from the same position or with the same weapon.

That means a coordinated attack can be prepared as a crossfire:

- the Spearhead attacks from its best rangeband;
- another participant attacks from an angle that denies Cover;
- another contributes a Disposable anti-armor/state weapon;
- another threatens from a short-range or rear angle.

The target now has to choose which participant deserves the direct ARO while suffering the consequences of the other attack vectors.

This often requires setup Orders before the coordinated attack itself. Do not judge the final volley in isolation; judge the whole sequence:

> **setup cost + Command Token + reduced Burst vs the value of the forced ARO fork.**

## 15. When Burst reduction makes coordination bad

Coordination is not automatically efficient just because four models activate.

It is often a poor attack when:

- the primary attacker badly needs full Burst to win the Face-to-Face Roll;
- several participants are expensive high-Burst gunfighters reduced to B1;
- the defender has an excellent Dodge and is happy to use it;
- the target is low-value and does not justify a Command Token;
- multiple enemy ARO pieces can each oppose a different participant;
- several participants are in bad rangebands;
- templates or deployables punish the grouped activation;
- the attack requires several different enemy targets, which coordination cannot provide;
- the Command Token is more valuable for Fireteam reconstruction, recovery, Combat Group restructuring, or a later scoring play.

A simple example:

A premium B5 gunfighter may be far more reliable attacking alone at full Burst than becoming a B3 Spearhead merely to add three mediocre B1 Rifle shots.

The relevant comparison is not:

> “Four attacks are better than one.”

It is:

> **“Does the ARO-choice pressure and activation compression gained from coordination outweigh the Burst, positioning, ammunition, and Command Token costs?”**

## 16. Reactive-player counterplay

Understanding the defensive answers improves offensive coordination.

When facing a Coordinated Order, consider:

- **Dodge** when several physical attacks would otherwise become Normal Rolls;
- **Reset** when several Comms Attacks are converging;
- target the participant whose unopposed action would create the greatest future cost, not automatically the Spearhead;
- use different reactive troops to choose different coordinated participants;
- exploit templates when multiple participants are physically clustered;
- force coordinated movers through Mines, Perimeter weapons, Repeaters, and other area-control layers;
- Delay against the most strategically important Camo Marker rather than the nearest one;
- remember that stopping one participant may still be enough if that participant is the specialist, state enabler, or route opener.

The active player should therefore ask before spending the Command Token:

> **What is the defender's best universal ARO, and does my coordination still create value if they choose it?**

If the answer is “they Dodge/Reset comfortably and I have paid a Command Token to reduce my own Burst,” do not coordinate.

## 17. Turn-by-turn use

### Turn 1

Command Tokens have high future value, so coordination should usually create a large positional or offensive advantage.

Best candidates:

- moving several midfield pieces into productive positions;
- coordinated Camo development;
- a high-value disposable volley against a centerpiece;
- coordinated AD when simultaneous arrival enables a real alpha strike.

### Turn 2

This is often the best turn for flexible coordination because the board state is clearer and Command Token opportunity cost has fallen.

Best candidates:

- mass repositioning;
- coordinated Discover before a decisive attack;
- coordinated hacking/CC against a now-accessible target;
- rebuilding midfield control after trades;
- end-turn Suppressive Fire.

### Turn 3

Scoring dominates resource conservation.

Best candidates:

- move several scoring bodies into zones;
- move several specialists toward objectives;
- re-Camouflage key survivors;
- establish several Suppressive Fire pieces protecting the final score;
- coordinated AD deployment for late scoring;
- use a final offensive coordination to clear the one model preventing victory.

A Command Token that ends the game unused has no value.

## 18. Practical Coordinated Order checklist

Before spending the Command Token, ask:

1. **What exact value am I buying: movement, state/action compression, or ARO-choice pressure?**
2. **Are all participants in the same Combat Group and compatible Training?**
3. **Can all of them legally declare the same Skill sequence?**
4. **If a target is required, can they all contribute meaningfully against the same target?**
5. **Who should be Spearhead after the Burst reduction?**
6. **Which secondary participants still produce useful B1 attacks?**
7. **What happens if the defender Dodges or Resets instead of shooting?**
8. **How many separate enemy ARO pieces can react?**
9. **Are templates, Mines, hacking areas, or Perimeter weapons punishing the simultaneous activation?**
10. **Would four separate activations actually be better because they preserve Burst or personal Orders?**
11. **Is the Command Token more valuable later?**
12. **What board state will remain after the coordination succeeds?**

The most useful summary is:

> **Coordinate when simultaneous activation changes the defender's decision structure or compresses several necessary actions. Do not coordinate merely because four models happen to be available.**
