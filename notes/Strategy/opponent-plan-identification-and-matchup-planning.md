# List-Building Strategy — Opponent-Plan Identification and Matchup Planning

Matchup planning is the bridge between **list knowledge, deployment, target priority, and turn planning**.

The goal is not merely to identify the opponent's strongest profiles. The useful pregame question is:

> **What does this army want its first meaningful turn to look like, and how can I make that sequence expensive, unreliable, or strategically irrelevant?**

A good matchup read turns an enemy list from a collection of profiles into a **plan with dependencies**. Once the dependencies are visible, deployment and early target priority become much easier.

## 1. Think in plans, not profiles

A dangerous model matters because of **what it enables the opponent to accomplish**.

Do not stop at:

> "That TAG is dangerous."

Translate it into a sequence:

> "The TAG wants to clear one long-range ARO, cross this lane, remove two Orders and a Specialist, then retreat behind Repeaters and mines."

That sentence is strategically useful because each step can be attacked.

Likewise:

- a Hacker is not just a Hacker; it may be the piece that turns a Pitcher into an Isolation threat;
- an Engineer is not just recovery; it may be what allows a TAG to take risky fights repeatedly;
- a warband is not just a cheap template; it may be the tool required to clear your midfield Camo before the premium attacker can move;
- a Camo Specialist is not just a marker; it may be the opponent's entire late-game scoring plan;
- a cheap linked body may be more important than its cost because it preserves Fireteam bonuses for the gunfighter.

The pregame task is to discover the **dependency graph** behind the opponent's likely plan.

---

## 2. The eight-part opponent map

Before deployment is complete, identify these eight elements as well as the available information allows.

| Element | Questions | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary attacker** | Who most wants several Regular Orders? Which piece has the best combination of mobility, Burst, MODs, range, and durability? | Predicts the likely spearhead. |
| **Secondary attacker** | What attacks if the primary matchup is bad? | Prevents overcommitting to one counter. |
| **Lieutenant shell** | Which models are plausible Lieutenants? Is there NCO, Chain of Command, Strategos, or an active LT package? | Predicts command resilience and LT-Order use. |
| **Hacking network** | Which Hackers matter? How are Repeaters projected? Where can Pitchers, FastPandas, deployables, or midfield Repeaters reach? | Predicts non-gun attack lanes and anti-HI/TAG pressure. |
| **DZ-strike package** | What can appear, infiltrate, impersonate, jump, climb, ride, or sprint into the backline? | Determines rear/flank protection and spacing. |
| **Scoring package** | Which pieces actually press buttons, dominate zones, carry objectives, or complete scenario tasks? | Reveals what must survive for the opponent to win. |
| **Recovery infrastructure** | Engineer, Doctor, Servants, Baggage, nearby support, state removal? | Tells you whether damage or states will stick. |
| **Likely reserve** | Which unrevealed piece benefits most from seeing your deployment? | Prevents one final placement from invalidating your setup. |

This map does not need perfect information. Infinity contains private information and off-table threats. The objective is to identify the **credible plan space**, not reconstruct the exact list with certainty.

## 3. Write the opponent's Turn-1 sentence

After identifying the pieces, summarize their preferred first turn in **one sentence**.

Examples:

- **"Long-range gunfighter clears my main ARO, then kills exposed Orders before retreating."**
- **"Pitcher reaches my TAG, Hackers Isolate/Immobilize it, then the main attacker advances through the weakened lane."**
- **"Impersonator attacks my Lieutenant/support shell, then the rest of the army exploits the disruption."**
- **"Fast Specialists take the midfield objectives, then mines/Repeaters make me spend my next turn digging them out."**
- **"Camo midfield delays me while the opponent preserves the premium attacker for Turns 2–3."**
- **"The opponent does not need to alpha strike; they only need to establish a scoring lead and make me attack into their defence."**

The sentence should describe the **result they want**, not merely the first model they will activate.

Then ask:

> **What must be true for that sentence to succeed?**

---

## 4. Convert the sentence into a dependency chain

A useful generic structure is:

> **Access → enabler → breach → payoff → extraction**

### Access

How does the plan reach the relevant part of the table?

Examples:

- an HMG lane;
- a bike route;
- a climb or Super-Jump path;
- a table-edge entry point;
- an Impersonation deployment position;
- a Pitcher firing point;
- Smoke/Eclipse access;
- a Camo-marker approach.

### Enabler

What makes the attack favourable?

Examples:

- Smoke;
- MSV;
- White Noise;
- Pitcher/Repeater;
- Hacker;
- Fireteam bonus;
- TinBot;
- Engineer;
- Sensor;
- Coordinated Order;
- disposable weapon;
- Spotlight;
- a cheap sacrificial piece clearing a mine or ARO.

### Breach

What obstacle must be solved first?

Examples:

- your long-range ARO;
- your midfield mine;
- a Hacker covering the route;
- a disposable template guard;
- a Camo marker forcing Discover tax;
- a null-deployed position forcing extra movement.

### Payoff

What does the opponent actually gain?

Examples:

- remove Orders;
- kill the Lieutenant;
- disable a TAG;
- destroy the Engineer;
- clear a scoring route;
- take a zone;
- activate objectives;
- force your army to spend the next turn recovering instead of scoring.

### Extraction

Where does the attacker finish?

Examples:

- back behind Total Cover;
- in Suppressive Fire;
- inside a Repeater network;
- protected by mines;
- on an objective;
- intentionally sacrificed after removing enough value.

You often do **not** need to stop every step. Breaking one cheap dependency can make the entire sequence inefficient.

## 5. Find the plan's choke point

Ask:

> **Which required step is cheapest for me to disrupt?**

This is frequently not the premium attacker itself.

Examples:

### TAG + Engineer

If the TAG can repeatedly take risky engagements because an Engineer is safely nearby, the Engineer may be the true dependency.

Possible response:

- deny the Engineer's route;
- threaten the Servant;
- isolate or kill the Engineer;
- force the TAG to finish far from recovery.

### HI attacker + aggressive hacking network

If the HI depends on Pitchers or midfield Repeaters to neutralize opposition first, attack the network.

Possible response:

- hide Hackable pieces from easy Repeater projection;
- guard the likely Pitcher firing point;
- destroy Repeaters;
- threaten the Hacker after it commits.

### Fireteam gunfighter

The strongest shooter may depend on cheap members for bonuses and movement efficiency.

Possible response:

- attack exposed support members;
- force templates into the team;
- break Coherency;
- remove the Specialist or support profile that makes the team multifunctional.

### Camo scoring shell

If the opponent's actual route to victory is two midfield Specialists surviving until Turn 3, killing an unrelated 50-point gunfighter may matter less than exposing or pinning those Specialists.

### Alpha-strike list with thin Order base

If the attacker needs nearly the entire pool, removing two or three Orders may be more effective than trying to kill the attacker directly.

The principle is:

> **Attack the dependency with the best ratio of disruption caused to Orders/risk spent.**

## 6. Identify the first meaningful destination

For mobile attackers, do not only look at where they deploy.

Ask:

> **Where does this model need to stand before it starts accomplishing anything important?**

Examples:

- a Spitfire must reach midfield before its range bands become favourable;
- an HMG may need one particular corner to see the relevant lane;
- a Pitcher carrier may need one firing point to project the network;
- an Impersonator may need a specific short path to reach the Lieutenant shell;
- a Specialist may need one objective room;
- a bike may need a particular gap between buildings to enter the backline.

That destination is often a better defensive target than the model's starting position.

Useful defensive tools at the destination include:

- mines;
- perimeter weapons;
- template guards;
- hacking coverage;
- hidden AROs;
- Camo markers;
- Suppressive Fire;
- crossfires;
- cheap bodies forcing awkward movement;
- a counter-attacker positioned to punish the final square.

This is **route defence**, not model defence.

---

## 7. Determine what the opponent wants to attack

Reverse the analysis.

Ask:

> **If I were the opponent, which of my pieces would I most want to remove on Turn 1?**

Common high-value targets are:

- exposed Regular Orders;
- Lieutenant shell;
- lone Engineer supporting a TAG/REM/VH package;
- only Hacker protecting the midfield;
- only anti-Camo piece;
- only long-range attacker;
- mission-critical Specialist;
- EVO enabling an airborne/guided package;
- exposed Doctor/Servant cluster;
- Fireteam member whose death collapses bonuses;
- scoring piece that cannot be replaced.

Then ask a second question:

> **Can the same attack run reach several of these targets?**

If yes, your deployment is giving the opponent **target density**.

Spacing and layered defence should force the attacker to choose rather than collect several strategic targets in one route.

---

## 8. Identify the pivot

Stopping Plan A is not enough.

Ask:

> **What does the opponent do if I make Plan A unattractive?**

This reveals the **secondary attacker or alternate scoring line**.

Examples:

### Plan A

Long-range TAG attacks the main lane.

### Your response

AP/EXP ARO plus hacking coverage makes that lane expensive.

### Pivot

Camo Specialists ignore the gunfight and push objectives.

---

### Plan A

HI Fireteam advances through midfield.

### Your response

Dense Repeater/Hacker coverage.

### Pivot

Unhackable warband or skirmisher clears Repeaters first.

---

### Plan A

Impersonator attacks the Lieutenant.

### Your response

Mines, guards, and spacing make the LT route ugly.

### Pivot

The Impersonator kills the Engineer or several Orders instead.

A good matchup deployment aims for:

> **Plan A = expensive, Plan B = awkward.**

It does **not** need to produce:

> **Plan A = impossible.**

Trying to make one plan impossible can overconcentrate defence and hand the alternate plan a free game.

---

## 9. Build a counter package, not a single counter

Avoid thinking:

> "I have one anti-TAG model, so the TAG matchup is covered."

A real counter package usually combines several layers:

1. **route tax** — mine, marker, deployable, awkward geometry;
2. **interaction threat** — gun, Hacker, template, E/M, state weapon;
3. **support denial** — pressure on Engineer, Repeater, Fireteam support, or Orders;
4. **fallback** — secondary answer if the first tool dies;
5. **counterpunch** — a piece positioned to exploit where the enemy attacker finishes.

The objective is not to guarantee a kill. It is to make the enemy spend Orders solving multiple different problems.

---

## 10. Use matchup planning to choose what **not** to defend

Defensive resources are limited.

Do not attempt to protect every model equally.

Some pieces can be deliberately exposed when:

- they are cheap and replaceable;
- their death does not affect the scoring plan;
- attacking them pulls the enemy away from meaningful targets;
- the opponent must spend several Orders to reach them;
- they trade for tempo even without dealing damage;
- revealing the attacker creates a strong counterattack.

A model's survival value depends on the matchup.

A 10-point Order near the main attack route may be more strategically valuable than a 25-point Specialist that has already completed its task. Conversely, a mediocre 15-point Specialist may be the most important model in the army on Turn 3.

Protect **functions**, not points.

## 11. Reserve prediction

Before the opponent places reserve, ask:

> **What important role has not appeared yet?**

Then:

> **Which missing piece gains the most from seeing my deployment?**

Common reserve candidates include:

- premium independent gunfighter choosing its lane;
- TAG choosing the best attack corridor;
- Impersonator choosing a victim;
- infiltrator choosing objective access;
- Minelayer countering a revealed route;
- defensive ARO matching your attacker;
- mission piece whose deployment depends on your scoring layout;
- final Fireteam member determining team position or function.

Do not finish main deployment with a single obvious weakness that says:

> **"Put the reserve here and my plan collapses."**

If one unrevealed piece can invalidate the entire deployment, preserve flexibility or use your own reserve to answer it.

---

## 12. Account for private and missing information

Matchup planning must include **uncertainty**.

Do not assume:

- every point is visibly deployed;
- the obvious Lieutenant is definitely the Lieutenant;
- an empty table edge is safe;
- an empty rooftop means no Hidden Deployment threat;
- the opponent lacks AD because none is visible;
- the visible Hacker is the only relevant network piece;
- a suspicious Camo marker is definitely a mine or definitely a troop.

Instead classify missing threats as:

### High-probability / high-impact

Build explicit protection.

Example: a faction/list shell strongly suggests an Impersonator or airborne attacker and your Lieutenant is vulnerable.

### Low-probability / high-impact

Avoid catastrophic geometry, but do not distort the whole army.

Example: one possible Combat Jump profile could land behind you, so leave a cheap rear guard rather than rotating the entire defence backward.

### High-probability / low-impact

Accept it and preserve resources for more important threats.

### Low-probability / low-impact

Ignore unless later information changes the assessment.

This prevents both **naive deployment** and **paralysis from imagining every possible hidden threat**.

## 13. Matchup-specific deployment questions

### Against a long-range gunfighter

Ask:

- Which lane does it want?
- Which of my pieces actually needs to contest that lane?
- Can I null deploy instead?
- Can I make it spend movement Orders before seeing anything valuable?
- Where will it finish if it pushes?
- Can my reserve counter its final lane?

Do not expose five models simply because one sniper can technically ARO.

### Against hacking pressure

Ask:

- Where can the first Repeater realistically arrive?
- Which firing/movement square enables that projection?
- Which Hackable piece is the intended victim?
- What does the opponent gain after Isolated/IMM/Targeted lands?
- Where is the Engineer and can recovery be denied?

Defend the **network route**, not merely the Hackable model.

### Against DZ strike

Ask:

- Which support/LT/Order cluster is the obvious payoff?
- Which table edge, roof, gap, or vertical route reaches it?
- What happens immediately after the attacker arrives?
- Can one cheap guard force an extra Order or bad trade?

The goal is often to make the **first Order after entry** expensive.

### Against Camo-heavy midfield

Ask:

- Is the opponent trying to kill me, delay me, or score around me?
- Which markers actually need Discovering?
- What route forces maximum Discover tax?
- Where are Sensor or template tools needed?
- Can I ignore part of the midfield and score elsewhere?

Do not turn every marker into a mandatory problem.

### Against a Fireteam

Ask:

- Which member is the actual gunfighter?
- Which member provides specialist utility?
- Which member is easiest to attack?
- Is the team's movement path predictable?
- Can templates, mines, hacking, or awkward vertical movement attack the team's structure rather than its strongest shooter?

### Against a TAG or premium HI

Ask:

- What lets it take repeated risks?
- Does it have Engineer support?
- What states actually disable its current role?
- Can it be ignored after being Isolated/IMM/Engaged?
- Does killing Orders achieve more than attacking the armour directly?

A strategically dead 70-point model does not need to become a physically dead 70-point model.

## 14. Turn-order implications

Opponent-plan identification should influence Initiative and Deployment decisions.

### Going first rises in value when

- the opponent has fragile but critical enablers exposed;
- their plan becomes much weaker if one network/support piece dies;
- they have a devastating first-turn attack that is easier to pre-empt than absorb;
- your own attack can reliably remove Orders or scoring infrastructure before it activates.

### Going second rises in value when

- the mission strongly rewards last-turn scoring;
- the opponent's alpha is easy to null deploy against;
- their army must advance into your control layers before becoming effective;
- you can preserve the scoring core while trading disposable pieces for tempo;
- their first turn is likely to be development rather than decisive damage.

### Keeping Deployment rises in value when

- table side materially changes attack routes;
- you need to counterdeploy a specific premium attacker;
- reserve information is especially important;
- one DZ is much safer against airborne/vertical threats;
- objective geometry strongly favours one side.

Do not decide Initiative in the abstract. Decide it **against the opponent's predicted plan on this mission and table**.

## 15. Plan to create Order debt

The matchup goal is usually not:

> **"Stop the opponent from doing anything."**

That is unrealistic.

A better goal is:

> **"Make the opponent's desired sequence cost more Orders than they can comfortably afford."**

Examples:

- force Discover before attacking;
- force movement around a mine;
- make the attacker change lanes;
- require a Repeater to be placed first;
- force a Dodge before reaching the target;
- make the Engineer spend Orders recovering a disabled piece;
- make a Fireteam reform or re-establish Coherency;
- force the premium attacker to retreat farther than planned;
- require a second attacker because the first matchup is bad.

If a six-Order plan becomes a nine-Order plan, the defence may already have succeeded.

Those extra Orders often remove the **payoff** or **extraction** step even if the breach itself succeeds.

---

## 16. Think about the ending square before the first Order

For each likely primary attacker, ask:

> **If this attack succeeds, where does the model probably finish?**

Then prepare the counterattack before the game begins.

Examples:

- TAG finishes midfield after killing an ARO → reserve AP attacker sees the expected lane;
- bike enters DZ and trades into support → cheap model survives nearby to finish it;
- Hacker advances to project Repeaters → KHD/counter-Hacker can reach the exposed network;
- Fireteam moves into objective room → templates/mines cover the likely cluster;
- Camo Specialist reaches console → Sensor or fast Specialist can contest next turn.

This turns Reactive Turn planning into **active-turn setup**.

A defence that loses pieces but leaves the opponent's premium attacker stranded in a punishable location may be strategically successful.

---

## 17. Re-evaluate after each reveal

The pregame plan is a hypothesis, not a commitment.

Update it when:

- reserve is placed;
- an unexpected Lieutenant clue appears;
- an airborne troop arrives;
- Hidden Deployment reveals;
- a Camo marker proves to be something different;
- the primary attacker dies or becomes disabled;
- an Engineer/Doctor/support piece is removed;
- the opponent starts scoring instead of attacking;
- the opponent commits Orders to a different Combat Group than expected.

Ask again:

1. What is their plan now?
2. Which piece is the new engine?
3. Which dependency matters now?
4. What can be ignored now?

Do not spend the whole game defending against the Turn-0 prediction after the board state has changed.

## 18. Practical 60-second matchup checklist

Use this before final deployment.

### Enemy plan

- **Primary attacker:** ______
- **Secondary attacker:** ______
- **Likely LT shell / command plan:** ______
- **Hacking network:** ______
- **DZ-strike threat:** ______
- **Scoring pieces:** ______
- **Recovery infrastructure:** ______
- **Likely reserve:** ______

### Turn-1 prediction

> **Their ideal Turn 1 is:** ______________________________________

### Dependency chain

- **Access:** ______
- **Enabler:** ______
- **Breach:** ______
- **Payoff:** ______
- **Extraction:** ______

### My response

- **One thing I must protect:** ______
- **One route I will tax:** ______
- **One dependency I can attack:** ______
- **One counter positioned for the ending square:** ______
- **Their likely pivot:** ______
- **My answer to that pivot:** ______

If those answers are clear, deployment becomes much less about vague fear of enemy profiles and much more about forcing bad Order economics.

## 19. Ultra-short mnemonic

Use:

> **MISSION → PLAN → ENGINE → ROUTE → PAYOFF → PIVOT**

### MISSION

How does this opponent most easily score?

### PLAN

What does their ideal first meaningful turn accomplish?

### ENGINE

Which piece or package consumes the Orders and enables that plan?

### ROUTE

Where must it physically go or project influence?

### PAYOFF

What of mine are they actually trying to remove, disable, or control?

### PIVOT

What do they do when I deny the obvious route?

Then make three concrete deployment decisions:

1. **Protect one thing** they most want to attack.
2. **Tax one route** their plan needs.
3. **Position one counter** to punish where the attack naturally ends.

That is the core of matchup planning.

---

## 20. Core principles

1. **Identify the opponent's plan, not just their best profile.**
2. **Predict the sequence that creates value.**
3. **Attack dependencies before armour when that is cheaper.**
4. **Defend routes and destination squares, not only starting positions.**
5. **Protect functions, not points.**
6. **Assume the opponent has a pivot.**
7. **Account for credible private-information threats without trying to cover everything.**
8. **Use reserve to solve information-sensitive matchups.**
9. **Create Order debt rather than demanding absolute denial.**
10. **Plan the counterattack around the enemy's likely ending square.**
11. **Re-evaluate when new information appears.**
12. **The best pregame question is: _What does their Turn 1 want to accomplish, and how do I make that expensive?_**
