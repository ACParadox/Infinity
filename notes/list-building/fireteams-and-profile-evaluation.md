# List-Building Strategy — Fireteams and Profile Evaluation

## Core Fireteam size

Five-trooper Core Fireteams are relatively rare because they are very unwieldy to maneuver and position efficiently. The most common Core configuration is a three-trooper Core.

## Prefer Fireteam Level 2

As a general list-building principle, Fireteams should usually be built to reach at least the **Level 2 Fireteam bonus**.

That normally means either:

- a **Duo** with two troopers that share the same Unit name or Counts-as keyword; or
- a **Haris/Core** containing at least two troopers that share the same Unit name or Counts-as keyword.

This is especially important for **ARO pieces and low-Burst weapons**, because **BS Attack (+1SD)** has a disproportionately large effect when the weapon starts with a small number of dice.

There are legitimate exceptions. A Duo with no Level 2 bonus can still be worthwhile when the two models have strong complementary roles—for example, a high-Burst attack piece paired with a specialist that provides a key capability the attacker lacks. In that case, the order efficiency and role pairing can justify giving up the Level 2 bonus.

Well-rounded or individually strong troopers can also be used solo when they do not need Fireteam bonuses or a partner to perform their role, but this is generally less common than building around a useful Fireteam package.

## Judge profiles by functional role, not troop classification

Troop labels such as **LI, MI, SK, or WB** are not reliable strategic roles by themselves. Infinity contains cheap LI, elite LI, line-troop MI, elite MI, skirmisher-like MI, and many non-SK troops with Camouflage or advanced deployment.

Evaluate a profile primarily by what it actually contributes:

- cost and Order type;
- deployment position and mobility;
- survivability;
- Face-to-Face competency;
- specialist capability;
- passive utility;
- deployment-zone or midfield control;
- ability to trade up;
- whether it needs Orders to contribute.

The troop classification is secondary to the profile's real battlefield function.

## Medium-cost and Medium Infantry profiles

There is no single generic "Medium Infantry strategy." Mid-priced profiles vary too much for the MI classification alone to determine their role.

The important principle is that a **medium-cost model should not merely be expensive line infantry**. Once a trooper costs enough that it is no longer an efficient cheerleader, it should justify that cost with meaningful competency or role compression.

A medium-cost profile should generally offer at least one of the following:

- strong Face-to-Face capability through BS, Mimetism, MSV, Surprise Attack, Marksmanship, or a strong weapon;
- meaningful resilience such as NWI, multiple Wounds, Immunity, strong ARM/BTS, or strong defensive MODs;
- valuable deployment such as Infiltration, Forward Deployment, Hidden Deployment, or Airborne Deployment;
- reliable mission capability with enough mobility or protection to actually reach objectives;
- multiple synergistic tools that are likely to matter from the same battlefield position.

Avoid paying a medium price for a model that is mediocre at several unrelated tasks and excellent at none of them.

## Toolbox units and multipurpose characters

Multipurpose units are strongest when their capabilities are both **competent and synergistic**.

A long list of skills is not automatically valuable. A good toolbox unit can perform several useful jobs without radically changing where it wants to be or how the list wants to spend Orders.

Examples of useful role compression include:

- competent gunfighter plus specialist;
- frontline hacker plus useful gun;
- Engineer accompanying the TAG or REMs it already wants to support;
- Sensor plus specialist plus useful deployable equipment;
- durable attacker that can also score objectives;
- attack piece that also contributes passive support or command utility.

Beware **toolbox bloat**: paying points for several mediocre or incompatible capabilities that will rarely be used in the same game plan.

Good generalists are also among the better candidates for **solo use**, because they are more likely to find a productive use for Orders without needing a Fireteam partner to make them functional.

## Fireteam positioning and deliberately leaving the team

Fireteam membership is an efficiency tool, not a requirement that every member remain linked forever.

A member can sometimes gain more value by **leaving the Fireteam for position** than by preserving the team bonus. Deliberately breaking Coherency or otherwise leaving the team can be correct when it lets a trooper:

- occupy a stronger ARO position;
- remain behind while the attack element advances;
- reach an objective or flank that the rest of the team cannot follow efficiently;
- avoid dragging the whole team through a dangerous lane;
- preserve a specialist or support piece in safety;
- exploit mobility that is being constrained by slower Fireteam members.

Treat Fireteam bonuses as resources to trade for board position when the position is more valuable.

## Choose the Fireteam Leader deliberately

All Fireteam members activate when the team receives a Fireteam Order, but for non-Movement/Reset actions **only the Fireteam Leader normally performs the declared Skill while the other members Idle**. Leader selection therefore determines which profile can actually exploit the second half of many Orders.

Choose the Leader according to the intended Order rather than leaving the token on the same model by habit.

Useful heuristics:

- when attacking, make the actual gunfighter, hacker, specialist, or other acting piece the Leader;
- when merely repositioning a defensive team, a low-value or well-protected member can often be a convenient Leader;
- a Prone Doctor/Paramedic, protected Lieutenant, or other backline member can be a reasonable default Leader for a defensive team when no active attack is planned;
- do **not** automatically make a Hacker the Leader unless the Order is intended to use that Hacker's programs or other capabilities;
- NCO or Tactical Awareness users spending their Tactical Order automatically become the Fireteam Leader, so account for that when planning the Order.

Leader choice does **not** make the other Fireteam members inactive for ARO-generation purposes: all members are activated, and reactive troopers may choose different Fireteam members as targets. Do not treat a safe Leader as a way to hide the rest of the team from AROs.

## TinBots and Fireteams

TinBots are especially efficient in Fireteams because **every member of the Fireteam benefits from the TinBot while its owner remains functional and eligible**.

For Firewall TinBots, this means one carrier can protect every Hackable member of the team—including a Hacker—from hostile Comms Attacks.

This creates several list-building implications:

- a TinBot carrier can be worth including even when the carrier itself is not the main attack piece;
- a Fireteam Hacker is substantially more attractive when the team can provide a Firewall TinBot;
- the TinBot owner is a support lynchpin and should not be exposed casually if the protection matters;
- multiple TinBots granting the same advantage do not stack; use the best applicable one.

The **Hacking Device and programs themselves are not shared** through the Fireteam. The TinBot protection is shared; each Hacker still uses only its own device/programs.

## Fireteams as movement packages

A Fireteam is often best understood as a way to **move several useful models with one Order** and then let the appropriate member act as Leader when needed.

A strong small team commonly combines roles that want to travel to roughly the same place, such as:

- gunfighter + specialist;
- gunfighter + Hacker with TinBot support;
- attack piece + Doctor/Engineer;
- long-range piece + short-range/template defender.

Avoid linking profiles whose preferred movement routes are fundamentally incompatible. If a Super-Jump, Climbing Plus, very fast, or otherwise highly mobile model is constantly forced to travel at the pace and route of ordinary infantry, the Fireteam may be destroying much of the value paid for that mobility.

# Advanced Fireteam Tactics

The most useful advanced mental model is:

> **A Fireteam is a multi-model activation and positioning system first, and a bonus package second.**

Its strength is not merely better rolls. A Fireteam changes how many models can reposition for one Order, which models generate AROs, where Coherency is centered, which model performs the important action, and when several models can be delivered into useful independent positions.

The corresponding danger is that **every Fireteam Order activates multiple models**. A link can therefore generate more enemy reactions, expose more valuable pieces, create template opportunities, or drag support models into danger even when only one member actually needs to act.

## Leader cycling is a core tactical skill

Do not think of the Fireteam Leader as a permanent job. Treat leadership as an **Order-by-Order resource**.

Use the appropriate model as Leader for the task:

- long-range gunfighter for the gunfight;
- Hacker for the hacking attack;
- specialist for the objective interaction;
- close-range or CC piece for the final assault;
- protected central model when the main purpose of the Order is repositioning.

Changing Leader also changes the **center of Coherency**. This is strategically important. A model on the edge of the formation may be the best attacker but a terrible movement anchor because making it Leader leaves the opposite side of the Fireteam outside Coherency.

Before switching Leader, ask:

1. What model needs to perform the second Skill?
2. What model should be the center of the team's movement this Order?
3. Which members will still be within Coherency after the switch?
4. If this is the final Fireteam Order of the turn, is this the Leader I want exposed in the Reactive Turn?

A good habit is to finish the turn with a **protected, centrally placed Leader** whenever practical. Leaving an exposed HMG, Missile Launcher, Hacker, or other high-priority model as Leader creates an avoidable failure point because losing or Isolating the Leader can collapse the Fireteam.

### NCO and Tactical Awareness can force a leadership change

If a Fireteam member spends an NCO/Tactical Awareness-type Tactical Order and the rules automatically make that model Leader, treat that as part of the cost of the Order.

A "free" Order is not free if the new Leader position:

- sheds a member through Coherency;
- exposes the TinBot carrier;
- pulls the Hacker into a hostile Repeater network;
- forces the rest of the team toward bad terrain;
- leaves the wrong model as the reactive-turn Leader.

Check the geometry before spending the Tactical Order.

## Keep the Leader safe while moving nonleaders aggressively

All members can move with the Fireteam's movement Skills, but they do not need to follow the same path or move the same distance.

This allows the Leader to remain protected while another member:

- crosses a gap;
- enters a doorway;
- rounds a corner;
- approaches an objective;
- moves toward the edge of Coherency;
- tests a dangerous lane;
- occupies a position the Leader does not need to occupy.

This can generate **ARO target pressure**.

Suppose a cheap or durable nonleader crosses the LoF of a reactive shooter while the Fireteam Leader also becomes a valid target. That reactive trooper can target only one activated Fireteam member. If it shoots the nonleader, the Leader may get to attack without being opposed by that model. If it opposes the Leader, the moving nonleader may reach its new position uncontested.

This is not a universal way to create Normal Rolls because different reactive troopers can choose different Fireteam members as targets. It is most useful against **one important reactive piece**.

The nonleader being exposed is still at real risk. Appropriate pieces for this role include:

- cheap members;
- durable HI;
- models whose new position is worth the risk;
- expendable short-range pieces.

Avoid casually using the Doctor, Engineer, TinBot carrier, Lieutenant, or critical specialist as the decoy movement piece.

## Track the Fireteam's full ARO footprint

Do not plan Fireteam movement only from the Leader's perspective.

Every activated member can generate enemy AROs. The Fireteam's effective **ARO footprint** is therefore the combined set of interactions created by all its members:

- Line of Fire;
- Zone of Control;
- Hacking Area;
- Repeaters;
- Mines and Perimeter weapons;
- template threat zones;
- Discover/Delay interactions;
- other triggered reactive effects.

A trailing HI that remains inside an enemy Repeater may generate hacking AROs every time the Fireteam activates even if that HI does not meaningfully move. A support model walking around the safe side of a wall may still enter ZoC of a hidden threat that cannot interact with the Leader.

Before an important Fireteam Order, ask:

> **Who can react to any member of this team if I activate it?**

This question is often more important than "Who can see my Leader?"

## Template spacing: use the entire Coherency bubble

Fireteam rules encourage proximity. Direct and impact templates punish proximity.

Do not build compact model stacks unless terrain forces it. The goal is to remain coherent while denying the opponent efficient multi-model attacks.

Useful formations include:

- staggered lines;
- shallow arcs;
- offset columns;
- loose clusters behind different pieces of cover;
- one exposed ARO model with the remaining members displaced laterally behind it.

At a doorway or corner, avoid lining multiple members immediately behind the point model. A Chain Rifle, Flamethrower, shotgun template, impact template, or speculative attack should ideally threaten **one important member**, not the whole support package.

Think about the likely **direction of attack**, not merely the distance between bases. Models that appear well separated from above may still line up perfectly for a template arriving from the flank.

Protect especially:

- Hacker + TinBot carrier combinations;
- Doctor/Engineer + premium gunfighter combinations;
- Lieutenant + decoy/support clusters;
- multiple specialists needed for the mission.

## Slingshot and rotating-coherency movement

Do not move a Fireteam as a rigid formation. Move it like an **accordion**.

A useful sequence is:

1. Keep the current Leader relatively central.
2. Move one or more members toward the forward edge of the Leader's Coherency.
3. Move rear members toward the center rather than forcing them to copy the lead model's exact route.
4. On the next Order, spend the Order on the forward member if useful, making it the new Leader.
5. Move the rest of the team forward relative to the new Coherency center.

This effectively **rotates the Coherency bubble** across the table.

The technique is useful for:

- turning corners;
- crossing between buildings;
- moving through alleys;
- bringing a specialist from the rear to the front;
- shifting an ARO model from one lane to another;
- allowing a faster model to exploit more of its MOV without immediately abandoning the team.

The movement is not free and each model still obeys its own MOV. The advantage is that the Fireteam is no longer wasting movement by trying to preserve a fixed geometric formation.

## Mixed MOV values: leapfrog instead of marching

A 6-X model should not automatically move only 4 inches because some linked partners have MOV 4-X.

Use the fast model's extra movement to:

- reach the forward edge of Coherency;
- move laterally around terrain;
- reach better cover;
- prepare to become the next Leader;
- set up the next Order's attack angle.

Meanwhile, slower members can take shorter and more direct paths.

The real constraint is not that everyone must move the same distance. It is that the **final formation must remain tactically useful and coherent**.

If slower members continuously prevent the fast member from using the mobility paid for on its profile, stop assuming the team must remain intact. At that point the Fireteam may be destroying more efficiency than it creates.

## Climbing and jumping are Fireteam stress tests

Vertical movement exposes incompatibilities inside Fireteams quickly.

Jump, Climb, Super-Jump, Climbing Plus, different MOV values, Silhouette size, ladders, narrow ledges, and terrain geometry may make a linked route much less efficient than the same route taken by one model.

Plan vertical movement around the **least mobile member you actually need to retain**, not automatically around the strongest movement Skill in the team.

Common correct choices include:

- letting the Super-Jump attacker leave the team;
- leaving a cheap filler below while the useful members continue upward;
- splitting the specialist toward the objective while the gunfighter takes the vertical route;
- choosing a different Leader before the movement so Coherency is centered on the correct elevation.

Do not spend several Orders dragging ordinary infantry through awkward vertical geometry solely to preserve a Fireteam bonus on one highly mobile model.

## Number 2 is leader-loss insurance

Number 2 should be treated as **Fireteam continuity insurance**, not as a general-purpose protection skill.

Its main tactical value is allowing the team to survive the current Leader entering a qualifying disabling/Null condition without immediately losing the Fireteam structure.

This makes Number 2 particularly useful in teams where the Leader is expected to take risks:

- aggressive gunfighter Haris;
- HI link pushing through hacking zones;
- active Hacker Leader;
- premium model repeatedly exposing itself to AROs.

The Number 2 model should usually be:

- protected;
- not standing beside the current Leader in the same template or attack lane;
- central enough that inheriting leadership preserves useful Coherency.

Do not overestimate the protection. Number 2 does not solve every form of Fireteam fragmentation. Coherency loss, ARO disagreement, individual members entering incompatible States, and voluntary separation still need to be managed normally.

## Protect the TinBot carrier as infrastructure

A Fireteam-wide TinBot makes its carrier a **support lynchpin**.

Do not evaluate the carrier only by its own profile. If several Hackable models depend on that Firewall, the carrier effectively holds part of the defensive value of the entire team.

Therefore:

- do not casually make it the member that crosses the exposed lane;
- do not leave it as the only model covering a disposable ARO;
- keep it away from obvious E/M, hacking, or isolation attacks when possible;
- keep it safely inside Coherency while the gunfighter/Hacker works near the edge;
- consider whether an opponent can remove the carrier first and then attack the rest of the Fireteam more efficiently.

Against a hacking-heavy opponent, the TinBot carrier may be a **higher strategic-value target than the Hacker itself** because removing it can weaken every other Hackable member.

## Hackers in reactive Fireteams

Multiple Hackers inside one Fireteam can create a strong reactive hacking architecture.

When the Fireteam makes an appropriate Hacking ARO, different reactive Hackers can often use **different programs**, allowing the team to present complementary consequences instead of simply duplicating the same attack.

Depending on devices and targets, this can create combinations such as:

- Immobilization pressure;
- Isolation pressure;
- Targeted pressure;
- anti-Hacker attacks;
- different programs chosen for different active models in multi-model activations.

A Firewall TinBot strengthens this architecture further.

The major constraint is **ARO compatibility**. A linked Hacker may want to hack while a linked gunfighter seeing the same active model may want to BS Attack, and another threatened member may want to Dodge. Fireteam ARO rules can punish those divergent incentives by ejecting members or destabilizing the team.

Good defensive Fireteam placement therefore aims to create **compatible reactive layers**, not merely the largest possible number of AROs.

For example:

- long-range gun watches the main lane;
- Hacker network protects an alternate approach;
- template weapon guards the close route;
- not every defensive member is simultaneously forced to answer the same Order in a different way.

## Deliberate fragmentation is often the correct lifecycle

A Fireteam does not need to remain intact for the entire game to justify its cost.

A very strong pattern is:

> **Fireteam as delivery system → mission position → controlled fragmentation → independent defence.**

Use the link to move several models efficiently into the midfield. Once they arrive, the Fireteam may have already delivered most of its value.

From there:

- specialist stays near the objective;
- gunfighter holds a lane;
- short-range piece guards a doorway;
- Hacker remains behind Firewall/repeater coverage;
- fast attacker continues alone;
- Doctor/Engineer remains in a protected recovery position.

The loss of the Fireteam bonus is sometimes much less important than gaining **multiple individually useful board positions**.

Members that leave an otherwise surviving Fireteam may be able to rejoin at the appropriate States Phase if they regain the required Coherency. If the team itself has been cancelled and must be newly created, account for the Command Token and legal Fireteam-chart requirements.

## Late-turn Suppressive Fire conversion

Suppressive Fire is one of the best reasons to stop preserving a Fireteam late in the turn.

A trooper that enters Suppressive Fire leaves the Fireteam, so this is deliberately incompatible with maintaining the existing link structure. That is not necessarily a drawback.

A useful sequence is:

1. Use the Fireteam to move several members efficiently.
2. Complete the attack or objective run.
3. Position useful members in separate defensive locations.
4. End the Fireteam lifecycle deliberately.
5. Put suitable surviving models into Suppressive Fire individually.

This converts:

> **one concentrated movement package into several independent reactive zones.**

It is particularly strong after the team reaches the midfield or an objective room, where the models no longer need to travel together.

Do not preserve a Fireteam bonus merely because it still exists if Suppressive Fire creates more future Order cost for the opponent.

## Rebuild damaged teams according to their new job

Do not mentally treat a damaged Core as a failed version of its starting composition.

A five-member defensive Core that loses two members may become a very effective three-model mobile package. For example:

- gunfighter + specialist + Hacker;
- attacker + TinBot carrier + Engineer;
- specialist + template defender + long-range weapon.

After casualties, reassess the survivors from scratch:

- What Fireteam bonuses remain?
- What range bands remain?
- Is there still a useful specialist?
- Is the TinBot still functional?
- Which member should now be Leader?
- Is the remaining team more useful moving, defending, or deliberately fragmenting?

If the Fireteam actually collapsed and you choose to recreate one with a Command Token, build the **best Fireteam for the current turn and mission state**, not a ceremonial reconstruction of the Turn 1 link.

A Turn 3 Fireteam often needs to be smaller, faster, and more scoring-focused than the Turn 1 defensive package.

## When not to activate the entire Fireteam

This is one of the most important advanced Fireteam skills.

A Fireteam Order is attractive because it moves several models for one Order. But activating several models is not always an advantage.

Avoid using the Fireteam activation blindly when:

- only one model needs to move through a hacking zone;
- a fast model wants to exploit mobility the rest of the team cannot follow;
- the whole team would generate unnecessary AROs;
- a trailing Hackable member remains inside an enemy Repeater;
- multiple members would become vulnerable to a template;
- moving the team exposes a Doctor, Engineer, TinBot carrier, Lieutenant, or important specialist;
- vertical terrain makes Coherency inefficient;
- the desired model needs to enter Suppressive Fire;
- the desired model needs a State or action incompatible with Fireteam membership;
- the Fireteam has already completed its delivery role.

When a linked model needs to act alone and a Fireteam Order would create excessive risk, the correct answer may be to **break or cancel the team first** rather than treating the Fireteam as mandatory.

The key question is not:

> **Do I still get Fireteam bonuses?**

The better question is:

> **Does activating these extra models improve this Order, or merely give the opponent more things to react to?**

That question should govern advanced Fireteam use.

## Advanced Fireteam checklist

Before spending an important Fireteam Order, quickly check:

1. **Leader:** Is the correct model Leader for both the action and Coherency geometry?
2. **ARO footprint:** What enemy pieces can react to *any* activated member?
3. **Templates:** Are multiple valuable members lined up or clustered?
4. **Hacking:** Does any Hackable member enter or remain in a hostile Hacking Area?
5. **TinBot:** Is the carrier being exposed unnecessarily?
6. **Movement:** Are fast/mobile members being constrained more than the link is helping them?
7. **Coherency:** Where should everyone finish, not merely where should the Leader finish?
8. **Reactive turn:** Who will be Leader after the Order, and where will each member defend?
9. **Fragmentation:** Has the Fireteam already completed its delivery job?
10. **Alternative:** Would activating only the important model—after intentionally breaking/cancelling the team if necessary—produce a safer or more efficient Order?

The advanced Fireteam player is not the player who preserves the link longest. It is the player who extracts the most **movement, bonuses, positioning, ARO pressure, and mission value** from the Fireteam before giving up the structure at exactly the right moment.
