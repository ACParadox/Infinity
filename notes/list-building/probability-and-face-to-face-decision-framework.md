# Probability and Face-to-Face Decision Framework

Infinity probability is most useful as a **decision framework**, not as a giant lookup table.

The practical question is rarely just:

> What are my odds?

Instead separate the interaction into three gates:

1. **Can I win the interaction?** — Face-to-Face probability.
2. **If I win it, will the attack actually do anything?** — hits, Saving Rolls, ammunition, VITA/STR, States.
3. **Is that outcome worth the Orders and risk?** — strategic value, opportunity cost, and downside if the attack fails.

This distinction matters throughout Infinity because:

> **FtF advantage != wound probability != incapacitation probability != good decision.**

A model can dominate a Face-to-Face Roll but still be inefficient at removing the target. A low-percentage attack can still be correct if failure is cheap and success is transformational.

---

## 1. Start With the Actual Dice

Before reasoning about an attack, reduce it to a compact description:

> **B4 on 11s versus B1 on 8s**

Then ask:

- Is this a Face-to-Face Roll or a Normal Roll?
- How many dice does each side roll?
- What is each side's Success Value after all MODs?
- What happens after a successful hit?
- Does one failed Save solve the problem, or are several needed?

Avoid judging an interaction from profile BS/WIP alone.

A BS14 gunfighter can still be making a poor attack if it is firing outside its range band, through Cover, into Mimetism, or against an opponent whose range band is better.

---

# 2. Burst and Target Number Do Different Jobs

A common mistake is treating Burst and Success Value as interchangeable.

They are not.

## Burst gives more attempts

Higher Burst:

- reduces the chance of rolling no successes;
- creates more chances to roll a Crit;
- creates more chances to produce several surviving hits;
- makes low individual target numbers less catastrophic;
- improves consistency.

## Higher Success Value raises the ceiling

In a Face-to-Face Roll, higher successful results matter because they cancel lower enemy successes.

Suppose you are rolling against an enemy success of 10.

A model shooting on 8s cannot normally beat that result except with a Crit.

A model shooting on 14s can beat it with 11, 12, 13, or 14.

This means Success Value has an extra function in FtF rolls:

> **It determines not only how often you succeed, but how high your ordinary successful rolls can go.**

This is why stacking negative MODs on the opponent and positive MODs on yourself is so powerful.

---

# 3. B4 on 8s Versus B2 on 14s

This is a useful benchmark because it shows why raw Burst does not tell the whole story.

On an unopposed Normal Roll:

- B4 on 8s succeeds at least once very reliably;
- B2 on 14s also succeeds very reliably;
- B4 produces slightly more expected successes.

But in a Face-to-Face Roll, the B2-on-14 attacker gains something B4-on-8 lacks:

- access to successful results from 9 through 14;
- the ability to cancel strong enemy successes without needing a Crit.

Therefore:

> **Against an opposed roll, favor the better Success Value more heavily than raw Burst suggests.**

> **Against a Normal Roll, Burst becomes relatively more valuable because nothing cancels your lower successes.**

### Practical heuristic

Choose **B4 on 8s** more readily when:

- the attack is Normal rather than FtF;
- multiple hits are especially valuable;
- the higher-Burst weapon has much better ammunition or PS;
- your failure branch is cheap;
- you only need some effect to land.

Choose **B2 on 14s** more readily when:

- the opponent is contesting you;
- the opponent has a meaningful Success Value;
- winning the FtF matters more than maximizing potential hit count;
- losing the FtF has serious consequences.

---

# 4. MOD Stacking Usually Comes Before Increasing Lethality

When preparing an attack, improving the FtF often matters more than increasing PS or choosing a more lethal ammunition type.

A strong sequence is often:

1. get into the correct range band;
2. force the opponent into a bad range band;
3. obtain or deny Cover;
4. exploit Mimetism, Surprise Attack, smoke/MSV, Targeted, visibility, etc.;
5. then choose the most efficient ammunition or damage mode.

Why?

Because a better weapon does nothing if its successful attack rolls are canceled.

This suggests a useful principle:

> **Improve the probability of delivering the hit before optimizing what the hit does.**

But this is not absolute.

Once you already dominate the FtF, damage can become the bottleneck.

For example:

- B4 Normal ammunition that wins easily but bounces off a TAG may be inferior to lower-Burst AP/DA/EXP;
- an attack that reliably causes only one weak Save may still take too many Orders to remove a durable target.

At that point the question changes from:

> How do I win the FtF?

To:

> How do I convert the FtF win into enough failed Saves or States?

---

# 5. Turning a Face-to-Face Roll Into a Normal Roll Is Often Better Than Improving BS

One of the strongest tactical improvements in Infinity is not increasing your target number.

It is removing the opponent's ability to oppose you at all.

A Normal Roll means:

- the opponent cannot cancel your successful dice;
- low successful rolls remain useful;
- Burst converts directly into multiple hits more often;
- Crits are no longer needed to beat an enemy's higher successful result.

This is why the following effects are strategically powerful:

- flanking;
- attacking from outside LoF;
- forcing the target to ARO against another threat;
- Coordinated Orders that create several simultaneous threats;
- hacking through unexpected Repeaters;
- templates when the target cannot oppose the attack effectively;
- attacking a model while another reactive model makes an unopposed shot;
- forcing bad ARO declarations.

A +3 MOD is useful.

Removing the opposing roll entirely can be much more important.

### Defensive implication

The same principle works in reverse.

A second ARO that cannot be opposed may be more dangerous than improving the first ARO.

This is one of the reasons crossfires and layered reactive pieces work.

---

# 6. +1SD Is Disproportionately Strong at Low Burst

Special Dice (+1SD) gives an additional die, then one die is discarded after seeing the rolls according to the skill rules.

Its value is unusually high when the original Burst is small.

Conceptually:

- B1 -> B1 +1SD is a massive reliability increase;
- B2 -> B2 +1SD is still very strong;
- B4 -> B4 +1SD is useful, but the marginal improvement is much smaller.

Why?

At B1, the extra die dramatically reduces the chance that your entire attack whiffs.

At B4, you were already unlikely to roll no useful result.

### Practical consequence

Give extra respect to +1SD on:

- Missile Launchers;
- Panzerfausts;
- Flash Pulses;
- low-Burst hacking AROs;
- CC attacks;
- powerful B1 weapons;
- disposable reactive attacks.

The important distinction is that +1SD improves **selection and reliability** without necessarily increasing the number of final hits in the same way true Burst does.

Therefore:

> **+1SD is often closer to an extra Burst die for winning the FtF than it is for increasing damage output.**

---

# 7. Crits Matter, but Do Not Make Bad Fights Good

For ordinary Success Values of 20 or less, each die normally has one Critical result.

So more dice produce more opportunities to Crit.

Crits matter because they can:

- defeat ordinary enemy successes that would otherwise beat you;
- create additional Saving Rolls from attacks;
- produce high-impact tail outcomes.

But Crit chance is not a sound plan by itself.

A poor FtF does not become a good FtF merely because:

> I can always Crit.

Instead:

> **Treat Crits as tail risk, not as your primary attack plan.**

This matters especially in the Reactive Turn.

Even a weak B1 ARO can retain a small but meaningful chance to punish an attacker, so no FtF involving a dangerous weapon is completely risk-free unless you remove or bypass the reactive roll.

---

# 8. Winning the FtF and Hurting the Target Are Separate Events

This is one of the most important probability distinctions in Infinity.

Suppose an attack has:

- a high chance to win the FtF;
- but usually lands only one hit;
- against a target that has a strong Saving Roll.

The attack may feel dominant while still being poor at actually removing the target.

Think in stages:

### Gate 1 — Win the FtF

Can your attack produce surviving successes?

### Gate 2 — Convert successes into hits

How many uncanceled hits are likely to remain?

### Gate 3 — Force failed Saves

What Save Attribute is being used?

- ARM?
- BTS?
- something else?

What modifiers or ammunition apply?

### Gate 4 — Produce the required game-state change

Does the target need:

- one wound;
- two or three wounds;
- Unconscious;
- Dead;
- IMM;
- Isolated;
- Targeted;
- Possessed;
- Engaged;
- simply forced to Guts out of position?

This produces the key distinction:

> **Chance to win the FtF is not the same as chance to incapacitate the target.**

---

# 9. Multiple Saves Compound Quickly

Once a hit has landed, forcing multiple Saves can increase lethality far more rapidly than intuition suggests.

If one Save has probability `p` of causing a wound, the chance that at least one wound occurs across `n` independent Saves is approximately:

`1 - (1 - p)^n`

Example: if each Save has a 35% chance to cause a wound:

| Saves | Chance of at least one wound |
|---|---:|
| 1 | 35% |
| 2 | 58% |
| 3 | 73% |
| 4 | 82% |

Therefore:

- DA/EXP-type multiple-Save effects can radically improve conversion after a hit;
- Crits can matter a great deal because the extra Save compounds damage probability;
- Burst that produces multiple uncanceled hits can overcome strong ARM/BTS through volume;
- one highly reliable hit is not always enough against multi-wound or heavily armored models.

This is why a weapon should not be judged only by its FtF profile.

---

# 10. Expected Orders Is Often More Useful Than Single-Roll Probability

Infinity is an Order-efficiency game.

For a repeated stationary problem where the chance of incapacitating the target remains roughly constant from attempt to attempt:

`Expected Orders ~= 1 / probability of incapacitating per Order`

Useful rough benchmarks:

| Removal chance per Order | Rough expected Orders |
|---|---:|
| 80% | 1.25 |
| 70% | 1.43 |
| 50% | 2.0 |
| 40% | 2.5 |
| 30% | 3.3 |
| 25% | 4.0 |
| 20% | 5.0 |

This is why:

> I win the FtF 70% of the time.

can be misleading.

If most wins produce only one weak hit and the target often passes the Save, actual incapacitation chance may be far lower.

For example:

- 70% chance to win the FtF;
- but only about 50% of winning outcomes ultimately remove the target;

means actual removal probability may be closer to 35%.

That implies roughly **2.9 expected Orders** for the removal, not 1.4.

### Important limitation

Real Infinity attacks are not perfectly stationary repeated trials.

After each Order:

- the target may Guts away;
- either model may be wounded;
- the target may become Unconscious;
- range may change;
- ammunition may run out;
- the attacker may die;
- another route may open;
- support pieces may become available.

So expected-Order calculations are heuristics, not promises.

They are still extremely useful for deciding whether an ARO is likely to consume one Order or an entire turn.

---

# 11. Decide an Order Budget Before Starting the Fight

Before attacking a durable ARO, decide:

> **How many Orders am I willing to spend before I stop?**

This prevents one stubborn reactive model from consuming the entire turn.

Example:

You have eight useful Orders left.

Your plan requires:

- two Orders to reach and press an objective;
- one Order to retreat;
- one likely retry.

You do not actually have eight Orders available for the gunfight.

You may only have four.

If the attack has roughly a 25% removal chance per Order, expecting to remove the target efficiently is already questionable.

Consider instead:

- another attack piece;
- another range band;
- smoke;
- hacking;
- speculative fire;
- a deployable;
- a template;
- Coordinated Orders;
- ignoring the model;
- attacking its support;
- scoring elsewhere.

---

# 12. Low-Probability Attacks Can Be Correct

A 30% attack is not automatically a bad attack.

Probability must be multiplied by consequence.

Consider:

### Disposable attack

- 30% chance to remove a 40-point critical ARO;
- attacker costs 8 points;
- failure costs one Order and possibly the attacker.

This can be excellent.

### Premium attack

- 75% chance your only major gunfighter wins;
- meaningful chance it becomes Unconscious or dies;
- losing it collapses the rest of the turn.

This can be strategically worse despite the better raw odds.

The proper framework is:

> **Probability x impact x cost of failure.**

### Take low-percentage attacks more readily when

- the attacker is disposable;
- the Order cost is low;
- the target is strategically crucial;
- success breaks a Fireteam or defensive network;
- the attack removes an Engineer/Doctor/Lieutenant rather than just points;
- the attacker is unlikely to be useful later;
- it is Turn 3 and preservation matters less;
- there is no safer route.

### Avoid even favorable attacks when

- the failure branch is catastrophic;
- the attack piece is irreplaceable;
- the mission can be won without the fight;
- you are gambling Orders required for scoring;
- a safer setup Order produces a materially better fight;
- you are attacking for points rather than mission effect.

---

# 13. Risk Is Asymmetric

Do not compare only:

- my chance to win;
- their chance to win.

Compare the **consequences** of each outcome.

A 15% enemy success can matter more than your 70% success if:

- your attacker is a 70-point TAG;
- their defender is a 15-point Missile Launcher;
- your TAG is your only attack plan;
- their model is intentionally disposable.

Conversely, a mediocre 35% attack may be correct when your attacker is expendable and the target is mission-critical.

This gives a useful question:

> **Who benefits from variance?**

The player with the cheaper disposable piece often benefits from increasing variance.

The player protecting the premium piece often wants to reduce variance.

---

# 14. Null Outcomes Usually Favor the Defender

A Face-to-Face Roll can end with neither side achieving its goal.

Examples:

- both sides fail;
- successful rolls cancel;
- Crits cancel;
- the attacker hits but the defender passes every Save.

The attacker still spent the Order.

This means:

> **A reactive piece does not need to kill the attacker to be successful.**

If it repeatedly forces null outcomes, it may consume several Orders and accomplish its defensive job.

This is fundamental to defensive architecture.

A 15-point ARO that costs the opponent three Orders before dying may have generated enormous strategic value.

---

# 15. Probability Changes by Turn and Mission Context

The same dice can represent different decisions depending on the game state.

## Turn 1

Preservation often matters more.

You may prefer:

- setup;
- development;
- low-risk attacks;
- forcing the opponent to spend Orders next turn.

## Turn 2

Trading becomes more acceptable if it opens the board or removes key infrastructure.

## Turn 3

Probability often becomes secondary to scoring necessity.

If you need an objective and have only two Orders, a 35% line may be the correct line because the 0% line is doing nothing.

This leads to another principle:

> **Probability should be evaluated against the alternatives, not against perfection.**

A 30% plan is excellent when every other available plan is 10% or impossible.

---

# 16. Compare Game-State Outcomes, Not Just Wounds

The best attack is not always the one most likely to kill.

Ask what each success changes.

Examples:

- Isolating a TAG may make killing it unnecessary;
- Immobilizing an ARO may be enough to cross the lane;
- forcing Guts may open the objective;
- breaking a Fireteam may be worth more than killing one member's points;
- Targeted may make the next attack dramatically easier;
- Engaging a gunfighter may neutralize it even without damage;
- knocking a Doctor Unconscious may make several other models effectively unrecoverable.

Therefore:

> **Optimize for the game-state change you need, not the largest damage number.**

---

# 17. Setup Orders Need Their Own Probability Test

Players sometimes over-invest in improving an attack.

Suppose the direct attack has a moderate chance of succeeding.

You could instead spend two Orders to:

- deploy a Repeater;
- Spotlight;
- move into a better range band;

and then make a much stronger attack.

That setup may be correct.

But if the target was already likely to die in one or two Orders, spending two extra Orders before attacking can be worse.

The correct comparison is:

> **Expected total Orders for setup + attack versus expected Orders for the original attack.**

Do not automatically spend Orders for every possible +3 MOD.

A setup Order is only worthwhile if the improved probability or reduced risk justifies its cost.

---

# 18. When to Stop Attacking

One of the most important probability skills is recognizing when the realized outcome has diverged from the expected outcome.

Suppose you expected a gunfight to take roughly two Orders.

You have spent four Orders and the defender is still standing.

The original plan is no longer relevant.

Recalculate from the current state.

Ask:

- How many Orders remain?
- What must still be scored?
- Has the target moved because of Guts?
- Is the attacker damaged?
- Is another route now better?
- Has the defender already accomplished its purpose by draining Orders?

Do not continue simply because:

> I've already spent four Orders on this.

Those Orders are sunk cost.

---

# 19. Practical FtF Heuristics

These are deliberately approximate table-side rules rather than exact probability thresholds.

### Strong active-turn fight

Usually attractive when:

- you have significantly more Burst;
- your Success Value is at least comparable to the defender's;
- losing the FtF is not catastrophic;
- the attack meaningfully advances the mission.

### Suspicious active-turn fight

Reconsider when:

- your Burst is high but your Success Value is very low;
- the defender rolls on a significantly higher number;
- one defender hit is disproportionately lethal;
- even a FtF win usually forces only weak Saves;
- the target has several wounds and strong ARM/BTS;
- the expected Order cost threatens scoring.

### Excellent disposable gamble

Often justified when:

- attacker value is low;
- defender value or strategic impact is high;
- one success can transform the board;
- failure costs little.

### Bad premium gamble

Often avoid when:

- attacker is essential;
- mission can be completed without the fight;
- the target is merely attractive rather than necessary;
- the defender has a small but extremely dangerous success branch.

---

# 20. Reactive-Turn Probability Thinking

Reactive models should not ask only:

> Can I kill the attacker?

Ask:

> **Which ARO creates the greatest expected future cost?**

Possible answers include:

- Shoot because even a low-percentage Missile hit threatens catastrophic loss;
- Dodge because survival preserves another ARO next Order;
- Reset because avoiding IMM/Isolated keeps the model strategically functional;
- Discover because revealing the marker enables later attacks;
- Delay because preserving information creates a stronger second-declaration decision;
- do nothing because revealing a marker now gives the opponent a cheap Normal Roll.

The best ARO may be the one that decreases the attacker's expected future efficiency rather than maximizing immediate wound probability.

See also `defensive-architecture-and-reactive-turn.md`.

---

# 21. Probability and Coordinated Orders

Coordinated Orders can deliberately change the probability structure.

The point is not simply to increase total Burst.

They can:

- force one reactive model to choose which attacker to oppose;
- leave other attackers making Normal Rolls;
- create multiple low-cost threats simultaneously;
- increase the defender's chance of choosing the wrong ARO target;
- exploit disposable weapons efficiently.

A mediocre individual attack can become strategically strong when the defender cannot oppose every participant.

See also `advanced-coordinated-orders.md`.

---

# 22. Probability and Trading

When comparing two attack options, do not merely compare expected points killed.

Consider expected strategic exchange.

A cheap model trading into:

- an Engineer supporting a TAG;
- a Fireteam member whose death breaks bonuses;
- the Lieutenant;
- a Doctor maintaining several Unconscious pieces;
- a midfield specialist needed for Turn 3;

may justify a lower probability than attacking a more expensive but strategically irrelevant target.

See also `trading-and-attrition.md`.

---

# 23. Probability and Weapons

Weapon selection should be separated into two questions.

## Question 1 — Which weapon best wins the FtF?

Consider:

- Burst;
- range band;
- visibility MODs;
- Cover interaction;
- Special Dice;
- whether the attack is Normal or FtF.

## Question 2 — Which weapon best converts the hit?

Consider:

- PS;
- ARM versus BTS;
- AP;
- multiple Saves;
- Continuous Damage;
- E/M;
- PARA;
- Stun;
- target wounds/STR;
- desired State rather than death.

A weapon can be best at the first question and poor at the second.

That distinction should drive weapon-mode selection.

---

# 24. Table-Side Decision Routine

When deciding whether to take a fight, run this sequence.

## Step 1 — State the dice

> B4 on 11s versus B1 on 8s.

## Step 2 — Ask if you can make it a Normal Roll

Can you:

- flank;
- smoke;
- hack;
- Coordinate;
- attack through another vector;
- force another ARO?

## Step 3 — Look for one cheap setup improvement

Can one Order materially improve:

- range;
- Cover;
- visibility;
- Targeted;
- Surprise;
- Burst;
- enemy Burst?

## Step 4 — Estimate conversion

If you win, roughly how many Saves will the target make?

Against which Attribute?

## Step 5 — Define success

Do you need:

- one wound;
- full removal;
- a State;
- displacement;
- Fireteam disruption;
- simply to cross the lane?

## Step 6 — Set an Order budget

How many Orders can you spend before the rest of the turn plan fails?

## Step 7 — Examine the bad branch

If the defender rolls well, what do you lose?

## Step 8 — Compare alternatives

Do not compare the attack to an imaginary perfect attack.

Compare it to the actual other actions available this turn.

---

# 25. Compact Heuristics

Keep these rules in mind at the table:

- **FtF probability and kill probability are different numbers.**
- **Higher Burst improves reliability; higher SV also raises your FtF ceiling.**
- **Against opposition, target number matters more than it first appears.**
- **Against Normal Rolls, raw Burst becomes more valuable.**
- **Turning an FtF into a Normal Roll is often better than another +3 MOD.**
- **+1SD is disproportionately valuable at B1 and B2.**
- **Crits are tail risk, not a plan.**
- **Multiple Saves compound lethality quickly.**
- **Expected Orders to solve the problem matters more than one-roll win percentage.**
- **Null outcomes favor the defender because the attacker still spends Orders.**
- **A 30% attack can be excellent when failure is cheap and success is decisive.**
- **A 75% attack can be bad when the failure branch destroys your plan.**
- **Decide how many Orders you will spend before beginning a stubborn fight.**
- **Setup Orders must save more expected Orders or risk than they cost.**
- **Recalculate after unexpected outcomes; do not chase sunk costs.**
- **Probability is always mission-relative.**

---

# Core Principle

> **Do not optimize the chance to win the die roll. Optimize the chance that this Order produces the game-state change you need, at an acceptable risk and Order cost.**

That is the probability framework that should sit underneath attack selection, ARO choice, weapon selection, trading, Coordinated Orders, State warfare, defensive architecture, and turn planning.
