# List-Building Strategy — Turn Planning and Order Budgeting

Turn planning is distinct from generic [Order efficiency](order-efficiency.md).

**Order efficiency** asks how much value an Order produces. **Order budgeting** asks how many Orders can be committed to one task before the rest of the turn becomes endangered.

A useful shorthand is:

> **Score → reserve → budget → attack → reassess → exit.**

The most important habit is:

> **Do not ask only “How many Orders do I have left?” Ask “How many Orders can I still afford to spend?”**

## 1. Build the turn backward from the required end state

Before deciding who attacks, decide what **must** be true at the end of the turn.

Examples:

- a console must be activated;
- a Specialist must reach a particular objective;
- an objective carrier must survive;
- a scoring zone must be controlled or contested;
- a model must be positioned to score at the end of the Round;
- an attacker must return to a position where it cannot be cheaply punished;
- a Turn-3 scoring piece must remain alive.

Then estimate the minimum Orders required to reach that state.

For example, with ten usable Orders:

- 2 Orders to reach the objective;
- 1 Order for the objective attempt;
- 1 Order reserved for a failed roll;
- 1 Order to move into a defensible position.

Those five Orders are already committed. The real discretionary attack budget is approximately five Orders, not ten.

A useful conceptual formula is:

> **Attack budget = usable Orders − scoring reserve − exit reserve − failure reserve − mandatory setup.**

Do not treat the whole Order pool as available for fighting merely because those Orders are currently visible in the pool.

## 2. Establish a scoring floor before attacking

Determine the minimum number of Orders that cannot be spent on optional combat without threatening the mission plan.

Example:

- start with 9 usable Orders;
- scoring plan requires 3 Orders;
- likely retry requires 1;
- final positioning requires 1.

The effective attack/development budget is only 4 Orders.

Think of the remaining five Orders as a **protected scoring floor**.

You may deliberately spend into that reserve if the board changes enough to justify it, but that should be a conscious change of plan rather than accidental overspending.

This reverses a common bad sequence:

> attack until only a few Orders remain → discover there are not enough Orders to score.

The stronger sequence is:

> reserve enough Orders to score → attack with the remainder.

## 3. Budget the whole attack run before starting it

Do not evaluate only the Orders needed for the first gunfight. Estimate the entire operation:

> **setup → approach → breach → exploitation → mission work → exit.**

Example:

- 1 Order for Smoke/Pitcher/other setup;
- 2 Orders to reach the firing position;
- 2 expected Orders to beat the primary defender;
- 2 Orders to reach the valuable follow-up target;
- 1 Order to retreat.

That is approximately an eight-Order operation.

If only five Orders are actually available after protecting the scoring plan, do not begin the eight-Order version of the attack.

Instead:

- attack only the first target;
- use a closer attacker;
- choose a cheaper route;
- develop position for the next turn;
- abandon the attack entirely if the mission is more important.

> **Do not start a plan whose successful completion already requires more Orders than you can afford.**

## 4. Budget for failure, not merely the success line

Turn plans often underestimate Order cost because they assume every roll succeeds immediately.

The optimistic plan might be:

> move → shoot → move → push button.

The realistic plan may be:

> move → shoot → target survives → shoot again → move → fail objective roll → try again.

Reserve likely retry Orders for critical tasks such as:

- objective rolls;
- Discover;
- difficult gunfights;
- hacking attacks;
- Doctor/Engineer attempts;
- speculative attacks;
- movement plans that can be disrupted by Dodge or unexpected ARO geometry.

A useful distinction is:

### Mandatory action

If failure would cause the turn's main plan to collapse, budget:

> **expected cost + at least one plausible retry.**

### Opportunistic action

If the payoff is optional, it can be reasonable to budget:

> **one attempt, then stop.**

This prevents optional fights from consuming the contingency Orders required for mandatory scoring.

## 5. Reserve the exit Order before beginning the attack

A deep attacker with one Order remaining often does not have “one more attack.” It has one Order needed to avoid being cheaply destroyed next turn.

Possible exit uses include:

- retreat into Total Cover;
- leave hostile Repeater coverage;
- move into a stronger defensive range band;
- return toward friendly support;
- reach a safe Suppressive Fire position;
- re-Camouflage where legal;
- use Cybermask;
- place a Mine or other deployable while withdrawing;
- move far enough away that retaliation becomes Order-expensive.

A practical habit is:

> **Pretend the last Order does not exist when planning the attack.**

That final Order belongs to exit/repositioning unless the value of spending it elsewhere clearly exceeds the risk.

The [Target priority and attack sequencing](target-priority-and-attack-sequencing.md) framework therefore treats approach, fighting, mission work, and exit as parts of the same attack budget rather than separate concerns.

## 6. Budget the turn by functions rather than by favourite model

Avoid thinking:

> “I am putting eight Orders into the TAG.”

Instead divide the turn into jobs.

Example with eleven effective Orders:

| Function | Budget |
| --- | ---: |
| Setup / development | 1 |
| Primary attack | 4 |
| Mission scoring | 3 |
| Failure reserve | 1 |
| Exit / defence | 2 |

Now the primary attack has a four-Order budget.

If it succeeds in two Orders, the unused Orders can be reassigned.

If it has not succeeded after four Orders, the budget has expired and the plan should be reassessed.

This is much safer than continuing to feed Orders into the same attacker simply because it has already received several.

## 7. Define branch points before rolling dice

Infinity plans should resemble decision trees rather than scripted sequences.

For example:

- **If the sniper dies within two Orders:** exploit the lane.
- **If the sniper survives after three Orders:** abandon the route.
- **If the attacker dies:** switch to the secondary plan.
- **If the defender Guts out of the relevant lane:** bypass it rather than pursue it.
- **If a Hidden Deployment or unexpected marker appears:** recalculate the scoring reserve before continuing.

This is one of the best protections against sunk-cost escalation.

A failed first step does not mean the original plan deserves more Orders. It may mean the plan has become bad.

## 8. Spend restricted Orders before flexible Regular Orders when they perform the same useful job

Regular Orders have high option value because they can usually be redirected to many different troops. Tactical Awareness, Lieutenant, Irregular, Impetuous, and similar resources are more restricted.

When a restricted Order can perform a useful action that was already part of the plan, spending it first can preserve flexible Regular Orders for later uncertainty.

Examples:

- use Tactical Awareness for movement the model already needed;
- use NCO to convert Lieutenant Orders into a planned attack or movement sequence;
- use an Irregular trooper's own Order for useful Smoke, movement, or trading before spending flexible pool Orders on it;
- use an active Lieutenant's own Order for productive positioning when exposing the Lieutenant is acceptable;
- use Impetuous movement when it meaningfully advances the plan without creating unacceptable exposure.

General principle:

> **Spend specialized currency on appropriate jobs before spending universal currency.**

However, do not perform a bad action merely to avoid “wasting” a restricted Order. An unused restricted Order can be preferable to exposing an important troop for negligible value.

## 9. Tactical Awareness and similar personal Orders are excellent development subsidies

A Tactical Awareness Order does not need to become an extra attack roll.

It can pay for:

- initial movement;
- climbing or repositioning;
- moving out of the Deployment Zone;
- approaching an objective;
- retreating after an attack;
- moving toward a later-turn staging position.

If a model would otherwise require two pooled Orders to travel and four to fight, a personal Order used for travel effectively reduces the pooled cost of the same attack sequence.

This is one reason special Orders should be included explicitly in the turn budget rather than treated as miscellaneous bonuses.

## 10. Preserve flexible Orders while uncertainty remains high

At the beginning of the Active Turn, many outcomes are still unknown:

- an attack may fail;
- an objective roll may need a retry;
- a marker may reveal something unexpected;
- a new route may open;
- an attacker may die;
- a Specialist may require an extra Move;
- a defensive model may Dodge into or out of an important lane.

Flexible Regular Orders are therefore most valuable early, while the decision tree is still broad.

A useful sequencing principle is:

> **Consume predictable or restricted resources early; preserve flexible resources for uncertainty.**

As the turn progresses and fewer branches remain, the option value of flexibility decreases and remaining Orders can be committed more aggressively.

## 11. Turn 1, Turn 2, and Turn 3 should usually have different budgets

The value of an Order changes according to how many future turns remain.

### Turn 1 — development and future efficiency

Turn-1 Orders can create value for multiple later turns.

Good investments include:

- developing attackers out of the DZ;
- positioning Specialists;
- building Repeater coverage;
- placing Mines and other deployables;
- moving Fireteams into useful staging positions;
- removing key enemy Orders or infrastructure;
- securing midfield routes;
- forcing the opponent to spend Orders recovering position.

Do not demand an immediate kill from every Turn-1 Order.

> **An Order spent making Turn 2 cheaper can be better than another mediocre Turn-1 gunfight.**

### Turn 2 — convert position into advantage

Turn 2 is often the most flexible turn.

Typical priorities include:

- exploit development from Turn 1;
- remove meaningful enemy capabilities;
- score enough to establish or maintain the required VP position;
- preserve or position the Turn-3 closer;
- determine which remaining enemy pieces can actually affect final scoring.

This is often the turn where attack and scoring budgets are most balanced.

### Turn 3 — cash out future value

There is no Turn 4.

Turn-3 budgeting therefore heavily prioritizes:

> **score → deny enemy scoring → contest → fight only as required.**

Future positioning, long-term survival, and preserving expensive pieces lose value unless they affect final scoring or denial.

A wounded premium model, Irregular troop, or valuable Specialist can be deliberately sacrificed if the sacrifice secures the game.

## 12. Plan Turn 3 backward from the final board state

The final Active Turn especially should be reverse-engineered from the desired end position.

Ask:

1. Which zones or objectives must be controlled?
2. Which models must end in those locations?
3. How many movement Orders are required?
4. How many objective attempts are required?
5. Which enemy pieces actually prevent this?
6. What is the minimum amount of combat necessary to remove or bypass them?

You may discover that an apparent attack turn actually has only one or two Orders available for combat once the final scoring sequence is protected.

That should change target selection immediately.

## 13. Define the attack's stop condition before starting it

Do not let “keep killing until the pool is empty” become the default Active-Turn plan.

Define what constitutes a successful attack before spending the first Order.

Examples:

- remove the Missile Launcher covering the scoring route;
- kill the Engineer supporting the TAG;
- remove two Regular Orders from the opponent's main Combat Group;
- break the Fireteam bonus;
- disable the TAG rather than destroy it;
- remove the only Specialist able to contest the objective;
- open the lane and stop.

Once that condition is satisfied, the original attack has succeeded.

Every additional Order is now a **new investment decision**, not part of an obligation to continue the same run.

## 14. Stop when marginal attack value falls below the alternatives

The first attack Orders in a run may have excellent value while later ones are poor.

Do not ask only:

> “Can I kill this model?”

Ask:

> **“Is killing this model the best use of my next Order?”**

Compare the next attack against alternatives such as:

- pushing an objective;
- advancing another Specialist;
- laying a Mine;
- repositioning an ARO;
- hiding the current attacker;
- repairing a useful piece;
- developing the opposite flank;
- preparing the next turn;
- denying an enemy scoring route.

The attack should usually stop when the expected value of the next Order has become worse than those alternatives.

This is the broader budgeting version of the marginal-value stop rule in [Target priority and attack sequencing](target-priority-and-attack-sequencing.md).

## 15. Use an Order-loss stop rule

A simple practical trigger is:

> **If one more failed attack would make the mandatory mission plan impossible, the attack is already at its spending limit.**

Example:

- 7 Orders remain;
- 4 are required for reliable scoring;
- therefore only 3 are safely available for the attack.

After spending two attack Orders, one more attempt may be acceptable.

A fourth attack Order means spending into the protected mission reserve. That should happen only if the expected payoff is large enough to justify risking the actual scoring plan.

This rule is especially useful when an attack has become emotionally tempting because the enemy is “almost dead.”

## 16. Separate breach cost from exploitation budget

The first kill in an attack sequence often exists only to open space.

For example:

1. kill the ARO;
2. advance through the lane;
3. reach exposed support troops;
4. attack Order generators;
5. reach an objective.

Only the first engagement may be the actual **breach**. Everything afterward is exploitation.

Treat them as separate budgets:

> **Breach budget** — how much am I willing to spend opening the route?

> **Exploitation budget** — how much am I willing to spend after the route is open?

This creates another useful stop point. Once the lane is open, it may be better to exploit it for scoring rather than for additional kills.

## 17. Budget separately by Combat Group

Total army Orders can be misleading because Orders in different Combat Groups are not freely interchangeable.

Before planning the turn, identify which group contains each important actor.

Example:

### Group 1

- primary attacker;
- main Specialist.

### Group 2

- Smoke warband;
- Engineer;
- secondary Specialist.

An army may appear to have fourteen Orders, but if the main plan requires nine Group-1 activations and Group 1 has only seven usable Orders, the plan does not currently have enough resources.

Possible solutions include:

- transfer a troop between Combat Groups;
- convert an Irregular Order where appropriate;
- use Strategos or another command effect;
- redesign the attack so Group 2 performs more of the setup;
- choose a different attacker.

Combat Group architecture therefore affects turn planning before the first Order is spent.

## 18. Keep a floating contingency Order when possible

Beyond the planned exit Order, preserving one flexible Order until late in the turn can absorb unexpected costs.

Typical uses include:

- an additional objective attempt;
- Discover;
- one more Move than expected;
- a defender surviving;
- an unexpected Dodge;
- a Hidden Deployment reveal;
- an attacker needing a slightly longer retreat.

Think of this as an **uncertainty reserve**.

Once the mandatory and uncertain parts of the turn have resolved, the contingency Order can be released into attack, scoring, or defensive setup.

## 19. Development Orders are real value

Do not evaluate an Order only by whether dice were rolled.

An Order that:

- advances an attacker toward next turn's target;
- moves a Specialist toward a future console;
- establishes a Repeater;
- improves range-band positioning;
- places a Mine;
- moves a Fireteam into a central staging position;
- spreads vulnerable troops against templates;
- moves an exposed unit into Total Cover;

can save several Orders later or force the opponent to spend additional Orders.

This is particularly important on Turn 1, when positional investment still has time to compound.

## 20. Recalculate after every major board-state change

The original budget is a plan, not a contract.

Reassess after:

- a major target dies;
- an attack unexpectedly fails several times;
- your attacker dies;
- an objective is completed early;
- an enemy marker reveals;
- a lane opens;
- a Specialist becomes unavailable;
- the opponent's scoring plan is substantially damaged.

Ask again:

1. What still must be accomplished this turn?
2. How many Orders does that now require?
3. Which reserved Orders are still necessary?
4. How many Orders are genuinely discretionary now?
5. Is the current attacker still the best place to spend them?

A strong player continually updates the budget as uncertainty disappears.

## Practical turn-budget checklist

At the start of each Active Turn:

1. **What must I score this turn?** Reserve those Orders first.
2. **What must the final board state look like?** Reserve movement and exit Orders.
3. **What can plausibly fail?** Reserve retries for mandatory actions.
4. **Which restricted Orders can perform useful planned jobs?** Allocate Tactical Awareness, Lieutenant/NCO, Irregular, Impetuous, and similar resources.
5. **Which Combat Group must perform each task?** Make sure the Orders are actually accessible to the relevant troops.
6. **What remains?** That is the real attack/development budget.
7. **What is the attack's stop condition?** Define it before rolling dice.
8. **What is the fallback branch?** Decide what happens if the first plan fails.
9. **After every major success or failure:** recalculate the budget.
10. **Before spending the last few Orders:** verify scoring and exit requirements again.

The core discipline is simple:

> **Score first on paper. Protect the Orders needed to make that happen. Attack with what is actually left.**
