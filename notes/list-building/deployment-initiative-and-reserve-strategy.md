# List-Building Strategy — Deployment, Initiative, and Reserve

## Current N5.3 airborne / Enemy-DZ attacker profile tiers (2026-10-23)

The [N5.3 cross-faction Enemy-DZ attacker guide](airborne-and-enemy-dz-attackers-apex-value.md) ranks **Parachutist(DZ), standard Parachutist, Combat Jump, deep Infiltration, Impersonation, Hidden/fast ground** by **safe insertion and kill-chain costs**. **Parachutist(DZ)** has no arrival roll but is vulnerable to edge guard AROs and cannot claim Partial Cover on entry; **failed Combat Jump returns to own DZ edge as a Model**. Specific threats to screen: **Rasyat/Van Zant/Mirage, Fiday/Speculo, Banshee/Hellcat/Meteor/Caskuda**, not an imaginary universal enemy-DZ Infiltration permission.


Deployment, Initiative, and Reserve should be treated as one combined **Turn Zero** problem.

The goal is not simply to decide whether going first or second is stronger. The real question is:

> **Which combination of turn order, deployment order, table side, and reserve information gives this list the best path to scoring the mission while denying the opponent an efficient first plan?**

This is one of the highest-impact strategic decisions in Infinity because deployment determines the geometry from which every later Order is spent.

For a concrete pre-deployment scan of route width, silhouette heights, objective access, rooftops, and escape positions, see [Terrain, silhouettes and table geometry](terrain-silhouettes-and-table-geometry.md). **New source-backed walkthrough:** [Loss of Lieutenant E172/E176 deployment clinic](#loss-of-lieutenant-deployment-clinic--parts-1-and-2-transcripts-reviewed-27-oct-2026).

## Rules baseline

Current project rules baseline: **N5.3 + N5 FAQ v0.1**.

Relevant rules:

- The winner of the Initiative Roll chooses to keep **Initiative** or **Deployment**.
- The player who keeps Initiative chooses who has the first and second Player Turns for the game.
- The player who keeps Deployment chooses **who deploys first** and **which Deployment Zone** they use.
- Normally each player holds back **one Trooper** as a reserve.
- **Strategos L1/L2** allows its Lieutenant's player to hold back **two Troopers** instead of one.
- The player with the **first Player Turn** may spend one Command Token during Deployment to hold back **one additional Trooper**.
- Combat Jump and similar rules can leave troops off-table without consuming the normal reserve simply to conceal them.

This means Initiative, table side, deployment order, and reserves are mechanically intertwined rather than independent choices.

---

## 1. Think in four Turn-Zero states

The four broad combinations are:

| Turn / deployment state | General value | Main effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Go first + deploy second** | Excellent offensive state | Maximum information for an alpha strike |
| **Go second + deploy second** | Excellent defensive/scoring state | Counterdeployment plus final activation |
| **Go first + deploy first** | Good but less informed | Tempo advantage, but opponent counterdeploys |
| **Go second + deploy first** | Usually the dangerous state | Opponent gets first strike and counterdeployment |

These are not fixed rankings. Mission, terrain, matchup, and army composition can reverse them.

The important warning is:

> **Going second is not inherently bad. Going second while also deploying first is the combination that most often gives away both tempo and information.**

---

## 2. Decision hierarchy after winning the Initiative Roll

Use this order of questions rather than defaulting to "always go first" or "always choose second."

### 1. Mission

Ask first:

- Is scoring checked every Round?
- Is the final board state disproportionately important?
- Are there zones, consoles, objective carriers, beacons, or other objectives where the last activation is powerful?
- Can an opponent undo early scoring during their own turn?

If the scenario heavily rewards final board state, **second turn rises sharply in value**.

### 2. Table side

Look for asymmetric Deployment Zones:

- safer roofs;
- stronger long-range positions;
- protected staging areas;
- better DZ exits;
- safer places for TAGs/Vehicles;
- fewer angles into the backline;
- stronger objective access;
- better Pitcher/Repeater lanes;
- safer table edges against Parachutists;
- terrain that favors or obstructs Super-Jump/Climbing Plus.

If one side is materially better, **keep Deployment** becomes very attractive.

### 3. Matchup

Ask:

- Can the opponent realistically cripple me on Turn 1?
- Can I realistically cripple them on Turn 1?
- Do they have Impersonators, DZ Parachutists, Combat Jump, fast bikes, Vehicles, Dogs, Super-Jump, Climbing Plus, powerful Pitchers, or other rapid penetration tools?
- Are they Camo/Hidden-Deployment heavy enough that an alpha strike will spend Orders solving information instead of removing Orders?

### 4. Your list

Decide whether your list is better at:

- attacking immediately;
- absorbing an alpha strike;
- counterdeploying;
- winning a late scoring turn;
- leveraging multiple reserves;
- exploiting a specific table side.

### 5. Comfort

If all strategic factors are close, use the turn order you execute more reliably.

A useful shorthand is:

> **Mission > matchup > table > list preference > comfort.**

Mission and table can swap priority when one DZ is exceptionally skewed.

---

## 3. The Deployment Fork

Keeping Deployment does **not** mean choosing to go second.

A powerful pattern after winning the Lieutenant roll is:

> **Keep Deployment → choose the better side → make the opponent deploy first.**

The opponent then owns the Initiative choice.

If they choose first turn:

- they deploy first;
- you counterdeploy;
- they act first;
- you receive the final turn.

If they choose second turn:

- they still deploy first;
- you counterdeploy;
- you receive first turn.

This creates a useful fork whenever your list is comfortable with either turn order:

> **"You may choose the turn order, but I will keep the table side and information advantage."**

This is especially strong for adaptable lists with both competent defense and a meaningful active-turn threat.

---

## 4. When to keep Initiative and take first turn

First turn rises in value when your army has an attack the opponent must not be allowed to prepare for.

Typical indicators:

- Impersonation;
- Combat Jump or strong Airborne Deployment;
- reliable deep deployment;
- fast motorcycles;
- Vehicles;
- Super-Jump or Climbing Plus assault pieces;
- very fast warbands/Dogs;
- TAGs or premium mobile attackers;
- Tactical Awareness attack pieces;
- NCO exploiting multiple Lieutenant Orders;
- other attackers that convert a small number of Orders into immediate DZ pressure.

The best alpha strikes attack **army structure**, not necessarily the opponent's strongest gun.

Good targets include:

- Lieutenant;
- exposed Order generators;
- critical Fireteam member;
- Engineer/Doctor;
- Hacker/EVO;
- missile or network support;
- mission-critical Specialist.

A 20–30 point attacker that removes several Regular Orders and one support piece can create more strategic value than fighting a 70-point gunfighter head-on.

### First-turn test

Before choosing first, ask:

> **Can I name a plausible opening sequence that produces a meaningful mission or Order-economy advantage?**

If the answer is only "I would like to shoot first," keeping Deployment may be more valuable.

---

## 5. When second turn is valuable

Second turn is strongest when the mission rewards **last meaningful action**.

Examples:

- end-of-round zone control;
- end-game objective control;
- last-turn console/button pushes;
- moving an objective carrier to safety;
- contesting or clearing a scoring area;
- destroying the opponent's scoring piece after they can no longer respond.

The second player receives superior information about the score they actually need to beat.

Instead of:

> "I will occupy these zones and hope the opponent cannot remove me."

The second player can often say:

> "They currently control two zones; I need three, and they have no Active Turn left to undo it."

Second turn therefore becomes much stronger when your army can **survive the first attack without losing its scoring capacity**.

---

## 6. Armies that can actively prefer second turn

Second turn becomes more attractive when the list has several independent defensive layers rather than one premium ARO piece.

Useful traits include:

- Camo density;
- Hidden Deployment;
- Minelayers;
- cheap Direct Template Weapons;
- cheap disposable AROs;
- warbands;
- strong midfield presence;
- durable HI/TAGs that are difficult to remove efficiently;
- Doctor/Engineer recovery;
- Chain of Command;
- Repeaters plus credible Hackers/KHDs;
- multiple pieces that can trade while preserving the scoring core.

Good defense is not:

> **"I have one terrifying sniper."**

Good defense is:

> **"There is no single efficient attack route that solves my deployment."**

---

## 7. Deployment Step Zero: information gathering

Before placing models, inventory all Open Information and visible clues.

Useful questions and observations include:

- the opponent Lieutenant's WIP during the Initiative Roll;
- number of visible Troopers and Markers;
- number of declared reserves;
- which Combat Group each visible piece belongs to;
- whether missing bodies make Airborne Deployment or Hidden Deployment plausible;
- what attack vectors the visible army already presents;
- what types of pieces are likely still hidden in reserve.

Do not treat inference as certainty. The purpose is to generate **threat hypotheses** that affect deployment.

A good deployment process repeatedly asks:

> **What do I know? What can I reasonably infer? What threat has not yet been revealed?**

---

## 8. Default deployment doctrine: deploy defensively, attack with Orders

A useful general principle from current deployment analysis is:

> **Deploy defensively; spend Orders to become aggressive.**

An attacker in Total Cover can move out and attack when needed.

A model deployed exposed can be pinned, killed, isolated, or forced to spend Orders escaping before it contributes.

This does **not** mean null-deploying every model. It means every exposed model should have a reason to be exposed.

### If you do not want it shot, hide it

For non-ARO pieces:

- use Total Cover;
- deploy Prone when appropriate;
- avoid accidental elevated LoF;
- check long diagonal firelanes;
- remember that Super-Jump and Climbing Plus change what "safe" geometry means.

### Do not make template bait

Avoid clusters where one template can hit multiple valuable pieces.

Especially separate:

- Hacker + Doctor + Engineer;
- Lieutenant + decoy/support;
- multiple Fireteam members;
- several Order generators;
- expensive REM/TAG support packages.

### Deploy AROs with an exit

An ARO piece should ideally be able to:

- fail/pass Guts to break LoF;
- fall Unconscious behind cover where a Doctor/Engineer can recover it;
- force several Orders before dying;
- deny a critical lane rather than merely offer itself as a target.

A Missile Launcher or sniper profile is allowed to start hidden or Prone. Paying for an ARO weapon does not obligate you to expose it on Turn 1.

---

## 9. Protect Orders, not just models

The first deployment audit should be:

> **How does the opponent remove four or more Orders from me?**

Do not concentrate the army into one defensive shell that a single successful breach can roll through.

Prefer layers:

1. midfield Camo/mines/Repeaters;
2. disposable ARO/interference pieces;
3. templates and ZoC guards;
4. main force;
5. Lieutenant/support/scoring core.

The purpose is to make the opponent solve **different defensive problems with different tools**.

---

## 10. Deployment against common DZ-strike vectors

### Parachutist / Parachutist (Deployment Zone)

Do not leave the back/side edge containing a chain of high-value support pieces.

The goal is not necessarily to prevent entry. Make the **next Order after entry** expensive by using:

- cheap sentries;
- templates;
- mines;
- ZoC coverage;
- crossfire;
- spacing.

### Combat Jump

Spread important pieces so one landing zone cannot immediately access:

- Lieutenant;
- Hacker;
- Engineer;
- EVO;
- several cheerleaders.

### Impersonation

Think about **base access and short movement paths**, not only LoF.

Protect critical pieces with:

- mines;
- template guards;
- Peripherals;
- physical blocking;
- ZoC;
- corners and intervening bases.

### Super-Jump / Climbing Plus

Audit roofs, balconies, upper floors, and wall routes.

A rooftop that is safe against ordinary infantry can be a direct attack lane for vertical-mobility pieces.

### Bikes / Vehicles / Dogs / other high-mobility attackers

Trace their likely movement route in Orders.

If a model can reach the backline with three Moves and a template attack, a long-range ARO covering the front does not by itself constitute defense.

Build **depth**, not only frontage.

---

## 11. Lieutenant deployment audit

Before finishing deployment, ask:

> **If I were my opponent, how would I kill my Lieutenant?**

Check explicitly for:

- Impersonation;
- Airborne Deployment;
- Super-Jump;
- Climbing Plus;
- Pitcher + hacking;
- Guided attack setup;
- Berserk/close combat;
- templates;
- long-range LoF;
- obvious Camo-Lieutenant clues;
- an attacker that can threaten the Lieutenant and another valuable target in the same run.

If one attack route simultaneously threatens the Lieutenant plus several Orders/support pieces, change the geometry.

---

## 12. Reserve doctrine

Reserve is not automatically:

> **"my strongest model"**

The better definition is:

> **Reserve the Trooper whose correct position depends most strongly on information revealed by the opponent's deployment.**

### Going first: offensive reserve

Strong reserve candidates include:

- major unlinked gunfighter;
- TAG;
- Impersonator;
- fast assault piece;
- information-sensitive infiltrator;
- attacker whose starting lane determines how many Orders it needs to reach a target.

The goal is to see the opponent's actual AROs, support structure, and openings before choosing the attack vector.

### Going second: defensive reserve

Strong reserve candidates include:

- Minelayer/interference piece;
- independent durable ARO;
- piece that can block the opponent's likely route;
- essential vulnerable model that must survive;
- an obvious Lieutenant when the opponent still appears to have an Impersonator or other reserve assassination piece.

In some matchups, **reserving the Lieutenant is more valuable than reserving your best gun**.

### Poor reserve candidates

Usually avoid spending reserve on pieces whose location is already obvious or does not materially respond to opponent information:

- cheerleaders with one safe location;
- Baggage/support REMs;
- support pieces whose nearby Engineer/Fireteam already telegraphs their position;
- linked ARO pieces whose team placement makes their eventual location obvious.

Airborne Deployment pieces already gain concealment by remaining off-table; they do not need the reserve slot merely to hide their deployment.

---

## 13. Multiple reserves are a real strategic asset

N5 gives additional reserve leverage to first-turn armies:

- normal reserve: **1 Trooper**;
- Strategos: **2 Troopers instead of 1**;
- first-turn player: may spend **1 Command Token for +1 reserve Trooper**.

Therefore a first-turn Strategos force can potentially hold back **three Troopers**.

That changes deployment pressure significantly. The opponent may have to prepare for several unrevealed attack vectors while committing most of their own force.

Use extra reserves on **different questions**, not three pieces whose optimal position is obvious.

Example:

- one primary gunfighter;
- one infiltrating/Impersonation attacker;
- one flexible defensive or mission piece.

This maximizes the value of late information.

---

## 14. Deploying first can occasionally have proactive value

Deploying second is normally informationally stronger, but deploying first is not purely a disadvantage.

Forward-deploying Camo, Infiltrators, Minelayers, perimeter weapons, Repeaters, and other midfield pieces can sometimes **claim useful geometry before the opponent places their own midfield package**.

This is most relevant in dense midfield matchups where placement restrictions, trigger areas, ZoC, or physical space can deny ideal enemy deployment locations.

Treat this as a niche matchup advantage, not a general reason to volunteer to deploy first.

---

## 15. Order of placement inside your own deployment

A useful heuristic is:

### First — fixed pieces

- obvious cheerleaders;
- Baggage;
- support REMs;
- pieces with only one sensible location.

### Second — defensive framework

- mines/deployables;
- cheap AROs;
- template guards;
- DZ-edge guards;
- network pieces whose position is structurally required.

### Third — support and scoring structure

- Doctor;
- Engineer;
- Hacker;
- protected Specialists;
- Lieutenant shell.

### Last — information-sensitive pieces

- primary attacker;
- premium independent ARO;
- infiltrating attacker;
- Impersonator;
- other reserve pieces.

Do not reveal the Turn-1 plan accidentally through placement order. An Engineer beside one TAG lane, a smoke source beside one MSV attacker, and every Repeater pointed toward the same flank can announce the intended attack before reserve deployment.

---

## 16. Camo-heavy opponents change the value of first turn

Against dense Camo/Hidden Deployment, first turn may spend substantial Orders on:

- Discover;
- Sensor;
- clearing mines;
- identifying real threats;
- avoiding unknown Hidden AROs.

The nominal alpha strike can therefore become an **information-solving turn**.

This raises the value of Deployment and sometimes second turn against armies such as Camo-heavy Ariadna or other marker-dense forces, provided your defense can absorb their first attack.

---

## 17. Null deployment is a tool, not a doctrine

Hiding almost everything can be correct when the opponent's active-turn pieces would otherwise erase exposed AROs efficiently.

But full null deployment can concede:

- midfield movement;
- Pitcher/Repeater projection;
- objective access;
- safe staging for warbands/fast attackers;
- deployment of enemy attack infrastructure without opposition.

A better default is a **layered defense** with enough visible/interference presence to tax movement while protecting the pieces that cannot afford to trade.

---

## 18. Practical Initiative matrix

| Situation | Strong default |
| --- | --- |
| Mission strongly rewards end-of-round/end-game control | Deployment; aim for a strong second-turn state |
| One DZ is clearly superior | Keep Deployment |
| You have a brutal immediate DZ attack | Keep Initiative; strongly consider first |
| Opponent has a brutal DZ attack | Keep Deployment; make them reveal placement first when possible |
| Opponent is extremely Camo/Hidden-heavy | Deployment rises in value |
| Your defense is deep and resilient | Deployment/second becomes attractive |
| Your defense is fragile | Initiative/first becomes more attractive |
| Open table + strong long-range architecture | Table side/deployment can dominate the decision |
| Dense table + excellent short-range assault | First-turn tempo rises |
| Strong Impersonator attack | First turn rises sharply |
| Heavy midfield deployment battle | Deployment order becomes unusually important |
| You genuinely like either turn order | Keep Deployment is often the strongest information play |

---

## 19. 30-second deployment checklist

Before declaring deployment complete:

1. Can anything attack into my DZ from its starting position?
2. Can Airborne Deployment enter behind my important pieces?
3. Can Super-Jump/Climbing Plus bypass my screen?
4. Can one template hit two or more valuable Troopers?
5. Can an Impersonator reach my Lieutenant cheaply?
6. Do I have at least one mine/template/ZoC/interference layer where needed?
7. Can my main attacker leave the DZ efficiently?
8. Can my Doctor reach likely casualties?
9. Can my Engineer reach likely TAG/REM/HI casualties?
10. Can enemy Repeaters cheaply project hacking onto my hackable pieces?
11. Are Fireteam members exposed without a specific defensive purpose?
12. What exactly is my reserve accomplishing?

If question 12 has no clear answer, the reserve choice is probably wrong.

---

## 20. Short decision rule to memorize

> **Bad or strongly asymmetric side? → Keep Deployment.**  
> **Need an immediate alpha strike? → Keep Initiative and consider first.**  
> **Mission rewards last activation and defense is strong? → Prefer a second-turn plan, ideally without surrendering deployment information.**  
> **Normally prefer making the opponent deploy first.**  
> **Reserve the piece whose placement gains the most from information, not automatically the most expensive piece.**  
> **Defend Orders, depth, and attack routes—not only firing lanes.**

And the central warning:

> **Going second can be excellent. Deploying second is excellent. Going second while deploying first is the state that requires the strongest justification.**

### First versus second: avoid the wrong Turn-1 job (E172 ~60–101m)

- **If going first**, establish a **gun → route → Specialist → safe endpoint** sequence. An aggressive opener that kills several enemies but ends stranded at midfield may leave the counterattack easier than before. Place the main gun centrally **if terrain permits** so it can pivot to the flank containing targets and scoring pieces; otherwise allocate a deliberate alternate lane.
- **First-turn attacker begins Prone or hidden** unless it must already threaten a reactive line, cannot go Prone, or needs to preserve a marker-state deception. Standing main gun in a visible spot allows counterdeployment of multiple enemy AROs, turning its first activation into an impossible fight. Finish by standing/positioning the appropriate defenders *after* the attackers do their work.
- **If going second**, assess a cheap visible ARO by **enemy Orders burned**. E172 gives the practical heuristic that a Warcor forcing about two Orders has already earned its slot; a durable recoverable model that burns three–five Orders and then goes Prone may do better than an expensive exposed 'must kill' lane. Those counts are **illustrations, not guaranteed estimates**.
- **Let defence control the first mission scoring route** rather than every open lane. A narrow ARO on the only console approach, a mine or Repeater on the exact interaction route, or a camo marker requiring Discover can cost multiple enemy Orders without fighting a TAG in a straight face-to-face shootout.
- **Reserve the LT / force hard target choices against Impersonation.** A premium attacking model and obvious LT in the same accessible route invite a double-value assassination. Sometimes the 'right' reserve is the cheap Lt to place it *after* the enemy Impersonator; keep a counterattack plan even if the first defensive ARO goes down.
- **Be realistic about deployment side**: one DZ may be better for *attacking* but not *defending* or vice versa. Part 1's practical terrain exercise around **[3:04:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=il2w0PSGr0U&t=11092s)** weighs sparse exterior cover versus internal compound routes, *roof height relative to fences*, which quadrants/objectives can truly be seen or reached, a protected entry under a stair/bridge and whether an S7 TAG can actually stage. **Do not infer actual terrain geometry from the audio alone**: replay with the shown table to apply its procedure to a real board.

### Part 2: radial (corner) deployments change the price of mobility (E176 ~18–39m)

- **Narrower, deeper DZ**: reduced horizontal room but much more rearward depth. Easier to layer serial defenses and protect a small Lieutenant, easier to **trap yourself** with blocked exits; check staging locations for larger silhouettes. Two nearest DZ points may be close while objectives/flanks/rear DZ may be far apart.
- **Long diagonal firelanes change weapon range bands**. A Sniper can sometimes attack outside the practical bands of weapons that usually dominate rectangular DZ-to-DZ shooting. Examine *actual* LoF and blocking buildings, not weapon labels.
- **Infiltration, FD and Parachutist gain reach on distant objective edges**. A forward specialist can save many Orders travelling from a corner DZ; deleting that isolated specialist may force its backup to traverse most of the board. Parachutist has more plausible **neutral edge arrival** around a corner setup. HVT distance and safe Classified routes can differ radically from rectangular maps.
- **Increased value for layered height and midfield exits**: if terrain gives only one safe DZ depth layer, too many models crowd there and template risk rises. Reserve safe ground access for scoring teams; do not overbuy an infantry-only plan that cannot reach the farthest marker.

### Layered AROs: serial tax first, simultaneous forks when they need different answers (E176 ~39–56m)

- **Serial order tax:** position defenders so the enemy has to **deal with threat A before threat B**; forcing \`Move+Move\` into \`Move+Dodge\`, then detouring a longer route, repeatedly taxes progress. This is different from placing three guns where **one smoke/White Noise/long-shot position solves all three**.
- **Don't stack mines on the same sacrificial approach**. Overlapping mandatory triggers can allow a cheap helper bot or other expendable model to consume several mines in one movement. Prefer mines guarding **different corners/doors, separate approach timing, or genuinely different targets**. In the podcast, *you do not choose to hold a mine when its valid trigger occurs*—verify exact triggers in current Mine rules.
- **Hidden AROs are stronger when they can wait** until an attacker crosses the decisive range/point of no return. Showing the first marker or short-range gun does not obligate the hidden sniper to reveal immediately; the unanswered question can extract extra movement/discovery Orders.
- **Build different-response forks when practical:** a normal mine + gun tends to force Dodge versus shooting; a **hacking attack + gun** pressures Reset versus FtF shooting; a **Cybermine plus physical attack** may pose another incompatible response. Actual triggering, hacking eligibility, Hacking Area and declaration/ARO rules govern each case; do not imply guaranteed multiple unavoidable hits.

### Deployment against current rapid threats (E176 ~57–76m, ~105–129m)

| Threat | Build/placement response | Reason and pitfall |
|---|---|---|
| **Early hacker/Pitcher intrusion** | Pick the job of your Hacker (defensive net, offensive network, backup specialist, Camo/Hidden tool) **before** deciding its location. Put frail support Hackers behind **another relevant hacking target layer** rather than within straightforward first Pitcher ZoC. | Your own Repeaters are also openings hostile KHD/HD may exploit. Even one cheap Kappa/Fusilier HD can improve a DZ defence *if* it survives and has actual Repeater coverage; strong enemy cyber factions can invert that advantage. |
| **Flyer / super-mobile high-angle DZ dive** | Cover **landing/entry geometry, rooftops, ladder exits and approach squares**, not merely DZ-to-DZ long lines. Use expendable mines/peripherals/close defenders, eligible hacking and a hidden or durable ARO that can contest the attacker **before** it reaches backline range. | A purely prone **null deployment** can let a flyer bypass distant sightlines, climb above shelter, score freely or arrive at point-blank range. Opponent may clear the defensive gate with a *different* gun first. |
| **Impersonation** | Spread important targets so one run cannot take LT + TAG + multiple support bodies; use terrain, a **reserve** and corner/body barriers to force choices. Treat a first attack as possibly unavoidable and make a second attack/order chain expensive. | A marker outside the enemy DZ can already be threatening; **risky deep roll** is not always worth losing the piece. Cheap bait that extracts several enemy Orders can be worthwhile; don't spend five of your own Orders reflexively Discovering a marker that isn't threatening the mission. |
| **Enemy scoring with no long gun** | Do not universally null-deploy. Contest **console/box approaches** with markers, mines, useful AROs and mission timing even if the opponent's superior active gun may win the eventual fight. | Keeping all fifteen bodies alive is meaningless if opponent captures the objective and locks the middle uncontested. |

### Impetuous and peripherals are *deployment* problems, not only activation rules (E176 ~76–85m and ~125–135m)

- **Backsolve the first Impetuous movement.** Decide the corner or Smoke-throw position the warband **must reach safely**, then deploy far enough **back** to make its *required* move arrive there. Deploying directly on the DZ line may make a free 4-inch advance expose it; starting an inch back can yield **some** safe free movement rather than forfeiting the activation. Under current N5.3 movement options, **Move + Idle** can be preferable to an unsafe Dodge; check current Impetuous requirements/LoF.
- **Specialist and Fireteam modules:** decide primary and backup button pushers pregame; a fire-support team may stay static but needs protected entrances, while a mission Haris needs access to midfield **without splitting enabler from Specialist**. The same bundle technique speeds tournament deployments (E176 ~80–93m).
- **Servant placement telegraphs reserve locations**: a lonely Engineer's Servant next to the only S7 hiding place may reveal the future TAG even when TAG was withheld as reserve. Ensure a real protected servicing route and avoid 'support dies to same Blast' spacing.
- **Disposable Peripherals are first defensive layer** when legal: e.g. Auxbot, Puzzler or expendable PuppetBot. Position the Peripheral so an approaching enemy must waste movement/declaration/Orders on it **before** seeing/killing the Controller. A dead disposable body may remove **no additional pooled Order**, whereas an exposed Controller removes the whole package's value. **Not all Cyberplug/Peripheral states or Orders are identical**; check the [multi-body guide](peripherals-synchronized-and-multi-body-troops.md).

### Table / event preparation (E176 ~90–105m)

- **Objective rooms need a stated LoF convention before deployment**: true-LoF, 'in/out but not through', or opaque/'muddy window' treatments change whether doorway-to-doorway shooting and Disco access are practical. The hosts prefer **true LoF with real interior scatter** but disagree on other house practices: **agree at the table or follow event packet**.
- Clarify **objective silhouette**, doors/window treatment, saturation or visibility zones, vertical LoF and legal full-base support. A strategically great roof that cannot physically hold a base is not free space under the rules.
- **Bad-table resilience is a list-building attribute:** some Forward Deployment/Infiltration, mobile Specialists, Hidden Deployment, Combat Jump, genuine low-LoF S7 shelter and independent support modules give more deployment options than a concentrated force needing five premium safe rooftop positions.

**Speaker disagreements explicitly retained:** (1) near-total **null deployment** still denies purely static gun lists, but Part 2 describes it as high-risk in modern N5 owing to flyers, fast Dogs/assault and current missions; (2) a **token cheap Hacker** is a sensible flexible option when it survives, but exposing it to enemy Repeater/KHD infiltration can hurt more than help; (3) **Hard second** and optional Fireteam/reserve interactions are situational, not automatic; (4) the choice of objective-room LoF convention requires organizer/table agreement. Distinguish old edition examples and table-specific anecdotes from current universal recommendations.
