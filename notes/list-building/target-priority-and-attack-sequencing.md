# List-Building Strategy — Target Priority and Attack Sequencing

Target priority in Infinity is not simply **kill the most expensive or dangerous model first**. The better question is:

> **Which removal most improves my position, damages the opponent's next turn, or opens the mission plan for the fewest Orders and least risk?**

This means a cheap Engineer, Hacker, Pitcher carrier, NCO, FT Master, Repeater, Specialist, or exposed Regular Order can be a better target than a TAG or apex gunfighter.

Target priority and attack sequencing should therefore be treated as an **Order-efficiency problem tied to mission goals**. The ideal attack sequence removes exactly the obstacles that matter, exploits the openings it creates, scores, and ends in a position that is difficult to punish.

A useful shorthand is:

> **Enable → breach → exploit → score → exit.**

## 1. Attack the opponent's plan, not merely the opponent's points

Before choosing a target, identify what the opponent is actually trying to accomplish on the next meaningful turn.

Examples:

- a TAG wants enough Orders and a safe route to make an attack run;
- a Hacker wants Repeaters or Pitchers to project the network;
- a Fireteam wants its gunfighter, vision-control piece, Specialist, and support models to move together;
- an objective list needs one or two surviving Specialists to reach specific consoles;
- an NCO attack piece may depend on Lieutenant Orders to reach full efficiency;
- an airborne package may depend on an EVO or on weak DZ coverage;
- a damaged premium piece may depend on an Engineer to become relevant again.

Instead of asking only **"What is the strongest enemy model?"**, ask:

1. What is the enemy's likely plan?
2. Which pieces enable it?
3. Which link is easiest to break?
4. What becomes possible if that link disappears?

A 10-point support model can therefore be strategically more important than a 70-point attacker.

## 2. Default target-priority hierarchy

There is no fixed hierarchy that overrides the mission and board state, but this is a useful default:

| Priority | Target type | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 — Immediate blocker or threat** | ARO preventing the current plan; attacker about to score or break the army | Must be solved now |
| **2 — Plan enabler** | Hacker, Repeater, Pitcher carrier, Engineer, Smoke carrier, EVO, NCO, FT Master | Removing one piece may collapse several others |
| **3 — Orders and command structure** | exposed Regular Orders, Lieutenant, TacAware/NCO infrastructure | Reduces future activation capacity |
| **4 — Mission-critical Specialist** | Specialist required for remaining objectives | Directly attacks the opponent's VP plan |
| **5 — Recovery/support** | Doctor, Engineer, Peripheral support | Makes subsequent damage stick |
| **6 — Efficient exposed target** | cheap/easy kill that costs little to take | Good attrition when the opportunity cost is low |
| **7 — Premium combat piece** | TAG, HI, apex gunfighter | Important only when it actually affects the plan |
| **8 — Irrelevant survivor** | badly positioned, Order-starved, non-scoring model | Often safe to ignore |

The key distinction is:

> **Points value is not target value.**

## 3. What needs to die now, and what can be ignored?

A model usually **needs to die now** when leaving it alive materially changes the current or next turn.

High-priority cases include:

- it prevents immediate scoring;
- it controls the route the main attacker or Specialist must use;
- it can realistically launch a devastating attack next turn;
- it is the only Specialist capable of completing an important objective;
- it is the only Engineer capable of recovering a damaged TAG/REM/VH;
- it is the Hacker making an aggressive Repeater network dangerous;
- it is the Smoke, MSV, Pitcher, White Noise, or other support piece completing an attack package;
- it is an exposed Lieutenant whose death will produce meaningful command disruption;
- killing it opens several additional targets or movement routes.

Conversely:

> **If killing the model does not change anything important, it probably does not need to die.**

An expensive attacker with no useful route and too few Orders can often be ignored. A cheap Repeater covering the only route available to several HI may be critical.

## 4. Kill the enabler before the threat when it is cheaper

Many strong Infinity pieces operate as part of a package. Do not evaluate the visible threat in isolation.

### TAG + Engineer

Instead of repeatedly attacking a repairable TAG, it can be more efficient to:

1. remove the Engineer or its Peripheral;
2. then attack the TAG once the damage will stick.

### Hacker + Repeater network

If the Hacker is inaccessible but the critical Repeater is exposed, kill the Repeater and collapse the local network.

If the network is extensive but only one important Hacker can exploit it, kill the Hacker instead.

If several complementary Hackers share the same network, removing one mediocre Hacker may accomplish very little; attack the network or the most important Hacker instead.

### MSV gunfighter + Smoke

Sometimes the best target is the MSV gunfighter.

Sometimes killing the Smoke carrier is much easier and removes the combo.

Attack whichever part of the package is easiest to remove **and actually breaks the package**.

### NCO + Lieutenant Orders

An NCO may be more valuable than its points suggest because it converts restricted Lieutenant Orders into productive attack activations.

Removing the NCO may strand those Orders or force them onto a far less useful Lieutenant.

### Fireteam package

Do not automatically attack the strongest gun first. Look for the member whose removal most damages the whole team:

- Specialist;
- TinBot carrier;
- FT Master;
- Doctor/Engineer;
- vision-control piece;
- support weapon;
- member whose loss removes an important Fireteam bonus;
- member whose death makes rebuilding awkward or impossible.

## 5. Attack the Order pool when the payoff compounds

Removing cheap Regular troops can be more damaging than shooting an expensive attack piece because Order losses affect every remaining unit in that Combat Group.

This is particularly attractive when:

- it is Turn 1 or early Turn 2;
- the opponent's main Combat Group is concentrated and Order-hungry;
- several cheap Regular Orders are exposed or clustered;
- the opponent relies on one premium attacker that needs many Orders;
- the Order generators can be removed without spending more Orders than the disruption is worth.

Example:

- Option A: spend three Orders attempting to wound a durable 55-point attacker;
- Option B: spend three Orders killing three exposed Regular cheerleaders.

Option B may reduce the opponent's available Regular Orders for every future turn and prevent the expensive attacker from ever receiving enough Orders to perform its intended job.

However, do not fetishize Order killing. Killing three Orders while ignoring the only enemy Specialist that can win the scenario can still be a losing trade.

## 6. Lieutenant hunting is conditional, not automatic

Killing the Lieutenant can be devastating, but only when the disruption is meaningful relative to the Orders and risk required.

Before committing to an LT hunt, ask:

- Is the Lieutenant reasonably identifiable?
- Is Chain of Command likely or already known?
- How many Orders are required to reach and remove the Lieutenant?
- How much of the enemy turn will actually be affected?
- Is the Lieutenant also a Hacker, attacker, Strategos piece, Specialist, or other high-value role?

An early Lieutenant kill can be very strong because it may affect a full upcoming turn.

A late-game Lieutenant kill may accomplish almost nothing unless:

- the mission rewards it;
- the Lieutenant itself is scoring;
- killing it removes a critical active piece;
- command disruption still matters before the game ends.

> **Target the Lieutenant for the disruption its death causes, not merely because it is the Lieutenant.**

## 7. Clear only the AROs that matter

A common Active-Turn mistake is trying to clean the entire board before executing the actual plan.

Instead, identify the specific ARO pieces that prevent the chosen route.

Example:

- sniper controls the left route;
- missile launcher watches the centre;
- Flash Pulse REM watches a right-side lane that will not be used.

If the plan uses the left route, solve the sniper. The other ARO pieces may be irrelevant for the entire turn.

Also remember that **solving an ARO does not necessarily mean killing it**.

Possible solutions include:

- Smoke;
- Eclipse;
- White Noise;
- Cautious Movement;
- another route;
- Climbing Plus/Super-Jump geometry;
- marker-state movement;
- Cybermask;
- hacking;
- forcing a Dodge or other unhelpful ARO while another piece moves.

> **The cheapest ARO to clear is often the one you never fight.**

## 8. Setup first when setup improves several later Orders

Do not default to:

> see target → shoot target.

Ask whether one setup Order will improve several subsequent activations.

Common sequences include:

- Smoke → MSV gunfighter attacks;
- Pitcher → Hacker attacks through the new Repeater;
- Spotlight → conventional or Guided follow-up;
- White Noise → advance through an MSV lane;
- Sensor → attack revealed markers;
- KHD removes hostile Hacker → HI/TAG advances;
- Engineer clears a disabling state → premium piece resumes the attack.

Setup is strongest when its value is **amortized across several later Orders**.

For example:

- one Smoke Order enabling five safe movement/attack Orders is excellent;
- one Smoke Order making a single already-favorable gunfight slightly better may be inefficient.

Therefore:

> **Do not spend several Orders preparing a fight that was already cheap to win.**

## 9. Attack easy targets first when they unlock harder targets

Starting with the easiest target is useful only when the kill changes geometry or removes an obstacle.

Example attack ladder:

1. kill the exposed Flash Pulse REM;
2. advance into the newly safe lane;
3. remove the mid-range defender covering the next corner;
4. move into a better range band;
5. attack the premium HI from the improved position;
6. exploit the breach to reach support troops or objectives.

The early easy kills are valuable because each one purchases **position**.

Do not spend Orders killing easy models that do not open a route, remove Orders that matter, break support, or contribute to the mission.

## 10. Sequence attacks so each success improves the next engagement

A strong attack run should be a chain of related actions rather than a series of disconnected gunfights.

Example:

1. remove a peripheral ARO;
2. move the attacker forward;
3. kill the Repeater covering the route;
4. advance HI safely through the former hacking zone;
5. kill the Engineer;
6. attack the TAG now that repairs are unavailable;
7. move the accompanying Specialist through the cleared route;
8. score the objective;
9. reposition for the Reactive Turn.

Compare this with spending four or five consecutive Orders simply shooting the TAG from the original position.

> **The best attack Orders improve the efficiency of later Orders.**

## 11. Choose the cheapest appropriate attacker

Do not use the army's best gunfighter for every problem.

Use the cheapest reliable solver that can handle the specific target.

| Problem | Typical efficient solver |
| --- | --- |
| weak exposed cheerleader | inexpensive rifle/Spitfire piece |
| extreme Mimetism | MSV, template, Sensor-assisted solution |
| TAG / armored HI | appropriate AP/EXP/Breaker/E/M/hacking solution |
| enemy Hacker | KHD/Trinity-capable Hacker |
| protected support model | Speculative Fire, infiltrator, AD attacker |
| clustered troops | template weapon |
| long open lane | long-range gunfighter |
| target behind total cover | hacking, Speculative Fire, CC approach, flanking |
| oppressive ARO | Smoke, White Noise, bypass, target-appropriate counter |

Using an apex attacker to kill every cheap target wastes both its exposure and the Orders needed to deliver it.

## 12. Preserve marker state and hidden information until the reveal pays

Attack sequencing includes **when not to reveal a piece**.

Relevant examples include:

- Camouflage;
- Hidden Deployment;
- Combat Jump;
- Parachutist;
- Impersonation;
- reserve-deployed attackers;
- Cybermask.

Before revealing, ask:

> **Is this target worth sacrificing the information, marker state, or positional protection that the model currently provides?**

A Camo Specialist shooting a cheap cheerleader may lose the marker-state safety needed to score later.

An undeclared airborne attacker may constrain enemy deployment, facing, and movement simply because the possibility remains alive.

Do not cash out hidden information for a mediocre target unless the resulting attack materially advances the plan.

## 13. Re-evaluate after every major success

Do not mechanically continue the sequence planned at the start of the turn.

After an important kill or objective, reassess:

- Did the route open more cheaply than expected?
- Did the target survive and make the planned continuation inefficient?
- Is a new high-value target now exposed?
- Has the mission already been secured?
- Has the attacker moved into a position where continuing becomes dangerous?
- Are enough Orders left to score and exit?

A successful attack should change the board. Therefore the optimal next action may also change.

## 14. Stop an attack run when marginal Order efficiency collapses

One of the most important Active-Turn disciplines is knowing when the attack is already successful.

> **An attack run should stop when the expected value of the next Order becomes poor compared with scoring, repositioning, or another attack path.**

Do not continue merely because the attacker is already deep in enemy territory. That is sunk-cost thinking.

Example:

The first four Orders:

1. remove an ARO;
2. advance;
3. kill a Repeater;
4. kill the Engineer.

Excellent result.

The next target now requires:

- two Orders to approach;
- an unfavorable Face-to-Face Roll;
- likely another Order to finish;
- two Orders to retreat afterward.

The correct decision may be to stop immediately.

## 15. Marginal-value check during long attack runs

Every two or three Orders, especially after a major kill, ask:

1. What does the next Order accomplish?
2. What are the odds it succeeds?
3. What happens if it fails?
4. How many additional Orders become necessary afterward?
5. Do I still have enough Orders to accomplish the mission?
6. Can the attacker still end the turn somewhere useful?
7. Is another model now a better Order sink?

A useful conceptual model is:

> **Marginal attack value ≈ enemy capability removed × probability of success ÷ Orders required and risk incurred.**

This is not intended as exact arithmetic. It is a reminder to compare the next attack with its opportunity cost.

## 16. Budget exit Orders before starting the run

Do not treat retreating or repositioning as something to think about only after the shooting is finished.

Before beginning, roughly budget:

> **approach + fighting + mission work + exit**

Example with ten usable Orders:

- 2 Orders to reach the attack lane;
- 3 Orders expected for important engagements;
- 2 Orders for mission work;
- 2 Orders to reposition safely.

That leaves only **one genuinely discretionary attack Order**.

Without this budgeting, a player can easily spend seven Orders attacking, discover that only three remain, and then choose between failing the mission and abandoning the attacker.

## 17. End-turn repositioning is part of the attack sequence

Once the attack has achieved its strategic purpose, remaining Orders can often create more value by shaping the opponent's next turn than by chasing another kill.

Useful end-turn actions include:

- retreat into Total Cover;
- enter Suppressive Fire;
- return to Camouflage where legal;
- Cybermask;
- reposition Fireteam members;
- move a Specialist toward the next objective;
- hide vulnerable command/support models;
- spread against templates;
- place Mines or Repeaters;
- establish new AROs;
- move outside hostile Repeater/Hacking Areas;
- move far enough away that retaliation becomes Order-expensive;
- rebuild or reshape a defensive Fireteam if useful.

A strong attack run often ends with:

> **enemy capability removed + mission progress + attacker difficult to punish.**

That is usually stronger than killing one additional cheap model while leaving a valuable attacker exposed in the enemy half.

## 18. Ask what the opponent's best first Order will be

Before passing the turn, ask:

> **If the Reactive Turn began now, what would the opponent's best first activation be?**

If the answer is obvious and dangerous, consider spending remaining Orders to interfere with it.

Possible answers include:

- kill the attacker;
- Isolate or Immobilize it;
- kill its NCO or support piece;
- destroy the Repeater enabling it;
- mine its exit route;
- Smoke or otherwise obstruct the important lane;
- reposition an ARO to force a costly engagement;
- remove one or two Orders from its Combat Group;
- move the likely target out of reach.

You do not necessarily need to kill the threat.

> **Making the enemy's next useful action expensive can be enough.**

## 19. Common dependency chains

Learn to recognize common chains and attack the weakest link.

### Hacking chain

**Repeater/Pitcher → Hacker → Targeted/IMM/Isolated → gunfighter/Guided payoff**

Break whichever link is cheapest and most decisive.

### TAG chain

**Orders → TAG → Engineer**

If the TAG is inefficient to attack directly, remove Orders or Engineer support.

### Fireteam chain

**Fireteam composition → support/vision control → gunfighter/Specialist**

Removing the correct support member may make the whole team much easier to handle.

### NCO attack chain

**Lieutenant Orders → NCO → mobile attacker → attack run**

Removing the NCO can strand resources even if the attacker itself survives.

### Objective chain

**Specialist → safe route → objective**

Kill the Specialist, deny the route, or control the objective rather than chasing unrelated combat pieces.

### Airborne/DZ-strike chain

**deployment support / open DZ → airborne attacker → vulnerable backfield**

Sometimes removing support matters. Sometimes the correct solution is simply protecting the landing/entry area so the attacker becomes inefficient.

## 20. Do not automatically finish Unconscious models

An Unconscious target is already temporarily removed from meaningful play. Spending additional Orders to finish it should have a reason.

Finish the model when:

- Doctor/Engineer recovery is likely and strategically important;
- the model can still contribute to mission scoring if recovered;
- the body itself matters for a mission interaction;
- the finishing attack is nearly free;
- preventing recovery is more valuable than any alternative use of the Order.

Leave it when:

- recovery is unlikely;
- the relevant Doctor/Engineer is dead or inaccessible;
- finishing the model costs a valuable Order;
- live targets are more important;
- the recovery attempt itself would force the opponent to spend several Orders.

An Unconscious TAG with no practical Engineer access may already be strategically dead.

## 21. A damaged survivor can become an Order trap

Sometimes leaving a damaged model alive is better than finishing it.

Example:

A premium HI is:

- wounded;
- badly positioned;
- separated from support;
- far from the mission;
- expensive to reposition.

Killing it may take three additional Orders.

Leaving it alive may tempt the opponent to spend several Orders recovering, moving, or trying to make the model relevant again.

This leads to an important distinction:

> **The objective is not maximum casualties. The objective is maximum reduction in the opponent's useful future actions.**

## 22. Mission priority overrides combat priority

Target selection should ultimately serve scoring.

Combat is usually justified because it does one or more of the following:

- opens a scoring route;
- prevents enemy scoring;
- protects Specialists or zone-control pieces;
- damages the opponent's ability to interfere later;
- creates a decisive Order advantage;
- changes the end-state of a zone or objective.

If killing a 40-point attacker costs four Orders while scoring two objectives costs three, scoring is usually the stronger play unless that attacker directly prevents it.

This is why target priority and mission strategy cannot be separated cleanly.

## 23. Practical Active-Turn sequence

Use this as the quick-reference process:

1. **Define the required end state.**
   - What must be scored, held, killed, protected, or positioned by the end of the turn?
2. **Identify the actual blockers.**
   - Ignore unrelated threats.
3. **Find dependency pieces.**
   - Orders, LT, NCO, Hacker, Repeater, Engineer, Doctor, EVO, Specialist, FT glue, vision control.
4. **Choose the cheapest reliable solver.**
   - gun, hacking, CC, Smoke, White Noise, Speculative Fire, Sensor, AD, bypass.
5. **Perform setup only when it improves enough later Orders.**
6. **Take easy targets that open routes or break packages.**
7. **Exploit the resulting geometry or capability loss.**
8. **Re-evaluate after every major success or failure.**
9. **Stop when marginal Order efficiency collapses.**
10. **Score.**
11. **Reposition for the Reactive Turn.**

The shortest useful target-selection checklist is:

> **What dies? What breaks when it dies? What does it cost to kill? What becomes possible afterward?**

And the shortest attack-sequencing rule is:

> **Remove only what matters, make each success improve the next Order, stop when the attack becomes inefficient, then score and exit.**
