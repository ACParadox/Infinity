# List-Building Strategy — Mission, Scoring, and Specialist Strategy

Infinity is not primarily won by destroying the enemy army. Combat is usually a means of creating or denying scoring opportunities.

A useful framework is:

> **Scoring efficiency = Objective Points gained or denied per Order, adjusted for reliability, access, timing, and the risk of losing the scoring piece.**

This changes how profiles should be evaluated. A mediocre gunfighter can be an excellent competitive piece if it reaches objectives cheaply, completes scenario actions reliably, survives long enough to matter, or carries a restricted Order that can be converted directly into scoring.

The mission packet should therefore influence list construction at least as much as the expected enemy factions.

## Current-season context

As of ITS Season 18 / N5.3, mission structure varies heavily. Some missions reward Classified coverage, some reward end-of-game control, some score Zones of Operations repeatedly, some make Specialists themselves targets, and some combine several of these pressures.

Do not build around a generic idea of “ITS missions.” Read the actual tournament packet and identify:

1. what scores;
2. when it scores;
3. who is allowed to perform the scoring action;
4. whether the result can be reversed;
5. whether a scoring model must survive or remain in position;
6. whether the mission rewards or punishes going first;
7. whether any objective is scarce, movable, destroyable, or permanently claimable.

The same army can want very different specialists and turn-order choices in different missions.

## 1. How many Specialists are enough?

There is no universal correct number because Specialist quality matters more than raw count.

For a normal 300-point competitive list, a useful starting heuristic is:

- **2 Specialists:** fragile. One casualty or bad matchup can remove most scoring capability.
- **3 Specialists:** lean but workable if they are independently positioned, mobile, protected, or multifunctional.
- **4–5 Specialists:** strong default range for an all-comers list.
- **6+ Specialists:** excellent when they are cheap, dual-role, or demanded by the mission packet, but raw quantity alone is not valuable.

The more important question is whether the list has **independent scoring paths**.

A good baseline is usually:

- at least one Specialist that starts near the midfield;
- at least one Specialist that can reach distant or lateral objectives cheaply;
- at least one Specialist that can survive or be preserved for Turn 3;
- more than one Specialist type for Classified and support coverage;
- no single casualty that completely removes the ability to perform the packet's main Specialist tasks.

Four Specialists standing together in one Fireteam are less redundant than three Specialists distributed across different routes and activation structures.

> **Count scoring routes, not Specialist labels.**

## 2. Specialist quality has several separate axes

A Specialist should be evaluated on at least six dimensions.

### Access

How many Orders are required before the model can physically interact with the objective?

Strong access tools include:

- Infiltration;
- Forward Deployment;
- high MOV;
- Motorcycle or Vehicle movement;
- Climbing Plus;
- Super-Jump;
- Airborne Deployment;
- Fireteam movement compression;
- Marker State for safe approach.

Deployment skills are particularly valuable because they save movement Orders before the game begins.

### Reliability

If the scenario requires a WIP Roll, higher WIP reduces the expected Order cost of scoring.

Some missions also give bonuses to specific Specialist types, so the best Specialist is mission-dependent rather than simply the highest-WIP model.

### Survivability

A model that reaches the objective but immediately dies may be acceptable for a permanent button press, but terrible when it must:

- carry an objective;
- remain in a zone;
- survive until the end of the game;
- preserve Specialist count;
- perform several tasks over multiple rounds.

Durability includes more than ARM and wounds. Camouflage, Mimetism, Prone access, mobility, Total Cover routes, NWI, Dogged, Remote Presence, Fireteam protection, and the ability to retreat after scoring can all increase mission survivability.

### Independence

A Specialist is more reliable when it does not depend on one fragile delivery system.

Examples of poor independence include:

- every Specialist being in the same Fireteam;
- all Specialists depending on the same Repeater network;
- all scoring models starting in the same Combat Group with no alternative Order sink;
- one Doctor being the only model capable of a mission-specific medical task.

### Secondary utility

Specialists are more attractive when their normal battlefield role remains useful even when the mission does not require them.

Examples:

- Hacker + network control;
- Engineer + TAG/REM repair;
- Doctor/Paramedic + recovery;
- Forward Observer + Flash Pulse/Targeted support;
- Chain of Command + Lieutenant insurance;
- Specialist Operative on a combat or mobility profile.

### Timing

A Specialist that is excellent on Turn 1 may be poor as a Turn-3 closer, and vice versa.

The list normally wants both early and late scoring capability.

## 3. Fast vs infiltrating vs durable Specialists

These are not interchangeable roles.

| Specialist type | Main strength | Main weakness | Best use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Infiltrating / Forward Deploying** | Starts close; effectively saves movement Orders | Can begin exposed to midfield pressure | Early buttons, central objectives, Classifieds, creating immediate scoring threats |
| **Fast** | Reaches either flank or distant objective late | Usually starts farther away | Turn-3 flips, lateral response, recovering from lost objectives |
| **Durable** | Can carry, hold, or survive in contested space | More expensive; may be slower | Objective carriers, zone control, repeated interactions |
| **Marker-state** | Safe approach and information pressure | May lose Marker State when interacting or carrying scenario elements | Midfield access, protected first interaction |
| **Airborne** | Bypasses normal approach lanes | Scenario exclusion zones and arrival constraints can reduce value | Late-game surprise scoring, remote objectives, Classifieds |
| **Fireteam Specialist** | Moves with the attack piece for no additional movement Orders | Fireteam route may not match objective geometry | Attacker + Specialist packages, repeated forward scoring |
| **Cheap backfield Specialist** | Low-cost redundancy and utility | High Order cost to reach the midfield | Reserve scorer, nearby Classifieds, emergency Turn-3 use |

The strongest general package is often a **complementary trio** rather than three copies of the same role:

> **midfield Specialist + fast Specialist + preserved/durable Specialist.**

## 4. Specialist redundancy

Redundancy should be measured by **task, geography, and timing**.

### Task redundancy

If the mission requires a specific Specialist type, losing the only model with that skill can effectively end the scoring plan.

When the packet strongly rewards a particular class, two independent examples are often justified.

### Geographic redundancy

Two Specialists on opposite flanks are more useful than two Specialists standing beside each other if the objectives are spread across the table.

A useful question during list review is:

> **If the opponent kills the Specialist closest to the left objective, how many Orders does the replacement require to get there?**

### Timing redundancy

Do not spend every Specialist aggressively on Turns 1–2.

A list often wants:

- an **opener** that can score early;
- a **worker** that can continue scoring through the middle game;
- a **closer** preserved for Turn 3.

The same model can fill several roles, but the plan should still exist before the game begins.

## 5. The Turn-3 closer

End-of-game scoring makes a preserved Specialist disproportionately valuable.

A good closer has one or more of:

- high MOV;
- Forward Deployment or Infiltration while still alive and hidden;
- Airborne Deployment where the scenario permits it;
- Camouflage or strong defensive MODs;
- NWI/multiple wounds;
- Tactical Awareness;
- NCO;
- a Fireteam that can move it with another useful model;
- access to a Combat Group that still has enough Orders on Turn 3.

The closer should usually be **pre-positioned on Turn 2**. A model that needs five movement Orders before it can score on Turn 3 is not a real reserve plan.

A common mistake is spending the final Orders of the game on an unnecessary gunfight while an uncontested objective can still be flipped.

Before beginning the last attack run, count the Orders required for the scoring route first.

> **Reserve the scoring Orders before spending the combat Orders.**

## 6. Objective carriers are different from button pushers

A Specialist that only needs to touch a Console and succeed once can be expendable after completing the task.

A Specialist carrying a scenario element is a different asset.

Carrier priorities are:

1. acquire the objective cheaply;
2. move it away from the enemy efficiently;
3. survive;
4. avoid being pinned in a location where the opponent can reclaim it;
5. preserve enough Orders to reposition again later.

Fast and durable Specialists therefore gain value in carry missions even when their WIP is mediocre.

Current ITS FAQ guidance also matters: a Trooper carrying a scenario element cannot enter Marker State. A Camo Specialist that is excellent at reaching the objective can therefore become much more vulnerable after taking it.

This creates a useful distinction:

- **retrieval Specialist:** best at reaching and taking the objective;
- **carrier Specialist:** best at escaping and surviving with it.

When the scenario permits transferring the objective between friendly models, transferring from a fragile retriever to a durable carrier can be worth the extra activation.

## 7. Zone-control missions

Zone missions often reward models that would not normally be considered scoring pieces.

The important resource is usually **Victory Points present in the zone**, not number of models.

This increases the value of:

- durable Heavy Infantry;
- TAGs;
- expensive midfield pieces;
- fast high-point models that can cross a zone boundary late;
- Baggage when the scenario specifically adds its bonus;
- Marker-state troops when the mission permits Models or Markers to count.

A 45-point model moving one inch across a zone boundary may change the score more efficiently than spending three Orders killing a 12-point enemy.

### Zone differential, not absolute presence

Do not ask only “How many points do I have in this zone?”

Ask:

> **How many more points than the opponent do I need to secure this zone?**

If the opponent has 18 points present, moving a 25-point model into the zone may solve the problem immediately. Killing three unrelated models elsewhere does nothing.

### Secure one, contest another

Trying to dominate every area can spread the army too thin.

A common efficient plan is:

- make one zone difficult for the opponent to take;
- contest or flip the most valuable second zone;
- force the opponent to spend Orders entering your prepared defensive geometry.

### Progressive zone scoring

If zones score every Game Round, positioning has immediate value. A unit that ends Turn 1 in the correct zone is already producing Objective Points or forcing the opponent to spend Orders removing it.

This makes early positional aggression more valuable than in missions that score only at the end.

## 8. Classified-objective coverage

Raw Specialist count is not the same as Classified coverage.

The local Classified reference includes requirements spread across several families:

- **information / hacking:** Hacker, Forward Observer, Spotlight;
- **medical / technical:** Doctor, Paramedic, Engineer;
- **command / training:** Lieutenant, NCO, Chain of Command, Veteran, Elite;
- **body type:** MI, HI;
- **equipment / utility:** D-Charges, Sensor, MSV, Biometric Visor;
- **close combat / positional tasks:** CC capability and reaching enemy-side positions.

Hackers and Forward Observers tend to cover several information-oriented cards, while Doctors and Engineers open different families and simultaneously provide battlefield support.

A useful all-comers goal is not “cover every Classified.” It is:

> **Avoid having a large portion of the Classified deck become effectively impossible for both lists.**

For tournament preparation, make a small matrix for each list:

| Capability family | Covered? | Best model | Backup |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hacker / Spotlight |  |  |  |
| Forward Observer |  |  |  |
| Doctor / Paramedic |  |  |  |
| Engineer / GizmoKit |  |  |  |
| CoC / Veteran / Elite |  |  |  |
| D-Charges |  |  |  |
| Sensor / MSV / Biometric |  |  |  |
| MI / HI |  |  |  |
| CC-capable scorer |  |  |  |

Classified-heavy missions such as Akial Interference can elevate this matrix from secondary consideration to a major list-building constraint.

## 9. Killing versus scoring opportunity cost

Combat should be evaluated by how it changes the scoring state.

Good reasons to attack include:

- remove an ARO piece blocking the scoring path;
- kill an enemy Specialist needed for the main objective;
- kill an objective carrier;
- remove enough enemy Victory Points to flip a zone;
- destroy a model that can reverse the objective next turn;
- reduce the opponent's Order pool enough that they cannot execute their scoring plan;
- protect a friendly Specialist or carrier;
- complete a kill-based mission objective or Classified.

Poor reasons include:

- the target is merely available;
- the target is expensive but irrelevant to the current scoring state;
- the attack requires several Orders after the main objective is already secure;
- the gunfight exposes the only remaining Specialist;
- the kill does not change what the opponent can score.

A useful decision question is:

> **If this attack succeeds, which Objective Point becomes easier for me to gain or harder for the opponent to gain?**

If the answer is “none,” the attack should face a high burden of justification.

### Score first or kill first?

Score first when:

- the objective is currently accessible;
- the scoring action is cheap and reliable;
- the result is permanent or difficult to reverse;
- combat failure could remove the chance to score;
- the game or round may end soon.

Kill first when:

- the objective can be immediately reversed;
- the defender can kill the Specialist during approach;
- the enemy scorer/carrier must be removed before the objective matters;
- removing one model dramatically reduces the opponent's ability to respond.

## 10. Going first vs second based on scenario

Turn order should be chosen from the mission's scoring timing rather than from a generic preference.

### Signals that favor going first

Going first becomes more attractive when:

- there are scarce objectives that can be seized and moved away;
- early possession creates a snowball;
- the mission scores progressively and you can establish hard-to-remove board control immediately;
- killing key Specialists can cripple the opponent's mission before they act;
- you can lock objectives behind mines, hacking, Repeaters, Suppressive Fire, or other denial;
- the opponent's list is highly vulnerable to an alpha strike;
- the mission rewards kills or destroying mission pieces;
- early interaction permanently scores rather than merely marking temporary ownership.

### Signals that favor going second

Going second becomes more attractive when:

- most important points are checked only at the end of the game;
- Consoles or Antennas can be flipped back and forth;
- zone control is checked after the second player's turn;
- the last objective interaction is difficult or impossible to answer;
- the mission rewards surviving Specialists or objective carriers;
- the table provides enough defensive deployment that the first-turn attack can be absorbed;
- final positioning matters more than early attrition.

### Progressive scoring does not automatically mean “always go second”

The second player may have the local advantage of acting immediately before an end-of-round scoring check, but the first player can often compensate by:

- occupying the area with durable pieces;
- creating mines or hacking coverage;
- forcing the second player to spend several Orders just to contest;
- removing the models required to score;
- scoring early rounds while preserving an Order or material advantage.

### Deployment choice can outweigh turn order

On asymmetric tables or missions, choosing deployment can be stronger than choosing first or second.

Important factors include:

- shortest safe routes to objectives;
- roof and vertical access;
- objective-room entrances;
- long firelanes;
- protected staging areas;
- Repeater/Pitcher geometry;
- whether a DZ gives fast Specialists a direct lane to several objectives.

Mission-specific rules can also tie important choices to deployment selection, so read the scenario before making the Initiative decision.

## 11. Preserving Specialists

The best way to preserve a Specialist is often to avoid asking it to solve combat problems.

A strong sequence is:

1. attacker clears or suppresses the route;
2. vision-control/network tools make the approach safe;
3. Specialist moves through the prepared route;
4. Specialist performs the scenario action;
5. Specialist retreats, goes Prone, or occupies Total Cover if continued presence is unnecessary.

Useful preservation techniques include:

- keep one Specialist completely hidden until late game;
- use Camo/Impersonation to approach rather than fight;
- avoid placing every Specialist in the same blast/template area;
- do not leave a button pusher standing beside the objective after the button no longer requires its presence;
- use Doctors/Engineers to restore mission-critical Specialists when the recovered model still has a realistic scoring path;
- preserve Command Tokens for Combat Group transfers or recovery when the mission plan depends on them;
- keep the closer in a Combat Group that is likely to retain usable Orders.

Some missions invert normal behavior. In Firefight, for example, having more surviving Specialists and killing enemy Specialists directly affects the score, so a normally expendable Specialist may need to be protected as a scoring asset in its own right.

## 12. Tactical Awareness, NCO, and other self-funded scoring Orders

Restricted Orders become much more valuable when the model carrying them can spend them on mission work.

### Tactical Awareness Specialist

A Tactical Awareness Specialist effectively brings a self-funded movement, interaction, or repositioning Order every Active Turn.

This is especially strong on:

- fast Specialists;
- durable Specialists;
- objective carriers;
- models already expected to move toward the midfield;
- Turn-3 closers.

A Tactical Awareness attacker that happens to be a Specialist can therefore be much more valuable than an otherwise stronger gunfighter in an objective-heavy packet.

### NCO Specialist

An NCO Specialist converts the Lieutenant Order into scoring flexibility.

This is particularly valuable when:

- the Lieutenant is intentionally protected and does not want to activate;
- the NCO already wants to move toward objectives;
- the list lacks Strategos conversion;
- the NCO is also a Fireteam member or strong combat piece.

### Strategos

Strategos can make Lieutenant resources more flexible and can reduce Combat Group friction. This indirectly improves mission scoring because the army is less likely to strand Orders away from the surviving Specialist or closer.

### Irregular Specialists

An Irregular Specialist can still be good when it can largely self-fund its own scoring route, especially if it begins near the objective. It is weaker when several Regular Orders are required to transport it across the table.

Command Token conversion can rescue this when the mission payoff is high enough.

> **A restricted Order is most valuable when its owner can convert it directly into Objective Points.**

## 13. Fireteam Specialists and activation compression

An attacker + Specialist Fireteam is one of the cleanest scoring packages in a Sectorial.

The attacker spends Orders clearing lanes while the Specialist receives the movement for free.

Strong pairings include:

- long-range attacker + midfield Specialist;
- durable assault piece + Engineer;
- Hacker + Pitcher carrier;
- vision-control model + Specialist;
- short-range assault model + Doctor/Paramedic;
- Specialist + Number 2 in a team expected to take casualties.

The danger is over-concentration. If every scoring piece is in one Fireteam, template attacks, isolation, loss of the leader, or route constraints can cripple the entire mission plan.

A useful structure is often:

> **one linked Specialist + one independent midfield Specialist + one independent late-game Specialist.**

## 14. When a mediocre combat unit becomes valuable because it scores

A profile should not be judged only by duel efficiency.

A seemingly mediocre combat profile becomes attractive when it combines several mission traits:

- Specialist status;
- Forward Deployment or Infiltration;
- 6-2, 8-2, Motorcycle, Vehicle, Super-Jump, or Climbing Plus movement;
- Camouflage or Mimetism;
- Tactical Awareness;
- NCO;
- Chain of Command;
- cheap points cost;
- Fireteam membership;
- D-Charges or Classified utility;
- Flash Pulse / Forward Observer;
- Doctor, Engineer, or Hacker utility;
- enough durability to survive objective duty.

A BS11 Specialist already beside the objective may be more valuable to the mission than a BS13 gunfighter that needs four Orders to reach it and cannot interact once it arrives.

The correct comparison is often:

> **combat value + scoring access + Order generation + mission utility**, not combat value alone.

Cheap Specialist upgrades are therefore frequently excellent purchases even when the weapon profile is otherwise unchanged.

## 15. Mission-specific Specialist archetypes

Different mission structures reward different profiles.

### Button missions

Prioritize:

- access;
- WIP;
- multiple independent Specialists;
- late-game reversibility;
- ability to retreat after interacting.

### Carry / extraction missions

Prioritize:

- fast and durable Specialists;
- safe extraction routes;
- carrier survival;
- backup retrievers;
- Turn-3 mobility.

### Zone-control missions

Prioritize:

- Victory Points that can enter zones efficiently;
- durable mobile models;
- Baggage where specifically rewarded;
- Specialists only to the extent the mission also includes Consoles/Antennas/Classifieds.

### Classified-heavy missions

Prioritize:

- broad skill/equipment coverage;
- several Specialist types;
- models that can reach the enemy half safely;
- preserving unusual capability pieces such as Engineer, Doctor, Sensor, D-Charges, or command-role troops.

### Specialist-hunting missions

Prioritize:

- redundancy;
- protected Specialists;
- Specialists that can defend themselves or remain hidden;
- killing enemy Specialists only when the mission actually rewards it.

### Kill missions

Do not automatically remove all Specialist investment. Many tournaments use two lists across a multi-mission packet, and even kill missions may reward Specialist survival or contain Classifieds.

## 16. Tournament-packet analysis

Before building the two lists, create a mission matrix.

| Mission | Main scoring timing | Specialist demand | Zone demand | Carry/extract | Classified demand | First/second lean | Special capability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mission 1 |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
| Mission 2 |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
| Mission 3 |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
| Mission 4 |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
| Mission 5 |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

Then ask:

- Which missions can List A solve cheaply?
- Which missions require List B?
- Is any mission poorly covered by both lists?
- Are both lists relying on the same fragile Specialist archetype?
- Is there at least one list with strong Classified coverage?
- Is there at least one list with fast/durable end-game scoring?
- Can either list play a zone mission without spending the whole game walking points forward?

This is more useful than trying to make both lists equally good at everything.

## 17. Practical scoring checklist during the game

At the start of each Game Round:

1. **What scores this round?**
2. **What scores only at the end of the game?**
3. **Which objectives can the opponent reverse?**
4. **Which of my remaining models can still score them?**
5. **Which enemy model is the biggest scoring threat?**
6. **How many Orders must I reserve to complete my scoring route?**
7. **What combat is actually required before scoring?**
8. **Where does my Turn-3 closer need to end this turn?**

Before the final attack run of the turn:

> **Recalculate the score before rolling more dice.**

It is common to discover that the correct move is no longer another attack.

# Condensed heuristics

- **At 300 points, 4–5 useful Specialists is a strong general default, but independent scoring paths matter more than raw count.**
- **Two Specialists is usually mission-fragile; three can work if they are protected, mobile, and geographically independent.**
- **Combine Specialist archetypes: midfield access + fast reach + durable/preserved closer.**
- **Redundancy means backup task + backup route + backup timing, not merely a duplicate skill.**
- **Preserve one real Turn-3 scorer and pre-position it before the final turn.**
- **In carry missions, retrieval and survival are separate problems.**
- **Zone missions reward Victory-Point movement, not just kills or Specialist count.**
- **Classified coverage requires non-Specialist capabilities too: D-Charges, Sensor/MSV/Biometric, command roles, MI/HI, Veteran/Elite, and CC access can matter.**
- **Kill only when the kill creates, protects, or denies scoring value.**
- **End-of-game reversible scoring generally increases second-player value; scarce/stealable objectives, early snowball, and specialist assassination increase first-player value.**
- **Tactical Awareness and NCO are substantially better on Specialists because restricted Orders can become direct scoring Orders.**
- **A mediocre fighter can be a premium mission piece if it combines mobility, Specialist status, deployment advantage, durability, or self-funded Orders.**
- **Read the exact mission packet before choosing the two lists. Mission coverage is a list-building stat.**
