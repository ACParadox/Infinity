# List-Building Strategy — State Warfare and Non-Lethal Control

Infinity does not require a model to be Dead before it stops mattering.

A better question than **“Can I kill this?”** is often:

> **“What is the cheapest way to remove the part of this model that matters?”**

A troop can be functionally dead because it cannot move, attack, receive useful Orders, score, project a hacking network, leave Close Combat, or recover without an unacceptable Order investment. State warfare is the strategy of creating those functional kills deliberately, then stopping the attack once the target is no longer worth further Orders.

## 1. States attack different resources

Do not treat every disabling state as interchangeable.

| State | Main thing it attacks | Typical strategic use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **IMM-A** | declarations and physical freedom | hard physical shutdown; force poor Dodge recovery |
| **IMM-B** | declarations and electronic freedom | hard shutdown; force penalized Reset recovery |
| **Isolated** | Order access, Fireteams, Comms and coordination | attack the Order economy and support network |
| **Targeted** | defensive efficiency and stealth | amplify follow-up attacks and tax Reset |
| **Stunned** | attacks and roll quality | temporary tempo/control tool |
| **Possessed** | ownership, activation and command resources | remove and potentially weaponize a premium target |
| **Engaged** | movement, LoF and legal declarations | positional imprisonment with a cheap body |
| **Unconscious** | activation and Order generation | recoverable kill |
| **Suppressive Fire** | freedom of movement through an area | proactive area-control state |

The important consequence is that **“disabled” is not one condition**.

- IMM-A and IMM-B can completely lock a model's declarations while it still contributes its Order to the pool.
- Isolated may leave a model physically capable of fighting while cutting it off from the Order Pool, Fireteams and Comms infrastructure.
- Unconscious removes the model's activation and Order contribution, but leaves a recovery window.
- Stunned may be strategically decisive even though it is temporary.
- Engaged can neutralize a gun platform without inflicting a Wound.

Choose the state that attacks the resource you actually need to remove.

## 2. The functional-kill principle

A **functional kill** is any result that makes further investment into the victim worse than changing targets.

Examples:

- a 70-point TAG is IMM-B and Isolated, far from useful recovery;
- an HMG is Engaged by a cheap warband and cannot influence the rest of the table;
- a sniper is Stunned, opening the route you needed for the rest of the turn;
- an objective Specialist is IMM-A on the wrong side of the table in Turn 3;
- a Hacker is Isolated, shutting down its programs and breaking part of the hacking network;
- an attack REM is Unconscious with its Engineer dead or unable to reach it.

Once the model is functionally dead, **recalculate before attacking it again**.

The points printed on the profile are sunk cost. What matters now is the model's **remaining future utility**.

### Stop shooting the 70-point model

Suppose a premium model is:

- Immobilized;
- disconnected from useful Orders or support;
- away from the scoring area;
- unable to threaten your next activation;
- expensive for the opponent to recover.

Spending three more Orders converting that condition into Dead may accomplish almost nothing.

Those Orders might instead:

- kill the Engineer;
- remove Order generators;
- attack a Specialist;
- establish Suppressive Fire;
- retreat the attacker;
- push an objective;
- disable a second premium model.

**Do not collect corpses for their own sake. Remove useful enemy actions.**

## 3. IMM-A — physical shutdown

IMM-A allows only **Dodge at PH-6**. The model still provides its Order.

That makes IMM-A particularly strong against pieces whose value depends on:

- moving into a new range band;
- crossing the table to score;
- shooting rather than Dodging;
- maintaining a specific firing position;
- escaping a close-range threat;
- reaching an Engineer only after substantial movement.

### Good IMM-A targets

- expensive gunfighters;
- TAGs or HI with poor recovery geometry;
- midfield Specialists that still need to move;
- objective holders that must reposition;
- low-PH pieces;
- troops whose only nearby recovery piece can be attacked separately.

### What IMM-A does *not* accomplish

It does not directly remove the target's Order contribution. If the strategic goal is to shrink the enemy Order pool, IMM-A alone is incomplete.

It also allows self-recovery through a successful penalized Dodge. Repeated attacks can therefore accidentally give the victim repeated chances to clear the state.

Sometimes the strongest follow-up to IMM-A is simply **leave**.

---

## 4. IMM-B — electronic shutdown and Reset pressure

IMM-B allows only **Reset at WIP-3**. Like IMM-A, the model still provides its Order.

This creates two values at once:

1. the piece is functionally locked until it recovers;
2. recovery itself may consume Orders.

A model that needs two or three attempts to Reset has already paid a substantial tempo cost even if it eventually succeeds.

### IMM-B changes the opponent's turn plan

The opponent now has to choose between:

- spending Orders trying to restore the premium piece;
- routing an Engineer to it;
- abandoning it and using the rest of the army.

All three outcomes can be good for you.

The purpose of IMM-B is therefore not necessarily permanent disablement. It can be an **Order-debt generator**.

---

## 5. Isolated — attack the army, not just the model

Isolated is fundamentally an **Order-economy and network state**.

An Isolated troop:

- cannot receive Orders from the Order Pool;
- if still Isolated at the start of following Active Turns, becomes Irregular for that Turn and does not add its Order to the pool;
- cannot convert that Irregular Order to Regular;
- loses Comms Attack / Comms Equipment functions and Hacking Programs;
- leaves its Fireteam, breaking the Fireteam if it was Team Leader;
- cannot participate in Coordinated Orders;
- suffers a severe Reset penalty.

This makes Isolated disproportionately valuable against models whose value is structural rather than purely offensive.

### High-value Isolation targets

- Fireteam Leaders;
- Lieutenants;
- NCO pieces;
- Hackers;
- Repeater carriers;
- Peripheral Controllers;
- premium attackers that require many pool Orders;
- Engineers or Doctors that need several pool Orders to reach a patient.

### Isolation is not physical paralysis

An Isolated troop may still be able to act with resources available specifically to it. Do not assume that Isolating an Engineer automatically prevents recovery.

For example, if an Engineer begins its turn close enough to a disabled TAG, its own activation may still be enough to reach and repair it.

Therefore:

> **Use Isolated to attack access and tempo; use IMM, Engaged, Stunned, Unconscious, positional denial, or death when you need the support model itself unable to perform the recovery.**

## 6. State stacking — build a recovery problem, not a token collection

State stacking is strongest when the states attack **different resources**.

Examples:

- **Isolated + IMM-B:** Order access is crippled and the target is also declaration-locked.
- **Targeted + IMM-B:** follow-up attacks improve while self-clearing Reset becomes less attractive.
- **Isolated + Targeted:** the target's network/economy value falls while subsequent attacks become easier.
- **Engaged + a state on the support piece:** the victim is trapped while its rescuer is delayed.

E/M is especially valuable against appropriate HI/TAG/REM/VH targets because a failed Save can create **Isolated + IMM-B** at once.

### Do not over-stack when one recovery action clears everything

N5 Engineer is a major limiter on state-stack value.

A successful Engineer state-clear can cancel **all states on the target that Engineer is allowed to cancel, except Unconscious**, in one WIP Roll. That includes common state-warfare results such as IMM-A, IMM-B, Isolated, Stunned and Targeted.

Similarly, a successful Reset can clear both Targeted and IMM-B together, while Isolated has its own Reset cancellation condition.

Therefore three tokens are not automatically three Orders of recovery.

If the enemy Engineer can safely touch the target, the next best “state” may be:

> **Engineer dead, Engaged, Immobilized, stranded, or otherwise unable to reach the patient.**

## 7. Attack recovery infrastructure before the centerpiece

Premium pieces often exist inside a dependency chain:

**TAG / REM / HI → Engineer → Servant / approach route → Order Pool**

or

**VITA attack piece → Doctor → Servant / approach route → Order Pool**

When the centerpiece is hard to kill but easy to disable, attack the chain instead.

### Example: Engineer before TAG

Rather than repeatedly shooting a TAG:

1. identify the Engineer and Servant paths;
2. determine whether the Engineer can clear the intended state in one activation;
3. attack or obstruct the recovery piece;
4. apply the disabling state to the TAG;
5. reassess whether the TAG needs any further attention.

The recovery piece can be denied by:

- killing it;
- putting it Unconscious;
- Immobilizing it;
- Engaging it;
- Isolating it when distance means one personal Order is insufficient;
- mining or covering the recovery route;
- killing or trapping the Servant;
- forcing it to expose itself to AROs;
- separating patient and support with terrain and threat zones.

The strategic objective is not necessarily **“kill the Engineer.”** It is:

> **“Make restoring this model cost more than the opponent can profitably pay.”**

---

## 8. Recovery traps

A recoverable model can become bait.

Suppose one E/M attack disables a premium piece. The opponent may now spend:

1. an Order moving an Engineer;
2. another Order completing the approach;
3. an Order attempting recovery;
4. another activation if recovery fails or positioning is poor;
5. more Orders moving the restored attack piece back into useful position.

Your one successful control attack may therefore produce several Orders of enemy work.

This is even stronger if the recovery route crosses:

- Mines;
- Repeaters;
- templates;
- overlapping AROs;
- Suppressive Fire;
- another E/M threat;
- a close-combat speed bump.

The opponent then has a bad choice:

- abandon an expensive model;
- spend Orders rescuing it;
- risk the recovery infrastructure too.

That is a successful state-warfare position even if nobody dies.

## 9. When Reset is worth Orders

Reset is not automatically correct just because a state can be cleared.

Ask:

> **“How many useful future actions do I buy by clearing this state?”**

### Reset aggressively when

- the model is the only viable attack piece for the current route;
- it must score this turn;
- clearing Targeted materially changes its survival odds;
- the model can immediately generate more value than the expected recovery cost;
- the state threatens a critical Lieutenant, Fireteam, hacking or scoring plan;
- no Engineer can clear the state more efficiently.

### Accept the state when

- the model's job is already finished;
- it is too far from anything relevant;
- the game is nearly over;
- another model can perform the same role more cheaply;
- the Engineer can later clear several states at once;
- repeated Reset attempts would consume the attack/scoring budget;
- recovery would restore a model that still needs several more Orders just to re-enter the fight.

### Reset opportunity cost

Do not evaluate a Reset as “one Order for a chance to recover.”

Evaluate it as:

> **“One Order not spent scoring, attacking, developing or retreating, multiplied by the probability that I still need additional recovery Orders afterward.”**

A low-odds recovery attempt on a strategically obsolete piece is often a trap.

## 10. Targeted — a force multiplier and tempo state

Targeted is not a hard disable.

Instead it makes the victim easier to attack:

- BS Attacks, Comms Attacks and Discover gain +3 against it;
- its Reset is penalized;
- it cannot use Cautious Movement;
- it cannot use Stealth.

The correct follow-up question is therefore:

> **“What became cheap now?”**

Possible answers:

- a direct gunfight;
- another hacking attack;
- Guided fire;
- Discover against a difficult target;
- forcing the victim to spend Orders clearing Targeted instead of advancing.

Targeted is often best when it changes the economics of the **next** interaction rather than being treated as an endpoint.

---

## 11. Stunned — temporary control can be enough

Stunned troops cannot declare Attacks and suffer -3 to their other Rolls except Saves. The state is temporary, but that does not make it weak.

The key question is:

> **“How much of the current turn do I need this model to stop influencing?”**

### Good Stun uses

- shut down a dangerous ARO long enough to cross its lane;
- interrupt an active attack run;
- break the value of a premium gun without spending Orders killing it;
- cancel Suppressive Fire and open a route;
- impose enough of a penalty that a follow-up becomes favorable;
- preserve a target for later while solving the immediate tactical problem.

If stunning one sniper lets four other models cross safely, the value of Stun is the **movement and Orders it enabled**, not damage caused.

Temporary control becomes more valuable as the game gets shorter. On Turn 3, “for the rest of the relevant window” can be functionally equivalent to permanent removal.

---

## 12. Engaged — positional imprisonment

Engaged is one of the cleanest examples of non-lethal control.

An Engaged model:

- is limited to a narrow set of CC/Engaged-legal declarations;
- cannot trace LoF outside the Close Combat;
- generally has to win or Dodge free before resuming its normal role.

This means a cheap troop reaching Silhouette contact can neutralize a much more expensive ranged piece without killing it.

### Engage for tempo

A cheap model can tie up:

- an HMG;
- a sniper or missile launcher;
- a TAG gun platform;
- a Specialist trying to reach an objective;
- a Hacker that needs to interact elsewhere;
- a Fireteam piece whose position matters.

The cheap model does not need to win the duel immediately. If the opponent spends two or three Orders escaping, the trade may already be excellent.

### Turn-3 imprisonment

Engaged becomes especially powerful late.

A premium model locked in CC with too few Orders remaining to escape and reach the objective is **strategically dead with all its Wounds intact**.

---

## 13. Possessed — control plus Command Token pressure

Possessed is more than “TAG disabled.”

A Possessed troop:

- cannot be activated by its owner or receive Orders from the owner's pool;
- contributes no Order in either player's Tactical Phase;
- becomes allied to the possessing player and enemy to its original owner;
- can be activated from the Combat Group of the troop that caused Possession;
- leaves any Fireteam;
- uses the Possessed profile.

The owner can automatically cancel Possessed during the appropriate Tactical Phase by spending a Command Token, and Total Control can also restore the model.

That creates three possible payoffs:

1. remove the premium target from the opponent's plan;
2. use its position and weapons against its own army;
3. force expenditure of a finite Command Token even if the state is cleared immediately.

Do not judge Possession only by how long the state remains on the table.

---

## 14. Unconscious — a recoverable kill

Unconscious is much closer to a conventional kill than IMM or Isolated:

- the model cannot activate or ARO;
- it does not contribute an Order;
- its Automatic Skills and Equipment stop functioning;
- it remains on the table and can potentially be recovered.

That creates the familiar **finish or leave** decision.

### Finish the model when

- its Doctor/Engineer has an easy recovery route;
- restoring it would return a critical attacker or Specialist;
- Remote Presence or other recovery tools make revival unusually efficient;
- the model is near an objective and could immediately matter after recovery;
- Coup de Grâce or another finishing action is cheap and safe.

### Leave it Unconscious when

- recovery infrastructure is dead or inaccessible;
- the support route is itself too dangerous;
- finishing it would consume scarce Orders;
- it is positioned too far from future relevance even if restored;
- attacking something else produces more immediate value.

The goal is not “make every Unconscious model Dead.” The goal is **remove realistic future contributions**.

## 15. Suppressive Fire — create a state that attacks enemy choices

Suppressive Fire is state warfare from the defensive side.

Within its 0–24 inch suppression range, enemies suffer -3 in Face-to-Face Rolls against the suppressing model, while the model reacts with B3 using the Suppressive Fire profile.

Its real job is not necessarily to hit anything.

Its job is:

> **“Make this route expensive enough that the opponent changes plans or spends Orders solving it.”**

### Suppression can be defeated without killing the shooter

Suppressive Fire is canceled by several control results, including:

- Engaged;
- Isolated;
- Immobilized states;
- Null states;
- failing Guts;
- declaring a non-SF ARO;
- other listed cancellation conditions.

Therefore, when facing a strong suppressive piece, consider whether the cheapest answer is:

- force a different ARO;
- Engage it;
- hack or E/M it;
- force Guts;
- bypass the lane;
- attack from outside its useful geometry.

Again, **solve the function, not necessarily the model**.

## 16. Make the opponent choose between recovery and mission play

The strongest state-warfare turns often leave several *different* recovery problems.

For example:

- one HI Isolated;
- another attacker IMM-B;
- a Specialist Targeted;
- the main shooter Engaged;
- the Engineer pinned behind a Mine or ARO.

The opponent's next turn cannot simply “undo one debuff.” They must decide how much of the turn to spend repairing the army.

Every Order spent on:

- Reset;
- Dodge to clear IMM-A;
- moving an Engineer;
- moving a Servant;
- escaping Close Combat;
- reviving an Unconscious piece;

is an Order not spent:

- scoring;
- attacking;
- reaching zones;
- establishing defence;
- repositioning for Turn 3.

This is **Order debt**.

State warfare is strongest when you create more debt than the target's restored value can repay.

---

## 17. Time remaining changes the value of control

A state does not need to be permanent. It only needs to last long enough.

### Turn 1

Recovery has high value because the model may contribute for two more turns. Expect the opponent to invest more heavily in restoring premium pieces.

This makes attacking recovery infrastructure especially important.

### Turn 2

Evaluate board position and mission timing. A restored model may still swing the game, but a distant or badly positioned model may already be strategically obsolete.

### Turn 3

Temporary control becomes extremely powerful.

If the opponent has four Orders left, a state requiring two recovery Orders plus two Orders of movement may already be permanent **for practical purposes**.

Turn 3 therefore rewards:

- IMM instead of overkilling;
- Engaged instead of winning the CC;
- Stun to open the final route;
- Isolated to strand a premium piece;
- Targeted as a threat that forces the opponent to choose between clearing it and scoring.

## 18. State priority by target role

### Cheap Order generator

If the objective is Order removal, **Unconscious/Dead or Isolated** is usually more relevant than IMM alone because IMM-A/B still let the troop contribute its Order.

### Premium gunfighter

IMM-A/B, Engaged or Stunned may be enough. Killing it may be unnecessary if it cannot influence the important lane or current turn.

### Hacker

Isolated is exceptionally valuable because it also disables Hacking Programs and relevant Comms functions.

### Fireteam member / Team Leader

Isolated can remove the model from the Fireteam; isolating the Team Leader breaks the team. This may be worth more than dealing a Wound.

### Lieutenant

Isolated can create a Loss of Lieutenant threat if the state remains at the required timing. This can make Engineer/recovery pressure much more urgent than the target's points value suggests.

### Engineer / Doctor

Ask what recovery action it needs to perform.

- If it needs several pool Orders to travel, Isolation may be enough.
- If it is already next to the patient, Isolation alone may not stop recovery.
- IMM, Engaged, Stunned where relevant, Unconscious, Mines, AROs or route denial may be better.

### TAG / HI / REM

Look for control packages before brute-force damage:

- E/M for Isolated + IMM-B where applicable;
- Carbonite for IMM-B;
- Oblivion for Isolated;
- Total Control against valid TAG targets;
- PARA/IMM-A delivery;
- cheap CC engagement after another disable.

Then stop and ask whether any further attack is required.

### Objective Specialist

A Specialist does not need to die if it cannot reach or interact with the objective before the game ends. IMM, Engaged, isolation from Orders or positional control may be the cheapest mission answer.

## 19. Sequence control effects around dependencies

Against a supported premium piece, a good control sequence is often:

1. **Identify the required function.** What exactly must the target stop doing?
2. **Identify recovery.** Who or what can clear the intended state?
3. **Attack the recovery path.** Support piece, Servant, route, Order access or network.
4. **Apply the minimum sufficient state.** Do not automatically pursue maximum token stacking.
5. **Exploit the opening.** Score, move, attack something else, or establish defence.
6. **Reassess before every follow-up attack.** Is the original target still worth an Order?

This is the state-warfare equivalent of target sequencing.

## 20. List-building implications

A list should be able both to **apply control** and to **recover from it**.

### Offensive control package

A robust list benefits from some combination of:

- E/M;
- PARA / IMM-A delivery;
- Oblivion / Carbonite / Spotlight;
- Total Control if TAG matchups justify it;
- Stun;
- cheap CC engagement pieces;
- Suppressive Fire-capable midfield pieces;
- deployables that protect disabled targets and recovery routes.

You do not need every state. You need **orthogonal answers** to expensive problems.

### Recovery package

When the list relies on TAGs, REMs, Hackable HI or expensive Specialists, audit:

- Engineer access;
- Doctor access where appropriate;
- Servant coverage;
- whether the recovery piece can reach the likely attack lanes;
- whether one support loss collapses several expensive models;
- whether the list can function if the primary attacker remains Isolated or Immobilized;
- whether backup pieces can score instead of spending half a turn on Reset/repair.

### Do not create a single recovery point of failure

If one Engineer is responsible for:

- the TAG;
- multiple REMs;
- clearing E/M;
- clearing Targeted;
- maintaining the hacking-support network indirectly;

then that Engineer is a high-priority structural target.

Deploy and protect it accordingly.

## 21. The state-warfare decision test

Before spending another Order on a controlled target, ask:

1. **Can it still attack effectively?**
2. **Can it move where it matters?**
3. **Can it still score?**
4. **Does it still contribute a useful Order?**
5. **Can it self-clear the state efficiently?**
6. **Can an Engineer/Doctor clear several problems at once?**
7. **How many Orders does recovery plus re-entry into the fight actually cost?**
8. **Can I attack the recovery infrastructure instead?**
9. **How many turns and Orders remain?**
10. **What strategic value do I gain by converting this functional kill into a literal kill?**

If the practical answer is:

> **“It cannot do anything important unless my opponent spends several Orders rescuing it,”**

then the correct play is usually:

> **Leave it alive and spend the Orders somewhere that changes the game.**

---

## Condensed doctrine

- **Disable the function you care about, not automatically the whole model.**
- **IMM removes actions; Isolated attacks Order/network access; Unconscious removes activation and Order generation.**
- **Stack states when they attack different resources, not merely to accumulate tokens.**
- **Remember that one Engineer action can clear multiple Engineer-removable states.**
- **Attack the recovery chain when that makes the disable effectively permanent.**
- **Isolation alone may not stop a nearby Engineer from using its own activation.**
- **Treat Reset, repair and escape attempts as Order costs you can deliberately impose.**
- **Use Stun and Engaged for tempo even when they cause no damage.**
- **Treat Suppressive Fire as an area-control state and attack its cancellation conditions.**
- **The later the game, the less permanent a disable needs to be.**
- **A 70-point model with no useful future actions is already strategically dead.**
- **Stop attacking when marginal Order efficiency collapses.**

The objective of state warfare is not to maximize casualties. It is to **minimize the opponent's remaining useful game actions per Order while preserving your own Orders for scoring and decisive attacks**.
