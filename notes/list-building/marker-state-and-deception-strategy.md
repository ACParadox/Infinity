# List-Building Strategy — Marker-State and Deception Strategy

**Current counter unit tiers:** [Anti-Tech — N5.3 apex/value](anti-tech-msv-mimetism-camo-impersonation-apex-value.md) compares affordable Sensor, MSV2/3, Biometric, Triangulated Fire, White Noise, Albedo and Eclipse carriers by *which technology they counter, Orders to deliver it, and opposing opinions*. This page retains the underlying marker and deception mechanics.

**Profile-specific N5.3 companion:** [Midfield Area Denial & Speed Bumps — apex/value](midfield-area-denial-and-speed-bumps-apex-value.md) differentiates cheap Decoy/Camo bluff (Strelok), Hidden double-mine (Shujae/Dasyu), Camo Minelayer (Chasseur/Foxtrot/Naga), and perimeter/Re​peater control (Moran/Gangbuster), including Sensor and Deactivator counters.

Marker-state play in Infinity is not mainly about getting one Surprise Attack. Its larger strategic value is that it converts **uncertainty into Orders, restricted movement, awkward ARO declarations, bad target selection, and deployment mistakes**.

The central question is therefore not:

> **How do I get the best attack from this marker?**

It is:

> **How much information, movement freedom, and Order efficiency can this marker deny before I reveal it?**

A marker that never attacks can still be highly successful if it makes the opponent spend Orders, choose the wrong route, protect the wrong target, delay an attack, or abandon an objective.

## 1. Marker states are an information economy

The value of a marker is not only defensive MODs or Surprise Attack. It also withholds information that the opponent would normally use to allocate Orders efficiently.

A visible trooper tells the opponent:

- what weapon it has;
- whether it is a Specialist;
- whether it is worth killing;
- whether it can threaten the attack route;
- whether it is likely to be the Lieutenant or support piece;
- what kind of response is needed.

A marker instead creates a question.

The opponent may need to determine:

- trooper or deployable;
- cheap piece or premium piece;
- Specialist or attacker;
- Mine or Camo trooper;
- real model or Decoy;
- dangerous marker or harmless distraction;
- visible threat or bait for a Hidden Deployment piece.

This means markers create value before any dice are rolled.

A useful heuristic is:

> **Marker value = combat value + information denied + Orders taxed + space controlled.**

This is why marker-heavy armies often feel more difficult to attack than their raw defensive stats suggest.

## 2. Staying Camouflaged instead of taking an ARO

A common error is revealing a Camouflaged trooper merely because it has a legal reactive shot.

Before declaring an Attack ARO, ask:

> **Is this B1 reactive attack worth surrendering the marker?**

Staying Camouflaged is often better when:

- the reactive shot is unlikely to stop the active trooper;
- the Camo model is an important Specialist needed later;
- revealing would let a second enemy attacker kill it efficiently;
- the marker is currently blocking or threatening an important route;
- the opponent would otherwise need to spend Orders Discovering it;
- the trooper will have a much stronger active-turn attack later;
- the opponent has declared a sequence designed to make you reveal voluntarily;
- the information value of remaining concealed is greater than the expected damage of the ARO.

This is especially important with expensive Camo Specialists. A low-probability rifle ARO that turns a protected marker into an exposed model can be a severe loss of value even if the shot itself is technically favorable.

The correct default is not **always shoot**.

It is:

> **Reveal only when the ARO meaningfully changes the opponent's plan.**

Examples include:

- stopping a Specialist from scoring;
- threatening a lethal template into a valuable cluster;
- killing or disabling the active attacker on a good probability;
- preventing a decisive Turn-3 move;
- protecting the Lieutenant or another critical asset.

## 3. The Discover tax

Discover is an Order-efficiency tax imposed by hidden information.

The ideal marker does not simply survive. It makes the opponent repeatedly pay to answer the question **"What is that?"**

The tax can include:

1. moving a suitable Discovering trooper into position;
2. spending an Order to Discover;
3. risking a failed roll;
4. bringing another trooper to try if the first one fails;
5. spending another Order to actually attack the revealed model;
6. repositioning afterward.

A cheap marker that dies after consuming several enemy Orders may have already traded very efficiently.

For example, if a low-cost infiltrator forces:

- one Order to approach;
- one Discover attempt;
- one additional Discover or Sensor action;
- one Order to kill it;

then the marker may have consumed more strategic resources than its points cost suggests.

The important metric is not merely:

> **Did the marker kill anything?**

It is:

> **How many Orders did this area of the table cost the opponent to solve?**

## 4. Make Discover attempts as inefficient as possible

Do not merely put markers somewhere difficult to shoot. Put them somewhere difficult to **identify efficiently**.

Good Discover-tax geometry includes:

- Cover;
- awkward ranges;
- terrain that forces the Discovering model to move first;
- positions where only one enemy can gain LoF at a time;
- locations where a failed Discover leaves the enemy badly positioned;
- positions where approaching the marker enters a Mine, Repeater, template, or other defensive zone;
- locations that force the opponent to expose a valuable Sensor or MSV platform.

The best marker position often creates a chain like:

> **Move to see marker → Discover → attack marker → reposition.**

That is much better than allowing:

> **Stand safely in DZ → several models all Discover → one model kills it.**

## 5. Discover geometry: avoid multiple simultaneous Discoverers

Marker defence becomes much weaker when many enemy models can all see the same marker and participate in the information-clearing process.

Therefore use terrain to expose valuable markers to as few observers as possible.

Prefer:

- corners rather than open rooftops;
- narrow lanes rather than broad plazas;
- positions that can be pie-sliced;
- markers behind staggered terrain;
- asymmetric positions where only the active model can see them.

This is the information equivalent of ordinary ARO slicing.

With shooting, you want to face one enemy gun at a time.

With markers, you want the opponent to have **one relevant Discoverer at a time**.

This makes each failed Discover more meaningful and prevents the opponent from solving the marker through sheer redundancy.

## 6. Discover + Shoot and the reveal decision

Discover + BS Attack creates an important marker-state decision.

If a marker stays concealed, the attacker must successfully solve the Discover portion before the attack can meaningfully proceed against the concealed target.

If the reactive marker voluntarily reveals with an Attack ARO, it may remove the need for the opponent to solve the information problem first.

Therefore do not automatically respond to **Discover + Shoot** by shooting back.

Ask:

> **Am I giving the opponent the information it was paying an Order to obtain?**

Sometimes the strongest response is to remain concealed and force the Discover roll to matter.

The principle is:

> **Do not voluntarily remove a tax the opponent has already paid to face.**

## 7. Force Discover-versus-Delay decisions

One of the strongest uses of marker movement is to force the reactive player to choose between identifying the marker and preparing for its reveal.

A marker moves into a threatening position.

The defender must decide whether to:

- Discover;
- Delay where permitted;
- ignore the marker;
- use another available defensive option.

The active marker then chooses its second Skill based on that declaration.

The strategic fork is approximately:

> **If you Discover, I may reveal and attack.**

> **If you Delay for my attack, I may simply keep moving.**

This gives marker movement intrinsic offensive value even before firing a weapon.

Do not become predictable, however.

If every marker always Move-Moves, opponents will increasingly favor Discover.

If every marker always moves and attacks, opponents will increasingly favor Delay.

Mix behavior so the opponent must continue solving the decision rather than following a script.

## 8. Use the first Short Skill to gather information

Marker play rewards delaying your own commitment.

A good first Short Skill often does three things at once:

- improves position;
- exposes the marker to the specific reactive model you want;
- forces the opponent to declare before you decide whether to reveal.

This is often stronger than beginning the Order with an attack plan already fixed.

Think of the first movement as asking a question:

> **What are you willing to commit to against this unknown threat?**

Then use the second Short Skill to punish the answer.

This makes marker-state play closely related to ordinary active-turn declaration forks, but with the added power of concealed identity.

## 9. Re-Camouflage is an information reset

Re-entering Camouflaged State is not merely defensive protection. It can recreate the opponent's information problem.

A strong attack run can therefore be:

> **Camo → attack → complete the objective → move behind Total Cover → Re-Camo.**

rather than:

> **Camo → attack repeatedly until the model dies.**

Re-Camo is particularly valuable for:

- midfield Specialists;
- expensive Mimetism pieces;
- assassins after killing the intended target;
- button pushers needed again later;
- models protecting an objective after scoring;
- models carrying mines or other area-control tools.

Before spending the Order, audit the conditions that can prevent re-entry into the state, including relevant enemy LoF, marker presence, Sensor coverage, and the exact current N5.3 requirements.

The larger strategic point is:

> **If the model has already accomplished its immediate task, restoring uncertainty may be worth more than another attack.**

## 10. Marker saturation and information saturation

One marker creates a question.

Six markers create a prioritization problem.

This is why Camo-heavy armies gain disproportionate value from marker density.

The opponent no longer asks only:

> **What is this marker?**

They must also ask:

> **Which markers are important enough to investigate at all?**

A dense marker network can contain:

- real attackers;
- Specialists;
- Minelayers;
- Mines;
- Decoys;
- cheap infiltrators;
- Hidden Deployment support nearby;
- markers whose only job is to consume attention.

This creates **information saturation**.

The defender should exploit it by making several markers strategically plausible. Do not place one obviously valuable marker and four obviously irrelevant ones.

Instead, create multiple believable threats around:

- objectives;
- attack lanes;
- DZ approaches;
- rooftops;
- corners;
- likely Specialist routes.

The opponent should be unable to tell which marker deserves the first Order.

## 11. Mine/Camo ambiguity

Mine/Camo ambiguity is one of the most efficient forms of deception because the opponent may need a completely different response depending on what the marker actually is.

The opponent may face:

- a mobile attacker;
- a Specialist;
- a disposable Mine;
- a Minelayer's real trooper;
- a marker intended only to redirect movement.

A useful setup is:

- one marker directly protecting the objective;
- one marker covering the likely approach;
- one marker positioned to attack if the first two are investigated.

The enemy must decide whether to:

- Discover;
- Dodge;
- Sensor;
- sacrifice a cheap model;
- detour;
- ignore the area.

This interaction is strongest when the markers' public characteristics make the ambiguity genuinely plausible. Silhouette, Mimetism value, and other public marker information can sometimes narrow the possibilities, so do not assume every Camo marker convincingly imitates every deployable.

The principle is:

> **Ambiguity only works when both explanations fit the visible information.**

## 12. Mix uncertainty with real area denial

Pure bluff is weaker than bluff backed by consequences.

Marker saturation works best when some of the markers really are:

- Mines;
- Minelayers;
- dangerous Camo gunfighters;
- Specialists that can score immediately;
- close-assault threats;
- network pieces or observers supporting another attack.

If the opponent learns that every marker can safely be ignored, the information game collapses.

The strongest marker architecture therefore combines:

> **unknown threat + real hazard + mission relevance.**

For example:

- a Camo Specialist beside an objective;
- a Mine on the direct approach;
- another marker covering the safe route;
- a Hidden Deployment piece capable of punishing the bypass.

Now every solution has a cost.

## 13. Decoys should occupy plausible positions

A Decoy is most effective when every possible location appears strategically credible.

Weak Decoy placement:

- two replicas in useless positions;
- one marker in the obvious firing or objective position.

The opponent can infer the answer without spending Orders.

Strong Decoy placement:

- all candidate markers have useful routes;
- several could plausibly be the Specialist;
- several could threaten the same attack lane from different angles;
- at least two positions would make sense for the real model.

The objective is not to create random noise.

It is to create **multiple coherent stories**.

A good Decoy setup should make the opponent think:

> **I can explain why the real model would be in any of these positions.**

## 14. HoloMask: lie plausibly

HoloMask is strongest when the disguise matches the army's visible structure.

Good disguises include:

- plausible Lieutenant candidates;
- ordinary line infantry;
- Fireteam fillers;
- cheap Specialists;
- support pieces;
- models whose presence explains the army's apparent deployment.

The best disguise is usually not the most surprising profile.

It is the one the opponent will **believe without investigating**.

A disguised model should therefore be placed where the imitated profile would reasonably stand.

For example:

- a fake line trooper protecting the backline;
- an apparent Specialist near an objective route;
- an apparent Lieutenant candidate in a safe position;
- a fake support profile beside a Fireteam or TAG.

The deception becomes more powerful when the real list already contains similar troops, because the opponent cannot easily distinguish normal duplication from HoloMask.

Be careful about public information that can expose an implausible disguise. In particular, interactions involving Hacker/Hackable status or other mandatory public information can reveal that the visible identity is not what it appears to be.

## 15. HoloMask and Lieutenant shell games

HoloMask becomes especially valuable when combined with genuine Lieutenant ambiguity.

A strong shell game can include:

- several same-WIP Lieutenant candidates;
- identical or near-identical line profiles;
- a HoloMasked model pretending to be another candidate;
- Chain of Command or other resilience hidden elsewhere;
- marker-state Lieutenant options where available.

The opponent should not be able to solve the Lieutenant simply from deployment logic.

The goal is not necessarily to make the Lieutenant impossible to find.

It is to make assassination require **additional Orders and uncertainty**.

This also protects non-Lieutenant pieces. If the opponent attacks the wrong candidate, the deception has traded information for Orders and positional exposure.

## 16. Holoechoes are target-selection tools

Holoechoes are more than fake wounds or visual clutter.

They create multiple candidate targets that can:

- approach from slightly different angles;
- force defenders to choose which echo to target;
- divide reactive attention;
- conceal the real model's intended final position;
- create several apparent threats during deployment.

The strongest Holoecho movement makes the real model **not obviously the best target**.

Do not place the echoes so the true model's route is clearly superior.

Use them to make several movement paths equally plausible.

## 17. Holoechoes as expendable probes

One especially useful tactical application is using echoes to interact with suspected deployable danger.

Because Holoechoes can count as real troopers for relevant trigger checks, they can be used to probe areas suspected of containing Mines or other automatic defenses.

This can provide information without exposing the real model to the same degree.

Possible uses include:

- testing a doorway suspected of being mined;
- moving an echo through a likely trigger area first;
- checking whether a route is genuinely safe;
- forcing a deployable to react before the real model commits.

This turns Holoecho into an **information-gathering and demining resource**, not merely a shooting deception.

## 18. Holoecho deployment can falsify defensive architecture

Holoecho + HoloMask can distort the opponent's understanding of your deployment before Turn 1 begins.

Several apparent copies of a dangerous profile can make it look as though multiple lanes are covered.

This can influence:

- reserve deployment;
- choice of table side;
- which attack lane the opponent plans to use;
- whether the opponent commits a premium piece to a particular flank;
- whether they spend Orders verifying a threat that does not actually exist.

A fake rooftop Sniper that changes where the opponent deploys has produced value before the first Order is spent.

The key is to create believable lane coverage rather than obviously impossible duplication.

## 19. Impersonation: the threat may be worth more than the attack

Impersonators are often evaluated as assassins, but their strongest contribution may be **deployment distortion and persistent threat**.

An Impersonation marker near the enemy backline can force protection around:

- the Lieutenant;
- Engineer;
- Doctor;
- hacker;
- Fireteam support;
- expensive Order generators;
- TAG support pieces.

Do not automatically launch the first available assassination.

Sometimes:

> **Impersonator parked beside the support network**

is stronger than:

> **Impersonator kills one cheap model and dies.**

The threat can force the opponent to:

- guard corners;
- move support pieces;
- spend Discover Orders;
- divert a Sensor/Biometric piece;
- break an efficient Fireteam position;
- delay a main attack because the backline is unsafe.

Evaluate the Impersonator by **how much of the enemy deployment it distorts**, not only by kills.

## 20. Do not attack merely because the Impersonator reached the target

Before revealing, ask:

> **What do I lose when this marker becomes a model?**

If the Impersonator currently threatens three valuable targets, revealing to kill the least important one may reduce pressure.

Good reasons to commit include:

- the Lieutenant is exposed;
- killing an Engineer collapses a TAG plan;
- removing a Doctor makes several unconscious bodies unrecoverable;
- killing a key hacker opens your heavy attack piece's route;
- the opponent is about to move away and the opportunity will disappear;
- the attack directly changes scoring.

Poor reasons include:

- a cheap Order generator happens to be nearby;
- the model can attack but the trade does not affect the enemy plan;
- revealing opens the Impersonator to trivial counterattack while stronger targets remain threatened.

## 21. Hidden Deployment makes empty space dangerous

Hidden Deployment extends the information game beyond visible markers.

Once the opponent knows that a faction or list can plausibly contain Hidden Deployment, **empty terrain itself becomes uncertain**.

The opponent may hesitate before:

- crossing a rooftop;
- entering a long firelane;
- moving a Specialist onto an exposed objective;
- advancing a valuable attacker through an apparently undefended flank;
- committing a Fireteam around a corner.

This means a Hidden Deployment model can influence movement even before it reveals.

More importantly, visible Camo markers become stronger when the opponent must also account for an invisible threat.

A marker screen plus Hidden Deployment creates two layers of uncertainty:

1. **What are the markers?**
2. **What is not on the table at all?**

## 22. Hidden Deployment should support a plan, not merely surprise

Do not reveal Hidden Deployment simply because a shot becomes available.

Preserve it when the opponent is still altering play around the possibility.

Reveal when doing so:

- stops a decisive scoring run;
- kills a premium attacker at favorable odds;
- creates a critical crossfire;
- protects the Lieutenant or Order pool;
- converts uncertainty into a major positional advantage.

A Hidden Deployment piece that reveals for a low-value target and immediately dies may have traded away the strongest part of its profile.

The hidden state itself is a resource.

## 23. Private information and concealed Lieutenant possibilities

Marker-state strategy becomes stronger when combined with the broader private-information structure of Infinity.

Relevant uncertainty can include:

- Lieutenant identity;
- marker contents;
- Hidden Deployment;
- HoloMask;
- Airborne Deployment;
- Chain of Command;
- Counterintelligence;
- other private deployment information.

The best deception lists create **overlapping uncertainties**.

For example, the opponent may need to ask simultaneously:

- Which line trooper is the Lieutenant?
- Is one of them HoloMasked?
- Is there a Camo Lieutenant?
- Is Chain of Command hidden somewhere?
- Is an Airborne attacker still off-table?
- Is the apparent gap in the defense actually covered by Hidden Deployment?

Each additional plausible explanation makes precise target priority harder.

The list-building goal is not to maximize secret rules individually.

It is to make them reinforce one another.

## 24. Markers can occupy space without attacking

A marker can control an area simply because the opponent cannot safely predict what happens if they approach it.

Useful marker positions include:

- beside objectives;
- behind corners;
- inside narrow approaches;
- near the edge of the enemy's preferred route;
- in places where a close-assault model would be dangerous;
- near your retreat route;
- between a DZ-strike attacker and your Order pool.

The marker forces a decision:

> **Do I spend Orders solving this, route around it, or accept the risk?**

All three outcomes can benefit you.

This is why a cheap marker protecting a doorway may be strategically successful even if it never fires.

Its job can simply be to make the doorway expensive.

## 25. Use markers to protect objectives

Objective defence is an especially strong role because the opponent cannot always ignore the marker.

A marker near a Console, Tech-Coffin, zone boundary, or other scoring location can force the enemy Specialist to solve the information problem before interacting safely.

Layer the position with:

- marker covering objective;
- Mine or deployable covering direct access;
- second marker covering the bypass;
- actual shooter covering the escape route;
- Hidden Deployment or Repeater support where available.

This converts a simple button push into a sequence of Order costs.

The defence does not need to kill the Specialist if it can make the scoring attempt arrive too late or consume too many Orders.

## 26. Coordinating Camo markers

Coordinated Orders can turn several scattered markers into a simultaneous positional problem.

A particularly useful application is coordinated movement rather than coordinated shooting.

For example:

> **Four Camo markers → Coordinated Move → several threatening forward positions.**

The reactive player may now need to decide:

- which marker to Discover;
- whether to Delay;
- which route is the real attack;
- whether a marker is a Specialist or decoy;
- whether clearing one marker exposes another route.

The primary payoff is **mass uncertainty and movement compression**.

One Command Token and Order can reposition several concealed threats into places that would otherwise require several separate activations.

This can be especially useful:

- after the opponent's main ARO piece is removed;
- late Turn 1 to establish midfield control;
- before Turn 2 attacks;
- when several Camo Specialists need to approach different objectives;
- when forcing the opponent to reveal which markers they actually fear.

Respect any current rules restrictions on specific marker-state skills, Decoy users, Spearhead interactions, and coordinated declarations.

## 27. Coordinated markers create a prioritization attack

The purpose of coordinating markers is not necessarily to make four attacks.

It can be to overload the opponent's decision capacity.

One marker asks:

> **What am I?**

Several moving together ask:

> **Which one matters, which one is real, and which one can I afford to ignore?**

This is particularly strong when different markers threaten different classes of target:

- one threatens an objective;
- one threatens the Lieutenant zone;
- one threatens an Engineer;
- one threatens a flank.

Even if only one becomes the actual attacker, the others may have already shaped the defensive response.

## 28. Sensor is the main anti-Camo system, not a universal anti-deception tool

Sensor is one of the most important counters to Camo-heavy and Hidden Deployment structures because it can solve several concealed elements efficiently within its area of effect.

This changes marker deployment fundamentally.

Against Sensor:

- do not cluster the entire marker network inside one Sensor activation area;
- spread important markers across different terrain pockets;
- force the Sensor carrier to expose itself to reach the ideal scan position;
- place Mines or other defenses on the route the Sensor model wants to take;
- consider killing the Sensor platform early if your defensive plan depends heavily on Camouflage;
- keep valuable models outside the Sensor platform's effective area when practical;
- make the opponent spend several Orders moving the Sensor piece before it can solve anything important.

A single Sensor REM may be worth attacking before a more expensive gunfighter if removing it dramatically increases the survival and information value of the rest of your marker network.

## 29. Attack the opponent's information-clearing pieces

Marker-heavy lists should identify anti-marker tools during deployment and target priority.

Potential priority targets include:

- Sensor platforms;
- MSV pieces that make Discover easier or bypass relevant concealment advantages;
- Biometric Visor models against Impersonation/Holo-related plans;
- fast troops capable of cheaply reaching multiple markers;
- template or Intuitive Attack platforms that can pressure markers without ordinary Discover sequences;
- cheap disposable troops that can safely probe mines.

Do not evaluate these targets only by their own offensive output.

Their real value may be that they let the rest of the enemy army interact efficiently with your marker network.

Therefore:

> **Kill the tool that makes all your other markers easier to solve.**

This is a form of dependency-chain targeting.

## 30. Anti-marker tools solve different problems

Do not treat Sensor, MSV, Biometric Visor, Intuitive Attack, templates, and disposable probes as interchangeable.

They attack different layers of deception.

### Sensor

Best against concentrated Camouflage/Hidden Deployment structures and for efficiently clearing uncertainty within its operating area.

### MSV

Useful against visual MODs and, depending on level/current rules, may greatly improve interaction with concealed targets. It is often a gunfighter/Discover enabler rather than a complete answer to every marker type.

### Biometric Visor

Particularly relevant against Impersonation and Holo-related threats. Identify these pieces before committing an Impersonator to a route that looks safe only against ordinary Discover.

### Intuitive Attack / templates

Useful for threatening some concealed targets without playing the normal gunfight, but the exact legal interaction depends on marker type and current N5.3 rules. Do not assume a tool that works against Camouflage also works against Impersonation.

### Disposable bodies

Sometimes the cheapest anti-marker tool is simply a low-value model that walks forward, triggers a Mine, forces a reveal, or occupies the marker's attention.

The practical lesson is:

> **Identify what type of deception the opponent is using before choosing the counter.**

## 31. Countering Sensor through spacing

Marker saturation is powerful only while the opponent must solve markers separately.

If one Sensor activation can meaningfully address several important markers, the network has become too concentrated.

When deploying marker-heavy forces:

- separate objective-defense clusters;
- put markers on opposite sides of large terrain pieces;
- avoid placing every midfield piece inside the same likely Sensor bubble;
- use elevation and walls to increase the Orders required for the Sensor model to reach the correct position;
- identify where a fast Sensor platform can be after one or two Orders, not merely where it begins.

Think about **future Sensor position**, not current Sensor position.

A Camo network that looks spread out from the DZ may become one dense cluster from the midfield.

## 32. Marker-state defence should have a second layer

Never rely on markers as the only defensive mechanism.

A good defence combines marker uncertainty with ordinary consequences:

- real ARO pieces;
- hacking zones;
- Mines;
- Perimeter weapons;
- direct templates;
- cheap screens;
- objective positioning;
- difficult movement geometry.

If the opponent solves the marker, there should ideally still be another problem behind it.

Example:

> **Discover Camo → kill Camo → enter Repeater → face Hacker → reach objective → trigger Mine.**

That is much stronger than:

> **Discover Camo → kill Camo → route is completely open.**

## 33. Do not Discover every enemy marker

When playing against marker-heavy forces, the opposite principle applies.

Do not allow the opponent to convert every marker into an Order sink.

Before Discovering, ask:

> **Does this marker actually need to be solved?**

Ignore or bypass markers that:

- are far from the mission;
- cannot realistically attack anything important;
- are not blocking your route;
- require several Orders to approach safely;
- can be screened by a disposable model;
- do not matter to the current turn's objective.

Prioritize markers that:

1. block the scoring route;
2. threaten the Lieutenant or Order pool;
3. directly protect an objective;
4. are likely to be important Specialists;
5. prevent safe repositioning;
6. enable another enemy plan.

The opponent wants you to spend six Orders clearing irrelevant uncertainty.

Do not cooperate.

## 34. Use cheap troops to probe uncertainty

A low-value model can often answer marker questions more efficiently than a premium attacker.

Possible jobs include:

- approaching suspected Mines;
- attempting Discover;
- entering a suspicious corner first;
- forcing an Impersonator to decide whether to attack;
- standing where the marker must reveal to pass;
- checking whether an apparently open route is protected by Hidden Deployment.

This is another reason cheap warbands, REMs, Peripheral bodies, and line troops can be strategically valuable even when their combat output is modest.

Do not expose a 50-point attacker to an unknown marker if a 7–12 point body can ask the same question.

## 35. Marker-state threat should change by turn

Marker value is not static across the game.

### Turn 1

Preserve uncertainty aggressively.

Markers often have maximum value because:

- both armies still have large Order pools;
- the opponent has not learned which markers are real threats;
- support pieces and Lieutenants are still protected in the DZ;
- the marker network can shape the first major attack route.

### Turn 2

Convert some uncertainty into attacks and scoring.

The opponent's anti-marker pieces may be dead or displaced, and markers can exploit openings created by Turn 1 combat.

### Turn 3

Mission value usually outweighs deception value.

A marker-state Specialist may need to reveal simply to score. A Hidden Deployment piece may need to appear to control a zone. An Impersonator may need to attack because there is no future turn in which preserving the threat matters.

A useful rule is:

> **Early game: information is a resource. Late game: spend the resource to score.**

## 36. Marker specialists should not trade like disposable attackers

A midfield Camo Specialist has two distinct values:

- combat/deception value now;
- future scoring value later.

Do not trade it for a mediocre target merely because Surprise Attack creates a favorable roll.

Ask:

> **Do I need this troop later?**

This is especially important in missions with:

- multiple Consoles;
- end-game scoring;
- Classifieds;
- zones that must be occupied late;
- objectives on opposite flanks.

A Specialist still concealed on Turn 3 may be worth far more than the 20-point target it could have killed on Turn 1.

## 37. Build marker networks, not isolated marker profiles

List construction should evaluate how marker pieces interact with one another.

A strong package might include:

- one or more Camo Specialists;
- Minelayers;
- Mines;
- a Hidden Deployment piece;
- an Impersonator;
- a Decoy profile;
- a HoloMask/Holoecho unit;
- ordinary troops that create Lieutenant ambiguity;
- anti-Sensor or anti-MSV attack capability.

The goal is not to maximize the number of special rules.

The goal is to create a battlefield in which **several different explanations remain plausible at once**.

This is stronger than taking one isolated premium Camo attacker in an otherwise completely transparent army.

## 38. Deception must survive public-information checks

A bluff that contradicts public information is not a bluff.

Before relying on deception, audit what the opponent is entitled to know.

Potential giveaways include:

- Silhouette size;
- marker MOD values;
- Hackable/Hacker status when it becomes relevant;
- visible Fireteam structure;
- impossible AVA/profile combinations;
- weapon or equipment information that becomes public under the rules;
- deployment positions that the imitated profile could not legally occupy.

The best deception is rules-consistent and mundane.

If the opponent has to use inference rather than a rules check to solve it, the deception is doing its job.

## 39. Make every marker tell a believable story

The most useful general principle from HoloMask, Decoy, Camo, and Impersonation play is the same:

> **Every false possibility should look like something you might genuinely have done.**

Examples:

- a fake Sniper belongs on a plausible Sniper rooftop;
- a fake Specialist belongs near a realistic objective route;
- a Decoy should occupy a position the real trooper could want;
- a Mine/Camo pair should both plausibly cover the same approach;
- an Impersonator should threaten targets worth protecting;
- a fake Lieutenant candidate should be deployed like a real Lieutenant candidate.

Randomness is not the same as deception.

Good deception creates **credible competing explanations**.

## 40. Practical marker-state decision tree

When activating a friendly marker, ask in order:

1. **Do I need to reveal at all?**
2. **Can movement improve my position while preserving the marker?**
3. **Can I force the opponent to choose Discover versus Delay?**
4. **How many enemy models can currently participate in Discover?**
5. **Is there Sensor, MSV, Biometric Visor, Intuitive Attack, or another relevant counter nearby?**
6. **If I reveal, what important information am I giving away?**
7. **If I attack, what does killing this target actually accomplish?**
8. **Can I return to a marker state afterward?**
9. **Do I need this model for scoring later?**
10. **Would simply occupying this position cause more problems than attacking?**

When facing an enemy marker, ask:

1. **Does this marker matter to my current plan?**
2. **Can I bypass it?**
3. **Can a cheap model probe it?**
4. **Can Sensor or another specialized counter solve several markers at once?**
5. **Am I walking my premium attacker into exactly the uncertainty trap the opponent wants?**
6. **What happens if I ignore it for one turn?**

## 41. List-building checklist for marker/deception packages

When evaluating a list, check whether it has:

- enough marker density to create uncertainty rather than one obvious threat;
- multiple plausible marker identities;
- Mine/Minelayer support;
- mission-relevant Camo Specialists;
- Hidden Deployment where useful;
- Impersonation or Holo tools where available;
- plausible Lieutenant ambiguity;
- ways to kill Sensor/MSV/Biometric counter-pieces;
- spacing that prevents one Sensor action from clearing the whole midfield;
- a second defensive layer after the markers are solved;
- enough ordinary combat power that the opponent cannot spend the whole game calmly Discovering markers;
- a plan for converting preserved markers into Turn-2/Turn-3 scoring.

A marker-heavy list should not merely contain many markers.

It should force the opponent to repeatedly make **expensive decisions with incomplete information**.

## 42. Core heuristics

The entire strategy can be condensed to the following rules:

1. **Preserve the marker unless revealing accomplishes something important.**
2. **Make the opponent pay Orders to obtain information.**
3. **Use movement to force Discover-versus-Delay decisions before committing.**
4. **Expose important markers to as few simultaneous Discoverers as possible.**
5. **Re-Camouflage after profitable attack or scoring runs when the model still has future value.**
6. **Create marker saturation so the opponent must choose what information is worth buying.**
7. **Mix real threats, Mines, Specialists, Decoys, and hidden pieces so ignoring everything is unsafe.**
8. **Make Decoy and Holo positions plausible; deception fails when one option is obviously correct.**
9. **Use Impersonators to distort the enemy deployment, not merely to collect one kill.**
10. **Treat Hidden Deployment as control over apparently empty space.**
11. **Attack Sensor and other information-clearing pieces when they enable the opponent's entire counter-plan.**
12. **Use specialized anti-marker tools against the marker type they actually solve.**
13. **Against marker spam, do not Discover anything that does not matter to the mission or attack route.**
14. **Early game, preserve uncertainty; late game, spend it to score.**
15. **A marker that never attacks can still win Orders, space, and the mission.**

## 43. Video-research takeaways

The most useful strategic themes from current and recent Infinity video material were:

- marker saturation produces a **prioritization problem**, not merely several separate Discover rolls;
- multiple enemy Discoverers weaken the marker tax, so marker placement should use the same kind of geometry as ARO slicing;
- first-Short-Skill movement is often an information-gathering tool before the marker commits;
- Sensor, MSV, Biometric Visor, and Intuitive Attack are different counters and should not be treated as one generic anti-Camo category;
- Holoechoes have practical value as probes against deployable threats, not merely as fake targets;
- HoloMask is strongest when the disguise supports a believable Lieutenant/deployment story;
- Decoy and Camo spam work best when combined with real Mines and mission pressure;
- Hidden Deployment makes apparently empty space part of the deception game;
- Impersonators can generate more value through persistent threat and deployment distortion than through an immediate low-value assassination.
