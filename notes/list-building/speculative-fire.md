# List-Building Strategy — Speculative Fire

**Cross-faction unit decisions (23 Oct 2026):** [Speculative Fire and Spotlight → Guided — N5.3 Apex and Points-Value Tier Guide](indirect-fire-speculative-guided-apex-value.md) compares exact Kuryer/Traktor/Haetae/Vertigo/Son-Bae/Samekh/Redeye/Jackal profiles, **when Speculative beats Guided on Orders**, and MCP-reviewed opposing opinions. Keep **Spotlight's AP/PS5 Saving Roll, weapon Range MODs, Reset/Dodge and FTO-vs-Guided profile distinctions** when applying older examples.

Speculative Fire is best evaluated as an **Order-efficiency and positional-pressure tool**, not simply as a low-accuracy way to deal damage.

The core question is:

> **Does the speculative attack save more Orders, risk, or positional cost than its B1 / -6 attack penalty is likely to waste?**

Under the current rules baseline, Speculative Attack is a Long Skill using a weapon with the Speculative Attack Trait. The attack is always B1, applies the Speculative Attack -6 MOD plus Range MODs, and does not apply other negative MODs such as Mimetism, Partial Cover, or Visibility Zones. With a Circular Impact Template, the impact point may be placed away from the Main Target as long as the Main Target remains inside the Area of Effect.

## 1. Skip the normal attack sequence

The main advantage is that Speculative Fire can bypass setup that would otherwise consume several Orders.

A conventional attack may require:

1. moving into position;
2. clearing or bypassing an ARO;
3. establishing LoF;
4. fighting the target;
5. retreating or repositioning afterward.

Speculative Fire may instead attack from the current safe position.

This is especially useful against:

- troops completely behind walls;
- prone rooftop pieces;
- protected support troops;
- models behind dangerous ARO lanes;
- targets whose location is useful but difficult to approach;
- models protected by strong Mimetism/Cover stacks.

A useful rule is:

> **Speculative Fire becomes attractive when the alternative requires multiple setup Orders or exposes a valuable attacker to meaningful retaliation.**

## 2. Positional pressure and forced response

A credible speculative attacker can create a **lose-lose positional problem** for an enemy hiding in total cover.

The opponent can:

- **stay in place** and continue absorbing safe indirect attacks;
- **move away**, spending Orders and abandoning a useful position;
- **come out and remove the speculative attacker**, exposing the target or another attack piece to conventional fire, AROs, hacking, CC threats, or other prepared defenses.

This means Speculative Fire can be valuable even before it causes a wound.

A durable or aggressive troop with Grenades is a good example. A Dog Warrior that reaches a protected position near an important enemy can repeatedly threaten that enemy from outside LoF. The target cannot simply remain safely hidden forever without accepting repeated attack rolls. Dealing with the Dog Warrior may require leaving cover or spending several Orders to approach it from another route.

This is especially powerful when the enemy is occupying a position it does not want to surrender, such as:

- an objective;
- a strong rooftop or firing position;
- a protected Lieutenant/NCO/Chain of Command position;
- a Fireteam staging point;
- a Hacker or support piece that needs to remain near another unit;
- a defensive piece whose value depends on remaining prone or hidden.

The key principle is:

> **Speculative pressure forces the opponent to choose between tolerating repeated safe attacks, spending Orders relocating, or exposing something to eliminate the threat.**

This makes durable, mobile speculative attackers particularly strong pressure pieces. Their value is not just the attack roll; it is the cost the opponent must pay to make the attacks stop.

## 3. Residual-Order conversion

Long-range Speculative Fire is one of the best ways to make otherwise stranded Orders productive.

A Combat Group often reaches the end of a turn with only one or two Orders remaining. That may be insufficient to begin a normal attack run because a conventional attack could require movement, setup, engagement, and retreat.

A speculative shooter already in position may require **no setup at all**.

Therefore:

> **Residual-Order conversion = turning one or two Orders that might otherwise expire unused into immediate offensive pressure.**

This is particularly valuable in **Group 2**.

Examples:

- the Group 2 specialist completed its objective faster than expected;
- the Engineer no longer needs to repair anything;
- the group's main secondary attacker cannot safely begin a full attack run with only two Orders;
- an Irregular or Tactical Order has no better immediate use;
- the group has completed its support tasks but still has one or two Regular Orders available.

A long-range speculative weapon can convert those remaining Orders into:

- a chance to inflict damage;
- forced Dodge rolls;
- pressure on a protected model;
- disruption of a Fireteam or support package;
- positional pressure that may cost the opponent Orders next turn.

This changes the value of low-percentage speculative attacks. A 30–40% attack may be unattractive if it consumes Orders needed by the main attack plan, but it can be completely reasonable if the alternative is allowing the Order to expire unused.

### Primary vs secondary speculative attackers

A **primary speculative attacker** needs strong reliability because the list expects to spend several Orders through it. Look for:

- high attack Attribute;
- good Range bands;
- +BS or equivalent bonuses;
- +SD;
- useful ammunition;
- strong template effects;
- a safe firing position;
- enough durability or mobility to maintain pressure.

A **secondary/residual-Order speculative attacker** has a lower threshold. It mainly needs:

- enough range to have plausible targets;
- a reasonable attack value;
- preferably another useful role in the list;
- placement in a Combat Group likely to end turns with one or two spare Orders.

For the secondary role, Speculative Fire is valuable because it has a very low **minimum activation threshold**: one spare Order can immediately become an attack.

## 4. Punish defensive MOD stacks

Speculative Attack applies the -6 Speculative MOD and Range MODs, but does not apply other negative MODs such as Mimetism, Partial Cover, or Visibility Zones.

This can make it attractive even when LoF could eventually be established.

For example, a target with Mimetism (-6) in Cover would normally impose a very large shooting penalty. Speculative Fire replaces those defensive MODs with the fixed Speculative penalty plus Range.

Therefore:

> **Mimetism-heavy targets in Cover are especially good speculative targets when the normal attack would suffer equal or worse MODs while also risking return fire.**

The tradeoff remains B1, so this is strongest when the speculative attacker also has a strong Attribute, good Range band, +SD, or a particularly valuable ammunition effect.

## 5. Cluster hunting

Circular speculative attacks become much more efficient when several enemy models can be caught under one template.

Good targets include:

- clustered Fireteams;
- several prone troops on one rooftop;
- cheerleaders packed behind the same wall;
- Doctor + patient;
- Engineer + TAG/REM;
- Lieutenant package + bodyguards;
- several specialists around an objective.

Each affected model must deal with the template independently. One successful Dodge does not protect the other affected troops.

This changes the expected value dramatically:

> **A mediocre B1 attack against one model may be inefficient; the same roll threatening two, three, or four models can be excellent.**

Speculative-capable templates therefore exert deployment pressure even if they never fire. An opponent who respects them must spread troops out, use less efficient cover positions, or separate support pieces from the models they support.

## 6. Optimize the impact point

For Circular Impact Templates, do not automatically center the template on the Main Target.

The impact point can be shifted as long as the Main Target remains inside the Area of Effect.

Use that freedom to optimize three things simultaneously:

1. keep the Main Target affected;
2. catch additional models;
3. improve the Range band to the impact point where possible.

Before rolling, examine the entire legal template-placement area.

> **The best impact point is the one that maximizes both template value and attack reliability, not necessarily the point directly under the target.**

## 7. Reliability matters more at B1

Because Speculative Attack is always B1, every improvement to the attack number matters disproportionately.

Strong speculative platforms may benefit from:

- high PH for Grenades;
- high BS for launchers and other BS-based weapons;
- favorable Range bands;
- +BS;
- +SD;
- Fireteam bonuses;
- X-Visor or other range-improving effects where applicable.

Raw +Burst does not solve Speculative Fire because the attack remains B1.

Short-range Grenades illustrate the importance of Range bands: a grenade thrown in its positive band may only be fighting the Speculative penalty after the range bonus, while the same grenade farther away can become extremely unreliable once a negative Range MOD is added.

Therefore:

> **Do not evaluate a speculative weapon only by its maximum range. Evaluate the actual target numbers it produces at the distances the model can realistically reach.**

## 8. Fireteam interaction

Fireteam bonuses can turn an otherwise mediocre speculative profile into a credible indirect-fire platform.

Relevant benefits are those that improve the B1 roll itself, especially:

- +BS;
- +SD;
- movement compression that brings the launcher into a better range band while moving the rest of the team.

This makes speculative capability worth checking when evaluating Fireteam profiles. A launcher that is only a secondary weapon solo may become an important safe attack option once linked.

A useful profile-evaluation category is:

### Speculative-fire quality

1. Attribute used;
2. Range bands;
3. +BS or equivalent bonuses;
4. +SD;
5. template size/effect;
6. ammunition quality;
7. Fireteam access;
8. ability to remain safe while firing;
9. whether the model has another useful role when Speculative Fire is unnecessary.

## 9. Target support structure, not only expensive attackers

The best speculative target is often not the most expensive enemy model.

Indirect fire can reach support pieces that are deliberately hidden from conventional shooting, including:

- Doctor;
- Engineer;
- Hacker;
- Lieutenant candidate;
- NCO;
- Chain of Command;
- FT Master;
- TinBot carrier;
- Fireteam glue;
- objective specialist;
- Repeater/network support;
- cheap Order generators.

Removing a support model can create a larger downstream effect than damaging the visible attacker.

Examples:

- kill the Engineer supporting a TAG, then attack the TAG later;
- remove FT Master and damage both Fireteam integrity and future Order generation;
- remove the NCO and strand Lieutenant Orders;
- kill the Doctor before attacking a multi-Wound piece;
- remove the specialist that must remain near a late-game objective.

This is especially important because these pieces are often cheap enough that opponents hide them rather than exposing them to normal gunfights.

## 10. Speculative Fire as an anti-marker / anti-hiding tool

Speculative Fire can reduce the normal Order tax imposed by hidden or protected positions.

Instead of spending several Orders on approach, Discover, repositioning, and attack, a suitable speculative weapon may be able to threaten the position directly.

This is particularly useful against static defensive models whose protection comes more from **not being directly engageable** than from raw durability.

It should be considered alongside other obstacle-clearing tools such as:

- Sensor;
- Intuitive Attack;
- templates;
- MSV;
- coordinated Discover;
- fast close-assault pieces.

The right choice is whichever solves the position with the lowest expected Order and risk cost.

## 11. Ammunition and utility effects

Speculative Fire should not be evaluated only by expected wounds.

A speculative hit carrying a strong disabling or utility effect may be worth much more than a normal-damage hit.

Depending on the weapon available, valuable effects can include:

- E/M or other disabling ammunition;
- strong anti-armour ammunition;
- continuous or persistent damage effects;
- vision-control effects;
- other template-delivered state or area-control effects.

A low-probability speculative attack can be worthwhile when one successful hit dramatically changes the target's usefulness.

Therefore:

> **Evaluate speculative ammunition by the value of one successful hit, not just by raw damage output.**

## 12. Group 2 role

Speculative-capable units are particularly attractive Group 2 candidates when they also provide another low-order role.

Good combinations include:

- warband + Grenades + Smoke;
- specialist + launcher;
- Fireteam member + speculative weapon;
- defensive piece that can fire indirectly when no reactive work is needed;
- durable secondary attacker with a long-range speculative option.

The ideal Group 2 speculative piece is **order-elastic**:

- with zero Orders, it still contributes passively or reactively;
- with one Order, it can immediately make a speculative attack;
- with two or three Orders, it can apply repeated pressure;
- with a larger surplus, it may become a genuine secondary attack piece.

This fits the broader Group 2 principle that the group should remain useful with few Orders while still having somewhere productive to put unexpected surplus Orders.

## 13. When not to speculate

A safe attack is not automatically an efficient attack.

Avoid falling into the trap of spending several premium Group 1 Orders on extremely low-percentage B1 shots simply because the attacker cannot be shot back.

Speculative Fire is weakest when:

- the hit number is extremely poor;
- only one low-value target is affected;
- a conventional attacker can solve the problem more reliably in the same number of Orders;
- the Orders are needed for mission scoring or a more important attack;
- the shooter must first spend several Orders moving into speculative range;
- the weapon is Disposable and repeated misses exhaust a scarce resource;
- the attack accomplishes nothing strategically even if it lands.

The correct comparison is not "Can I make this attack safely?" but:

> **What else could this Order accomplish?**

## Practical decision rule

Speculative Fire is especially attractive when at least one of the following is true:

- the template threatens multiple models;
- the attack bypasses one or more setup Orders;
- it avoids a dangerous ARO or return-fire situation;
- it attacks through a severe Mimetism/Cover/Visibility stack;
- it threatens a high-value support or command model that is otherwise protected;
- one successful disabling hit has very high value;
- it creates positional pressure on an enemy that wants to remain hidden;
- it uses a residual Order that would otherwise be difficult or impossible to spend productively.

The strongest cases combine several of these at once.

## List-building shorthand

> **Speculative pressure:** force protected enemies to absorb repeated indirect attacks, relocate, or expose themselves to remove the shooter.

> **Residual-Order conversion:** use long-range Speculative Fire to turn isolated or surplus Orders—especially the final one or two Orders in Group 2—into productive offensive attempts rather than letting them expire.

> **Speculative efficiency:** use indirect fire when the Orders and risk saved by bypassing normal setup are worth more than the accuracy lost to B1 and the Speculative penalty.
