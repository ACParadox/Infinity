# List-Building Strategy — Trading and Attrition

## Current N5.3 airborne / Enemy-DZ attacker profile tiers (2026-10-23)

The [N5.3 airborne/DZ-striker apex and value guide](airborne-and-enemy-dz-attackers-apex-value.md) adds an **expected-trade decision framework**: multiply successful **legal delivery** by *conditional* win/kill chance, then count removed future Orders, LT/CoC protection, specialist/Engineer/EVO infrastructure and mission score against attacker cost, attack/escape Orders and failed insertion tempo. It explicitly distinguishes **Fiday23/Rasyat25/Van Zant33** from **Mirage60/Caskuda64**, and *does not invent* universal unit kill probabilities.


Trading in Infinity should not be evaluated only as **points killed versus points lost**. A model's printed points value is only one part of its practical value on the table.

A better question is:

> **What future game value did I remove, and what future game value did I give up to do it?**

Useful value can include:

- points;
- Regular Orders;
- Lieutenant or Tactical Orders;
- Specialist capability;
- Fireteam integrity;
- support and recovery;
- hacking or vision-control infrastructure;
- board control;
- mission access;
- wounds/STR remaining;
- future turns of useful work.

A useful shorthand is:

> **Trade value ≈ enemy capability removed + Orders denied + mission swing + positional gain − your future capability lost − Orders/risk spent.**

This is not exact arithmetic. It is a framework for deciding whether a sacrifice actually improves the game state.

## 1. A 10-point model dying to kill 25 points

A cheap model dying to kill a more expensive model is usually attractive, but **points ratio alone is not enough**.

A 10-point warband killing a 25-point gunfighter can be an excellent trade because the cheap troop converts itself into:

- 25 enemy points removed;
- one enemy Order removed if the target is Regular;
- less enemy combat capability;
- possibly a safer lane or weaker Reactive Turn.

But the same nominal 10-for-25 exchange can be much better or much worse depending on what each model still needs to do.

### Better than it looks

A cheap troop may be trading into something whose strategic value is much higher than its points:

- the opponent's only Engineer;
- the only Specialist capable of reaching a key objective;
- a Fireteam member whose death breaks an important bonus;
- an NCO that turns Lieutenant Orders into productive activations;
- a Smoke carrier enabling the main gunfighter;
- a Repeater/Pitcher carrier enabling the hacking plan;
- a Doctor supporting an expensive attack piece.

### Worse than it looks

A cheap troop can also be too important to sacrifice.

That 10-point model may be:

- your only Smoke or Eclipse;
- your only nearby Specialist;
- protecting your Lieutenant;
- generating an Order you need for two more turns;
- the only model guarding your DZ against Airborne Deployment;
- necessary to maintain a Fireteam bonus.

Therefore:

> **Do not ask only "Did I trade up?" Ask "Which model had more useful work remaining?"**

## 2. Orders can matter more than points

A cheap Regular troop is not merely a cheap body. Early in the game it may represent:

> **body + board role + one Regular Order on every remaining Turn.**

That can make killing exposed Order generators more damaging than wounding an expensive centerpiece.

For example, removing three cheap Regular troops may reduce the enemy's ability to:

- deliver a TAG or HI attacker;
- repeat failed attacks;
- score afterward;
- retreat after an attack run;
- rebuild a Fireteam;
- recover casualties;
- reposition for the Reactive Turn.

The expensive enemy model may still be alive but unable to receive enough Orders to matter.

### Order-removal breakpoints

Order attrition becomes especially powerful near functional breakpoints.

Dropping a group from ten Orders to nine is useful.

Dropping it from six to five or four can be devastating if the enemy's intended sequence requires:

> **approach → clear ARO → attack → score → exit.**

At that point the attack piece may remain on the table while becoming operationally stranded.

> **Sometimes the best way to neutralize an expensive model is to kill the cheap models that power it.**

## 3. Trade into enablers, not only centerpieces

Many premium pieces function as packages rather than isolated models.

If a cheap disposable attacker can remove the part of the package that makes the premium piece efficient, the trade can have outsized value.

### TAG + Engineer

Trading a warband or infiltrator for an Engineer supporting a TAG is often excellent even if the Engineer itself is cheap.

The Engineer's practical value includes:

- its own Order;
- Specialist capability;
- clearing disabling states;
- restoring STR;
- making previous damage reversible.

Once the Engineer is gone, later damage to the TAG is more likely to remain permanent.

A strong sequence can therefore be:

> **kill support → damage centerpiece → stop.**

rather than repeatedly attacking a recoverable TAG while its support remains intact.

### Hacking packages

The same logic applies to:

- Hacker + Repeater network;
- Pitcher carrier + Hacker;
- EVO + Airborne package;
- TinBot + HI Fireteam;
- Smoke carrier + MSV gunfighter;
- NCO + Lieutenant Orders.

Attack whichever link is cheapest to remove **and actually breaks the package**.

## 4. Sacrifice models to break Fireteams

A trade can be good even when you lose more points than you destroy if the casualty damages the whole Fireteam.

Useful targets can include:

- FT Master;
- TinBot carrier;
- Specialist;
- Doctor/Engineer;
- Smoke or White-Noise support;
- close-defense piece;
- premium weapon;
- member required to maintain an important Fireteam bonus;
- member whose death makes rebuilding awkward or impossible.

If a 15-point disposable attacker dies killing a 12-point Fireteam member but removes an important bonus from the remaining team, the real exchange was not simply 15 for 12.

It was closer to:

> **15 points → 12 points + degraded Fireteam effectiveness.**

This makes templates, Mines, E/M, Berserk, close-combat attacks, and other sacrificial assault tools especially useful when they can cause **structural damage** rather than merely remove points.

## 5. Preserve your own Order generators

Cheap does not automatically mean expendable.

Early in the game, a cheap Regular troop may still generate Orders for multiple future turns. Throwing it away for a marginal attack can damage your own economy much more than its points suggest.

Protect cheap Regular troops when their future Orders matter, especially:

- cheerleaders;
- cheap Fireteam filler;
- Flash Pulse REMs;
- Baggage REMs;
- cheap Specialists;
- support troops that can still contribute later.

Conversely, an enemy attacker that trades itself for several such troops may have made an excellent attack even if its own points cost was significant.

> **Early-game Order generators are economic assets, not merely cheap bodies.**

## 6. Trading changes by turn

The value of survival declines as the game progresses because there are fewer future Orders and fewer future jobs left to perform.

### Turn 1 — future value dominates

A surviving model may still:

- generate Orders for several turns;
- score later;
- attack later;
- heal or repair later;
- defend several enemy turns.

Turn-1 trades should therefore heavily weight **future economic value**.

Particularly attractive early targets include:

- Regular Order generators;
- command infrastructure;
- Engineers and Doctors;
- attack enablers;
- primary Specialists;
- Fireteam infrastructure.

Preserve your own equivalents unless the payoff is substantial.

### Turn 2 — preserve what the endgame needs

Turn 2 is often the transition between attrition and endgame preparation.

Ask:

> **What does each army need alive for Turn 3?**

Then attack those pieces.

This often means prioritizing:

- fast Specialists;
- midfield objective holders;
- the attacker that can clear the final objective;
- Engineers or Doctors supporting premium pieces;
- Order generators necessary for the final attack run.

### Turn 3 — cash in almost everything

Future Order generation has little or no remaining value.

A model that dies after performing its final useful task may effectively cost nothing strategically.

Examples:

- Specialist runs through an ARO to push the winning button;
- warband Berserks a model controlling a zone;
- TAG accepts a dangerous fight to remove the last enemy Specialist;
- NWI troop fights until it drops;
- cheap Regular troop leaves safety to Contest a scoring zone.

Turn 3 changes the central question from:

> **Will this model survive?**

into:

> **Can this model convert its remaining existence into Victory Points?**

## 7. Wounds and STR are resources

Durability was paid for and should be used when doing so produces greater value.

A multi-Wound or multi-STR troop does not need to end the game undamaged for its durability to have been useful.

A Wound can be spent to buy:

- crossing a dangerous lane;
- saving movement Orders;
- another attack;
- reaching an objective;
- entering template range;
- forcing a key enemy reveal;
- reaching a valuable support model.

Sometimes it is more efficient to accept a reasonable chance of taking one Wound than to spend several Orders eliminating every minor risk first.

> **Spend durability when it buys more Orders, position, capability, or VP than the lost Wound is worth.**

However, distinguish expendable durability from critical durability.

A TAG going from full STR to damaged-but-functional may still be an acceptable cost.

A TAG dropping to its final functional STR immediately before the opponent activates strong anti-TAG tools may sharply reduce its future freedom.

## 8. Do not waste premium durability either

The opposite mistake is assuming that a durable model should accept every risk merely because it can survive one failure.

A Wound on an important HI or TAG can reduce its ability to:

- challenge later AROs;
- cross Mines or templates;
- enter hostile Repeater coverage;
- survive a bad Face-to-Face Roll;
- defend effectively in the Reactive Turn.

The correct principle is not "tanks should tank."

It is:

> **Spend durability when the gain exceeds the reduction in future freedom.**

## 9. Dogged and NWI change the trade calculation

Dogged and NWI make some troops particularly good at converting a nearly-spent life into additional work.

Once a model's long-term survival value has collapsed, prioritize the work it can still perform now.

Possible final jobs include:

- push an objective;
- kill another model;
- move deeper into the enemy position;
- Engage something valuable;
- force another ARO;
- place a Mine or Repeater;
- control a scoring zone;
- block a route.

Do not automatically spend Orders retreating or preserving a model whose remaining strategic value lies in **doing one more useful thing before it disappears**.

A useful rule is:

> **Once survival stops being valuable, convert the remaining model into damage, position, Orders denied, or VP.**

## 10. Ask: "Do I need this troop later?"

This is one of the best pre-trade questions in Infinity.

Before intentionally exposing a model, ask whether it is still needed for:

- scoring;
- Order generation;
- Lieutenant security;
- Smoke/Eclipse;
- engineering/healing;
- hacking;
- Fireteam integrity;
- DZ defense;
- anti-TAG or anti-HI duty;
- defending an objective;
- Discover/Sensor work;
- solving a matchup that few other troops can handle.

A practical classification is:

| Model status | Trading attitude |
| --- | --- |
| **Essential later** | Protect aggressively |
| **Useful later** | Trade only for meaningful advantage |
| **Redundant** | Trade relatively freely |
| **Job completed** | Strong candidate for sacrifice |
| **Likely to die next turn anyway** | Cash it in now if possible |
| **Cannot realistically contribute later** | Convert immediately into damage, position, or VP |

This can change during the game.

A Turn-1 Specialist may be irreplaceable.

The same Specialist after completing the final objective on Turn 3 may become fully expendable.

## 11. Opportunity cost determines whether a trade is efficient

A cheap model killing something expensive can still be a bad exchange if the attack consumed too many Orders.

Example:

An 8-point warband kills a 35-point model but requires six Regular Orders to reach it.

That looks excellent in points but those six Orders might instead have:

- scored two objectives;
- killed a mission-critical Specialist;
- positioned the main Fireteam;
- established defenses;
- enabled another attack with much higher strategic value.

Therefore evaluate:

> **piece spent + Orders spent + resulting position**

not merely:

> **your points lost versus enemy points killed.**

A modest 15-point kill achieved in one or two Orders can be strategically better than an impressive 40-point kill that consumes half the turn.

## 12. Force the opponent to spend Orders killing cheap models

Trading and attrition also happen in the Reactive Turn.

A cheap model can trade well simply by forcing several enemy Orders before dying.

For example, a 7-point troop might force:

- movement into position;
- Discover;
- an attack;
- a second attack after a failed roll;
- Doctor/Engineer recovery;
- repositioning afterward.

The troop may die without killing anything while still having damaged the opponent's turn.

Strong Order-tax pieces include:

- Camo markers;
- Mines;
- warbands around corners;
- cheap ARO troops;
- Repeaters;
- cheap Engage threats;
- disposable midfield blockers.

> **A cheap model that consumes three enemy Orders may have traded better than a more expensive ARO piece that dies immediately.**

## 13. Some of the best trades do not kill anything

Trading should include **tempo, position, states, and forced actions**, not only casualties.

Examples:

### Engage a valuable shooter

A cheap troop Engages a premium gunfighter.

Even if the cheap troop never kills it, the opponent may need to spend several Orders escaping or solving the combat before the gunfighter can perform its intended role.

### Deliver a Repeater

A cheap Repeater carrier enters the area around an enemy HI or TAG and dies.

If the Repeater remains useful or the carrier has already projected the hacking threat, its job may be complete.

### Force defensive AROs

A disposable troop forces a Fireteam or valuable defender to Dodge or reposition while another model exploits the resulting geometry.

### Block movement

A cheap body occupying a doorway, ladder, narrow route, or objective approach can force movement or attacks that would otherwise be unnecessary.

Therefore attrition includes:

> **points + Orders + actions + position + future options.**

## 14. Trade differently when ahead or behind

Game state changes the amount of risk worth accepting.

### When ahead

If you are already ahead on:

- mission score;
- Orders;
- board position;
- Specialists;
- remaining attack pieces;

there is often little need to accept volatile trades.

Preserve the position and force the opponent to solve it.

### When behind

If the current board state will lose the game, merely preserving your remaining models preserves the losing position.

Higher-variance plays become more reasonable:

- Berserk;
- close-combat runs;
- risky Airborne Deployment;
- template trades;
- aggressive Combat Jump;
- speculative attacks;
- sacrificing an expensive attacker for a decisive target.

A useful principle is:

> **Ahead → reduce variance. Behind → accept more variance when it can reverse the game.**

## 15. Prefer asymmetric trades

The best exchanges are often ones where your model was designed to be disposable but the enemy model was not.

Typical asymmetric traders include:

- cheap warbands;
- Impetuous troops;
- disposable infiltrators;
- Airborne attackers after completing their run;
- Dogged/NWI assault pieces;
- Peripherals;
- cheap template carriers;
- Mines and other Deployables.

Good targets include:

- premium attackers;
- Engineers/Doctors;
- Specialists;
- Fireteam infrastructure;
- command pieces;
- Repeaters/Pitcher carriers;
- Orders that are difficult to replace.

The objective is not merely to trade **up in points**.

It is to trade a **low-future-value resource** into a **high-future-value resource**.

## 16. Stop trading when the marginal value collapses

A successful attack run can create a temptation to continue because the attacker is already deep in enemy territory.

Reassess after every significant exchange.

Ask:

1. What useful enemy capability remains nearby?
2. How many Orders will the next attack take?
3. What is the chance of success?
4. What happens if the attacker dies now?
5. Do I still need Orders for scoring or retreat?
6. Has this troop already completed its main job?

The first two trades in an attack run may be excellent while the third is poor.

Do not let sunk cost turn a successful attack into an overextension.

## 17. Practical trade checklist

Before committing to a deliberate sacrifice, ask:

1. **What exactly disappears if this works?**
2. **How many future Orders disappear?**
3. **Does the target break a package, Fireteam, network, or mission plan?**
4. **How many Orders will my attempt consume?**
5. **What happens to my attacker afterward?**
6. **What future role am I giving up?**
7. **Do I need this troop later?**
8. **Which Turn is it?**
9. **Can a cheaper model make the same trade?**
10. **Does the trade improve the mission position?**

A compact version is:

> **Trade cheap bodies for expensive capabilities.**  
> **Trade early for future Orders.**  
> **Trade support pieces to cripple expensive platforms.**  
> **Preserve models whose future role matters.**  
> **Spend Wounds when they buy Orders, position, or VP.**  
> **By Turn 3, cash in anything whose death no longer matters.**

The central principle is:

> **A model's real value is the useful work it can still perform, not merely the points printed beside its name.**
