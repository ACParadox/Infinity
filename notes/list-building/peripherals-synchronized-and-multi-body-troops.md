# Peripherals, Synchronized Models, and Multi-Body Troops

**Concrete verified double-FO exception:** [Drummer + Drumbot-5](https://www.human-sphere.com/Drummers) **21/0**, with *both* controller and synchronized bot possessing **Forward Observer**, is unusually effective at two distinct legal FO attack origins on one Order, because [N5.3 Peripheral](https://infinitythewiki.com/Peripheral) requires the **same declared Short Skill** but **not the same target**. Keep Coherency; a 2-model package still yields one Regular Order. **Sirius FO+SiriusBot** and **Coyote FO+attached BambaBot** do **not** inherit bot FO by association; Coyote's **BambaDroid FO** is a separate dependent REM.

**Current N5.3 rare-combination tiers:** [Rare Abilities & Unusual Combinations — apex/value and opponent matchups](rare-capabilities-and-unique-combinations-apex-value.md). Separates **Posthuman G:Jumper 2–3 Proxies/one Order**, N5.3 autonomous Cyberplug versus Synchronized/Control, and **controller-only FO** from a truly independent second FO unit; includes current exact package costs and new transcript debates.

Multi-body troops are a distinct Infinity subsystem because they change the normal relationship between **Orders, Combat Group slots, physical bodies, threat projection, and risk**.

For **Servant and Cyberplug recovery reliability versus full package cost**, see [Doctor/Engineer apex and value tiers](doctors-engineers-and-recovery-apex-value.md); this guide remains responsible for multi-body activation and placement mechanics.

The central idea is:

> **Multi-body value comes from having more physical presence, positional reach, or tactical options than the unit's Order and Combat Group footprint would normally suggest.**

However, not all multi-body mechanics work the same way.

- **Synchronized and Control Peripherals** multiply what happens during an activation.
- **Servants** project a Doctor or Engineer's location-dependent Skill to another body.
- **Cyberplugs** combine connected remote control with autonomous bodies under the current N5.3 rules.
- **G: Jumper** does not activate several bodies at once; instead it distributes one Trooper's useful presence across several pre-positioned bodies.

Treating all of these as merely "extra models" misses most of their strategic value.

Current rules baseline: **N5.3 + N5 FAQ v0.1**. See `../../rules/README.md`, `../../rules/5b_Special Skills/Peripheral.md`, `../../rules/5b_Special Skills/Peripheral_N5.3_Update.md`, and `../../rules/5b_Special Skills/G__Jumper.md` for authoritative rules text.

---

# 1. First distinguish the architectures

| Architecture | What one Order provides | Main strategic value | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peripheral (Synchronized)** | Controller + Synchronized Peripheral(s) activate together and declare the same Skills | activation compression, multiple angles, ARO-choice pressure | must maintain Coherency with Controller |
| **Peripheral (Control)** | Controller + Control Unit activate; one Peripheral is the Spearhead for targeted actions | remote assault pack, expendable successive bodies | targeted Skills are concentrated through Controller + Spearhead |
| **Peripheral (Servant)** | Controller + one selected Servant activate | remote Doctor/Engineer reach | primarily support; two Servants cannot both be active at once |
| **Peripheral (Ancillary)** | deployable secondary body tied to Controller | delayed remote body / utility projection | must be deployed and interacts with Fireteam eligibility |
| **Peripheral (Cyberplug)** | Connected body acts through Controller, Autonomous bodies can act independently | distributed remote presence with reduced controller dependence | mode management; Connected Controller normally Idles |
| **G: Jumper / Proxies** | choose one Proxy to activate | several useful positions sharing one Trooper identity | no simultaneous Active-Turn multi-body action |

The first strategic question is therefore:

> **Am I compressing several actions into one Order, projecting a Skill remotely, or choosing among several pre-positioned bodies?**

That determines how the unit should be moved, protected, and valued.

---

# 2. Multi-body pieces create slot efficiency, not necessarily more Orders

A Controller and its Peripherals count as one Trooper for Combat Group limits. G: Jumper likewise occupies one Trooper slot and provides one Order despite placing several Proxy models on the table.

This creates **slot efficiency**.

A list entry can represent:

- several physical bases;
- several deployment locations;
- several ARO locations;
- several weapon ranges;
- a support specialist plus remote recovery body;
- or several role-specific Proxies.

This is different from Order density.

A Peripheral package may generate only one Regular Order, yet physically occupy two or three useful places. A G: Jumper package may similarly generate one Order while placing several substantial bodies across the table.

Therefore evaluate:

> **Order density = usable Orders per point.**

> **Slot efficiency = useful bodies, capabilities, and positions per Combat Group slot.**

> **Activation compression = useful actions or movements gained from each Order spent.**

Multi-body units can have exceptional slot and activation efficiency even when their raw Order generation is ordinary.

---

# 3. Synchronized models: the ARO-choice problem

One of the most important combat effects of standard Peripherals is that a reactive Trooper must choose **one** of the activated Controller/Peripheral models as the target of its ARO.

Meanwhile, the Controller and Peripheral are not required to choose the same target.

This creates an **ARO fork**.

A classic pattern is:

**Controller with conventional gun + cheap Peripheral with Direct Template Weapon**

Move both so that they threaten the same defender.

The defender now has to decide which active body to oppose.

If the defender shoots the Controller:

- the Controller's attack may become Face to Face;
- the Peripheral may still deliver an unopposed template or other attack.

If the defender shoots the Peripheral:

- the expendable body may die;
- the Controller's stronger attack may become a Normal Roll.

If the defender Dodges:

- the defender gives up the opportunity to shoot;
- but may evade both threats.

The synchronized package therefore does not need to guarantee damage to create value.

> **Forcing a dangerous enemy to Dodge instead of shooting is itself a successful outcome.**

This can allow another body to cross a lane, preserve the Controller, or make the next Order safer.

---

# 4. The Peripheral does not need good BS if its job is geometry

A cheap combat Peripheral is often strongest when its attack does not depend heavily on conventional Face-to-Face gunfighting.

Useful Peripheral weapons and roles include:

- Direct Template Weapons;
- shotguns at very close range;
- E/M or PARA threats;
- disposable weapons;
- close-combat engagement;
- mines or other area-control equipment when available;
- objective interaction or Forward Observer duties on appropriate profiles.

The Peripheral's function is often:

1. reach a different angle;
2. force a target-selection decision;
3. impose a Dodge;
4. trade itself for something more important;
5. occupy a dangerous position the Controller should not enter.

Therefore do not evaluate a synchronized body purely by its BS or weapon Burst.

> **A mediocre shooter can be an excellent Peripheral if it creates a threat the enemy cannot ignore while the Controller attacks from a different angle.**

---

# 5. Create forks, not clusters

The worst way to use a synchronized pair is frequently to move the two models shoulder-to-shoulder.

The important advantage is that they share an activation while occupying **different geometry**.

Useful patterns include:

### Opposite-side cornering

Controller approaches one side of a building while the Peripheral approaches the other.

The enemy cannot necessarily oppose both bodies with the same ARO.

### Range-band split

Keep the Controller in its preferred rifle/Spitfire/HMG band while pushing a template or shotgun Peripheral closer.

The enemy must solve two different engagement distances at once.

### Safe Controller, exposed Peripheral

Leave the expensive or Order-generating Controller behind Total Cover while the Peripheral crosses the dangerous lane.

### Objective split

The Controller moves toward a future objective while the Peripheral moves toward a flank, corner, or casualty.

Both models gain useful position from the same movement Order.

### Crossfire setup

Use one shared movement Order to establish future LoF from two directions rather than stacking both bodies behind the same piece of scenery.

The general rule is:

> **Same Order does not mean same route.**

---

# 6. Exploit every shared Move

Synchronized models create major movement compression.

Suppose a Controller and Peripheral both need to improve their positions. If three Orders move both bodies usefully, the army has effectively gained six meaningful model-moves from three Orders.

That is not literally three extra Orders, but the practical positional result can be similar.

Before spending an Order on a synchronized pair, ask:

> **What useful future position can each activated body gain from this Move?**

The Controller may move toward:

- a safer corner;
- a future objective;
- better range;
- a coherency anchor;
- the next attack route.

The Peripheral may move toward:

- a template attack;
- a flank;
- a recovery target;
- a blocking position;
- the next disposable trade.

Sometimes the Controller should remain still because preserving it is more important than movement compression. But wasting the Controller's movement opportunities by default gives up a large part of the mechanic's value.

---

# 7. Put the disposable body in danger first

Standard Peripheral packages usually have asymmetric risk.

Losing the Peripheral often removes only the secondary body. Losing or Isolating the Controller can shut down the entire standard Peripheral package by sending its Peripherals into Disconnected State.

This makes the Controller the **keystone**.

The natural risk hierarchy is:

> **Peripheral takes risk → Controller preserves the Order and command function.**

Good uses for expendable Peripheral risk include:

- crossing a dangerous ARO lane;
- entering template range;
- triggering or clearing a Mine when the exchange is worthwhile;
- engaging a shooter in CC;
- trading for a specialist, Hacker, Engineer, or other important support piece;
- forcing repeated Dodges;
- occupying a doorway or route;
- revealing hidden defensive structure.

Do not confuse "cheap" with "worthless," however. A Peripheral that still has a major future mission should not be thrown away merely because it is expendable in principle.

---

# 8. Controller safety includes hacking and ZoC, not only LoF

A Controller hidden behind Total Cover is not automatically safe.

Because the Controller activates with its Peripheral, consider every way the activation can create an enemy response.

Check:

- Line of Fire;
- Zone of Control;
- enemy Hacking Areas;
- nearby template geometry;
- Perimeter weapons;
- Mines;
- enemy CC threats;
- whether a movement path exposes the Controller even briefly.

This is especially important when the Controller is Hackable.

A Peripheral may be doing the visible attack several inches away while the opponent exploits the Controller's activation through a Repeater network.

Therefore the safe remote-attack question is:

> **Can I expose the Peripheral without exposing the Controller to a more valuable form of attack?**

---

# 9. Template vulnerability is the natural tax on extra bodies

Multi-body mechanics create more physical assets, but templates punish physical concentration.

If a Controller and Peripheral are both caught by the same template, the opponent may convert one attack into damage against multiple parts of the package.

This can reverse the normal efficiency advantage.

Avoid:

- Controller and Peripheral directly beside one another;
- lining both models along the same teardrop path;
- stacking several Peripherals behind one corner;
- clustering support bodies around the same casualty;
- parking a Servant immediately beside the TAG or REM it supports;
- moving several Control bodies through the same narrow template lane when only one needs exposure.

Use terrain and the full legal spacing available to separate them.

> **Coherency is a maximum radius to exploit, not a formation to fill tightly.**

The opponent should ideally have to choose between threatening the Controller **or** the Peripheral rather than catching both.

---

# 10. Peripheral (Synchronized): keep Coherency in mind before committing

Synchronized Peripherals must maintain Coherency with the Controller and check it at the relevant times defined by the rules.

This limits how aggressively the disposable body can be projected.

Before sending a synchronized Peripheral around a corner, ask:

1. Can it finish the Order still coherent?
2. Does the Controller need to move forward too?
3. Will moving the Controller expose the keystone?
4. Is the attack still worthwhile if the Peripheral cannot retreat?

A common mistake is seeing an excellent Peripheral attack angle that requires dragging the valuable Controller into an unsafe position.

The correct attack may instead be:

- a shorter Peripheral push;
- a different route;
- one setup Order first;
- or abandoning the attack entirely.

---

# 11. Peripheral (Control): treat it as a remote assault pack

Control Peripherals operate differently from a simple synchronized wingman.

The Peripherals form a **Control Unit** and use a designated **Spearhead** for Coherency. Each time the Controller and Control Unit activate, a Spearhead is designated for that Order.

When a declared Skill requires a target, only the Controller and the Spearhead perform that Skill; the other Control Peripherals Idle for that portion of the Order.

This suggests a strong tactical pattern:

> **Move the whole pack efficiently, but expose and fight with one expendable Spearhead at a time.**

Useful sequence:

1. move several Control bodies toward the attack area;
2. keep most behind Total Cover;
3. select the body with the best weapon, position, or expendability as Spearhead;
4. attack through it;
5. if it dies, rotate the next body into Spearhead on a later Order.

Do not expose every member merely because every member activates.

Think of the rear bodies as **reserve ammunition for the unit package**.

Control packages are especially valuable when different bodies solve different problems or when losing the current Spearhead does not meaningfully reduce the Controller's Order generation.

---

# 12. Peripheral (Servant): positional delegation

Servants are covered in detail in `../Support Architecture - Doctor Engineer Servants and Recovery.md`, but the multi-body perspective is important.

A Servant does not primarily add combat power.

It **delegates the physical location** from which a Doctor or Engineer can apply their Skill.

That converts:

**Doctor/Engineer must walk to casualty**

into:

**cheap remote body moves to casualty while Controller remains safe**

The effective savings may be several Orders.

This means the Servant's real value includes:

- movement Orders saved;
- reduced exposure of the valuable support specialist;
- broader recovery coverage;
- the ability to support two different flanks with two pre-positioned Servants;
- occasional secondary body utility.

With two Servants, place them in different likely recovery zones rather than beside one another.

A Servant can also have secondary value as:

- a screening base;
- a cheap body blocking a path;
- an additional CC participant when the situation justifies it;
- a body that forces the opponent to spend an Order removing the recovery node.

Do not sacrifice a Servant casually if it is the only practical recovery route for a premium model later.

---

# 13. Peripheral (Ancillary): delayed body projection

Ancillary Peripherals begin off-table and are deployed by their Controller using Place Deployable.

This changes the evaluation compared with a normal synchronized body.

The Controller can initially operate without the body on the table, then convert an Order into additional remote presence when the position becomes useful.

Strategic questions include:

- When is the extra body worth spending the deployment Skill?
- Where should it be placed so later activations generate value?
- Does deploying it interfere with the Controller's Fireteam role?
- Is keeping it undeployed strategically better for scenario scoring or flexibility?

The current FAQ also clarifies that an undeployed Ancillary does not count as Killed for scenario objectives; it only counts as Killed if deployed and later Dead or in a Null State at the end of the game.

Therefore Ancillary deployment is not always automatic.

> **Deploy it when its projected board value exceeds the opportunity cost of spending the Skill and changing the Controller's tactical status.**

---

# 14. Cyberplug in N5.3: do not use the old controller-dependency model blindly

N5.3 significantly changes how Cyberplugs should be conceptualized.

A Cyberplug can use:

- a **Connected Profile**; or
- an **Autonomous Profile**.

At the start of the Order or ARO, determine the relevant profile.

While Connected:

- the Cyberplug activates with the Controller;
- it declares the same Skills;
- the Controller normally performs Idle instead of that Skill;
- Doctor/Engineer has its specific exception.

While Autonomous:

- the Cyberplug can receive Orders from the Order Pool independently;
- it can declare its own AROs independently.

With two Cyberplugs, only one may use the Connected Profile at a time; the other operates autonomously.

Most importantly, if the Controller enters Isolated or a Null State, Cyberplugs switch to Autonomous Profiles instead of simply collapsing into the standard Disconnected behavior.

Strategic consequence:

> **Killing or Isolating the Controller of a Cyberplug package does not necessarily remove the remote bodies from useful play.**

Cyberplug packages therefore have more distributed resilience than ordinary Controller + Peripheral arrangements.

When using them, exploit that independence.

When fighting them, do not assume that removing the Controller solves the whole package.

---

# 15. N5.3 FAQ: one body failing a Skill does not stop the others

Current FAQ wording clarifies an important multi-body interaction.

If the Controller cannot legally perform the declared Skill because of Stunned, Immobilized, Engaged, or another failed Requirement, the Peripheral may still perform that Skill if it can.

The illegal body performs Idle while the legal body continues.

This matters strategically because disabling the Controller does not always prevent the current Peripheral activation from accomplishing the declared task.

Likewise, when planning an Order, one body's movement or state restriction does not necessarily invalidate the useful action of every other activated element.

Ask:

> **Which bodies can legally perform this Skill, and which will simply Idle?**

Do not abandon an otherwise useful Peripheral activation because one component cannot participate.

# 16. G: Jumper is distributed optionality, not activation compression

G: Jumper deserves inclusion in multi-body strategy, but it works almost opposite to Synchronized Peripherals.

A G: Jumper Trooper deploys multiple Proxies but provides one Order and activates only the Proxy carrying the Active Proxy Token.

During the Active Turn:

- choose whichever eligible Proxy should act;
- place the Active Proxy Token there;
- spend the Order on that body.

This means the strategic advantage is not "two bodies act for one Order."

It is:

> **The same Order can be spent from several different pre-positioned locations without paying Orders to move one model between those jobs.**

A G: Jumper package can therefore place:

- a long-range attacker in the rear;
- a midfield specialist near objectives;
- a support or close-range body elsewhere;

and then spend Orders through whichever body is currently relevant.

That is exceptional **positional optionality**.

---

# 17. Build G: Jumper Proxies for complementary jobs

Because only one Proxy acts at a time, several Proxies competing for the same role often waste the package's main advantage.

Strong combinations usually cover different problems.

Examples:

- long-range gun + midfield specialist + Engineer;
- Hidden/Marker-state shooter + objective runner + support piece;
- heavy attacker + Hacker + Forward Observer;
- defensive body + mission body + recovery body.

The list-building question is:

> **Does each Proxy give me a useful place to spend the shared Trooper's Order that the other Proxies cannot provide?**

This is role compression through physical distribution.

---

# 18. G: Jumper trades capability before it trades Orders

A major G: Jumper strength is that losing one Proxy does not necessarily remove the Trooper's Order.

The G: Jumper Trooper is considered a casualty only when all Proxies are in Isolated or Null States.

That makes individual Proxies unusually good trading pieces.

If one Proxy dies, you may lose:

- its points for Victory Point calculations;
- its weapon;
- its specialist role;
- its physical position;

but still retain the Trooper's Order through another functioning Proxy.

This distinction is critical:

> **Order resilience is not the same as points resilience or capability resilience.**

A Proxy may be strategically expendable from an Order-economy perspective while still being valuable because it carries the only Engineer, Hacker, long-range weapon, or scoring role in the package.

---

# 19. G: Jumper reactive limitation: pressure several Proxies at once

In the Reactive Turn, if several Proxies from the same G: Jumper Trooper receive AROs from the same enemy Order, only one can be selected as the Active Proxy.

The Active Proxy can react normally.

Inactive Proxies that react are restricted to Dodge or Reset.

This creates an important counterplay principle:

> **When possible, design an enemy Order that threatens more than one Proxy simultaneously.**

The G: Jumper player must then choose where the meaningful reactive capability exists for that Order.

Useful ways to create this pressure include:

- movement routes that enter LoF/ZoC of two Proxies;
- Coordinated Orders that create simultaneous threats;
- attacks whose geometry threatens different Proxy locations;
- forcing one Proxy to need a shooting ARO while another needs to defend itself at the same time.

This is the inverse of the synchronized-Peripheral ARO fork.

**Synchronized player:** presents several active bodies and forces the defender to choose a target.

**Opponent of G: Jumper:** creates one enemy Order that forces the Jumper player to choose which Proxy gets the unrestricted ARO.

---

# 20. G: Jumper and prepared defensive positions

A Proxy in Suppressive Fire remains in Suppressive Fire even when a different Proxy becomes Active.

This creates persistent prepared positions across the board.

A useful sequence can be:

1. establish Suppressive Fire with Proxy A;
2. later activate Proxy B for mission work or attacking;
3. retain Proxy A's defensive state for a future reactive situation.

This does not allow every Proxy to make unrestricted simultaneous AROs. The normal G: Jumper Active Proxy limitations still apply.

The value is instead **setup persistence**: one body can remain prepared while another receives active attention.

---

# 21. G: Jumper and Marker / Hidden Deployment states

Selecting a Proxy as Active does not by itself reveal a Proxy in Camouflaged, Impersonation, Holoecho, or Hidden Deployment State.

This makes hidden or Marker-state Proxies unusually flexible.

The opponent may know the overall G: Jumper package exists but still be uncertain about:

- which body will become relevant;
- which hidden location contains the dangerous Proxy;
- whether the next Order will emerge from the midfield, rear, or another prepared position.

This preserves **latent positional threat** without spending Orders moving a single model into place.

---

# 22. Forcing ARO choices with multiple bodies

The best multi-body attack asks the defender a question for which every answer has a cost.

Examples:

### Shoot the Controller

The defender protects itself from the high-value gunfighter but may accept an unopposed Peripheral attack.

### Shoot the Peripheral

The expendable body may die, but the Controller may receive a strong Normal Roll.

### Dodge

The defender may survive both threats but forfeits offensive retaliation and may concede position.

### Reset

Appropriate against hacking/state pressure, but may leave conventional weapon attacks unopposed.

### Ignore one angle to defend another

The defender survives the immediate attack but allows the second body to gain useful board position.

Therefore the active player's goal should not simply be:

> "How do I maximize my Burst?"

It should often be:

> **"How do I make every plausible ARO declaration strategically unpleasant?"**

Multi-body mechanics are excellent at creating these decision forks.

---

# 23. Expendable attacks: failure can still be efficient

A cheap Peripheral can justify attacks with mediocre success odds when the consequences of failure are small and the upside is large.

A 5–10 point secondary body attempting to trade into:

- a 30-point Hacker;
- an Engineer supporting a TAG;
- a key specialist;
- a Fireteam member holding bonuses together;
- a difficult ARO piece;

can be a good exchange even when the Peripheral is likely to die.

This is especially true if:

- the Controller remains safe;
- the Controller's Order survives;
- the attack also forces a Dodge or consumes enemy Orders;
- the Peripheral has already delivered most of its future value.

The evaluation should therefore include:

> **expected target value + Orders consumed + positional gain − future Peripheral value − risk to Controller**

Do not require a cheap secondary body to have premium gunfighting odds before using it aggressively.

---

# 24. Cheap Controllers can project disproportionate list value

A cheap Controller with a useful Peripheral package can appear modest on a profile-by-profile comparison yet produce much more table presence than another troop of similar cost.

Its effective value can include:

| Source of value | Strategic effect |
|---|---|
| **Slot compression** | several bodies consume one Combat Group Trooper slot |
| **Activation compression** | several useful movements or threats occur on one Order |
| **Threat projection** | cheap body operates farther forward than Controller |
| **Risk asymmetry** | Peripheral can be lost without necessarily losing the generated Order |
| **ARO-choice pressure** | defender must choose which active body to oppose |
| **Spatial coverage** | one list entry occupies several lanes/areas |
| **Screening** | extra base blocks movement and protects valuable pieces |
| **Role compression** | one package combines gun, template, specialist, or support roles |
| **Recovery reach** | Servant projects Doctor/Engineer without moving Controller |

The package also has real costs:

| Cost / tax | Strategic consequence |
|---|---|
| **Controller coupling** | standard Peripherals may collapse when Controller is Null/Isolated |
| **Coherency** | Synchronized bodies cannot separate without limit |
| **Template vulnerability** | several nearby bodies can multiply losses |
| **Fireteam / Coordination restrictions** | some other activation systems are unavailable |
| **VP exposure** | cheap secondary bodies can still surrender mission value |
| **movement complexity** | poor geometry can make the extra body obstruct rather than help |

A useful list-building shorthand is:

> **Multi-body package value = printed combat value + saved Orders + board presence + ARO pressure + slot efficiency − controller coupling − geometry/template tax.**

This is why a low-cost Controller with a threatening synchronized body can be much stronger than its conventional statline suggests.

---

# 25. Multi-body models as screens and physical obstacles

Every additional body has value even when it never attacks.

A Peripheral can physically:

- block a doorway;
- prevent easy base contact with the Controller;
- force an attacker to take a longer movement path;
- occupy a corner so an enemy cannot end movement there;
- stand between a warband and a premium target;
- protect a retreat route;
- create a speed bump before the opponent reaches the main defence.

Because the secondary body may be cheaper or more expendable than the Controller, this screening role can be efficient.

However, do not create template chains where the attacker can hit both screen and protected model.

A good screen forces an extra Order or attack.

A bad screen gives the opponent a two-for-one template.

---

# 26. Multi-body troops and Close Combat

Peripherals follow the relevant multiple-trooper Close Combat rules, so additional bodies can improve a CC engagement when properly positioned.

Strategic applications include:

- sending the cheap Peripheral into base contact first;
- following with the Controller when that improves the CC math;
- using the Peripheral simply to Engage a shooter and disable its ranged role;
- adding a secondary body to a fight that the opponent cannot efficiently escape.

But CC clustering increases exposure to:

- templates;
- counter-assault pieces;
- multiple-model casualties;
- difficult extraction afterward.

Use Peripheral-assisted CC when tying down the enemy is strategically valuable, not merely because multiple bodies can participate.

---

# 27. Reactive-turn use: separate ARO locations without overexposing the package

Standard Controller and Peripheral models can each generate their own ARO opportunities depending on LoF/ZoC, although the Peripheral rules constrain how their declarations interact.

This means deployment can create several reactive checkpoints from one list slot.

Useful deployment concept:

**Controller covers one approach → Peripheral covers a different short-range route**

The opponent must then spend Orders solving more than one physical location.

However, do not expose both simply to maximize the number of AROs.

The goal is **layered reactive cost**, not simultaneous sacrifice.

A cheap Peripheral hidden around a corner may be more valuable than one standing in LoF, because it threatens a template or Engage only after the attacker commits deeper.

---

# 28. When fighting Peripherals, identify the keystone

Against a standard Peripheral package, ask:

> **What happens if I remove the Controller instead of the forward body?**

The visible Peripheral may be the immediate threat, but the Controller often determines whether the whole package continues functioning.

Priority targets can include:

- Controller itself;
- Repeater enabling an Oblivion or other state attack on the Controller;
- Engineer who can reconnect or restore important pieces;
- coherency path that forces awkward repositioning;
- support structure keeping the Controller safe.

Do not spend several Orders hunting a 5-point Peripheral if one attack on the Controller effectively removes the whole package.

Exception: **Cyberplug** packages in N5.3 are less controller-dependent because Autonomous Profiles remain useful after the Controller becomes Null or Isolated.

---

# 29. When fighting Servants, attack the recovery network when appropriate

A Servant itself may be cheap, but its strategic value can be large if it is the only practical recovery route to a TAG, REM, HI, or key ARO piece.

Killing a Servant can therefore be equivalent to denying several future recovery Orders.

Good reasons to spend an Order removing a Servant include:

- it is positioned beside a premium Unconscious target;
- killing it forces the Doctor/Engineer to walk several Orders;
- the Controller has only one practical recovery node on that side of the table;
- the supported model is likely to stand up and create a major problem again.

Do not automatically hunt every Palbot or equivalent. Attack the recovery network when doing so meaningfully increases the opponent's future Order costs.

---

# 30. Mission and Victory Point considerations

Do not assume that an expendable secondary body is strategically free.

Peripherals with a listed points cost contribute to Victory Point and zone-control calculations according to the rules. G: Jumper Proxies also count their Cost Attributes separately for Victory Points.

Therefore distinguish:

- **Order loss**;
- **capability loss**;
- **Victory Point loss**;
- **zone-control loss**;
- **mission-function loss**.

A cheap Peripheral may be safe to trade from an Order-economy perspective but still matter late in the game because its body contributes to a zone or because scenario scoring punishes casualties.

Ancillary Peripherals have their specific FAQ interaction: an undeployed Ancillary does not count as Killed; once deployed it can count as Killed if it ends Dead or in a Null State as defined by the current ruling.

Turn 3 therefore changes the trade calculus.

Early game:

> trade disposable bodies for enemy Orders and infrastructure.

Late game:

> preserve bodies that contribute directly to scenario score, zones, survival, or Classified requirements.

---

# 31. Common mistakes

## Moving every body together

This gives up angle pressure and increases template vulnerability.

**Better:** use shared Orders to occupy different useful routes while maintaining required Coherency.

## Risking the Controller to enable a marginal Peripheral attack

The package's keystone is often worth much more than the disposable body.

**Better:** calculate Controller exposure before committing.

## Evaluating a Peripheral only by its BS

Its main job may be forcing Dodge, creating a template threat, screening, or trading.

**Better:** evaluate geometry and ARO pressure.

## Treating a Servant as a spare base beside the Doctor

This wastes its positional projection.

**Better:** pre-position it near likely casualties.

## Putting two Servants in the same place

Only one activates at a time and both cover the same recovery area.

**Better:** create separate recovery zones.

## Treating G: Jumper like a Fireteam

Only one Proxy receives the active Order.

**Better:** use Proxies to provide several places where the same Order can be spent.

## Building redundant G: Jumper bodies

Three bodies that all want the same Orders in the same range bracket reduce positional optionality.

**Better:** choose complementary jobs.

## Assuming killing a Cyberplug Controller shuts everything down

N5.3 Autonomous Profiles change that interaction.

**Better:** evaluate the remaining autonomous bodies before selecting the target.

## Ignoring mission value because the Peripheral is cheap

The body may still contribute points, zones, or objective denial.

**Better:** trade differently on Turn 3 than Turn 1.

# 32. List-building evaluation checklist

When considering a Peripheral or multi-body package, ask:

1. **How many physical bodies do I receive for this Combat Group slot?**
2. **Do those bodies create genuinely different positions or roles?**
3. **Can one Order move or threaten with more than one useful body?**
4. **Does the Peripheral force an ARO decision rather than merely add a weak attack?**
5. **Can the cheap body take risks while the Controller remains safe?**
6. **What happens if the Controller becomes Null or Isolated?**
7. **Is the package unusually vulnerable to templates or coherency pressure?**
8. **Does the Controller become exposed to hacking when the Peripheral activates?**
9. **Can every shared Move improve more than one body's future position?**
10. **Does a Servant save enough movement Orders to justify its points and deployment position?**
11. **For G: Jumper, do the Proxies cover complementary jobs and locations?**
12. **If one Proxy dies, what capability is lost even though the Order survives?**
13. **Can the opponent pressure several Proxies in one enemy Order?**
14. **Does the cheap extra body matter for zones, Victory Points, or Turn-3 scoring?**
15. **Would another independent Trooper provide more useful Order generation than this multi-body package?**

# 33. Core strategic principles

The most important practical rules are:

1. **Count Orders, slots, and bodies separately.** Multi-body units derive value from breaking the normal one-Trooper/one-position relationship.
2. **Create forks, not clusters.** Use different angles and ranges so the opponent must choose which threat to answer.
3. **Expose the disposable body before the keystone.** Preserve the Controller unless its own participation is worth the risk.
4. **Make every shared Move useful.** Activation compression is partly movement compression.
5. **Protect the Controller from hacking as well as guns.** Remote-looking attacks can still expose the Controller through ZoC or Repeaters.
6. **Spread against templates.** Extra bodies become a liability when one template catches several of them.
7. **Rotate Control Spearheads.** Treat the pack as a sequence of expendable forward bodies, not a blob.
8. **Use Servants as remote infrastructure.** Their real value is saved movement and safe recovery projection.
9. **Treat G: Jumper as positional optionality.** One Order can originate from whichever pre-positioned Proxy currently matters.
10. **Pressure several Proxies at once when fighting G: Jumper.** Only one receives the unrestricted ARO for that enemy Order.
11. **Remember N5.3 Cyberplug autonomy.** Removing the Controller may not remove the remote bodies.
12. **Trade according to mission timing.** A cheap extra body can be disposable early and critical for scoring late.

The central multi-body question is therefore:

> **How can I make one list slot threaten, support, or occupy more than one part of the table without allowing the opponent to remove all of that value with one attack?**
