# Deployables and Area Control

## Core principle

A deployable succeeds when it changes the opponent's route, Skill choice, attack sequence, or Order budget — even if it never inflicts a Wound.

Do not evaluate deployables only as extra attacks. Evaluate them as **space-control and Order-tax pieces**.

The most important distinction is:

- **Attacking space** means trying to damage something now.
- **Threatening space** means making the opponent spend Orders, choose worse routes, expose different models, or accept bad tactical forks before they can use that space.

A mine that never detonates but causes three enemy models to avoid a corridor may have done more work than a mine that kills one disposable Warband.

## 1. Control destinations, not current positions

Do not ask only:

> Can this mine protect my model?

Ask:

> Where does the enemy actually want to finish movement?

Good deployable locations are usually:

- the far side of a corner;
- behind an objective;
- at the exit of stairs or ladders;
- on likely rooftop access points;
- behind the cover an attacker wants to occupy;
- along pursuit and retreat routes;
- in front of support pieces only when that is also a useful route;
- in likely DZ-entry lanes for Airborne Deployment or very fast attackers.

A deployable beside the model it is defending often gives the attacker one obvious problem. A deployable covering the **space the attacker must enter to reach that model** is usually stronger.

## 2. Cover corners rather than models

Mines and similar deployables are excellent tools for owning corners.

The goal is not necessarily to place the mine beside the defended trooper. Instead, place it so the enemy must enter its Trigger Area after rounding the corner or crossing the doorway.

```text
enemy
  ↓
█████████ corner
        ↘ threatened exit
          X mine
```

A strong placement makes the opponent deal with the deployable only after committing movement toward the useful position.

This is especially effective when the mine itself is hidden from the attacker's original firing position.

## 3. Defend objectives from behind

A common strong pattern is:

```text
enemy approach
      ↓
 [ objective ]
      |
 [ mine / repeater / perimeter ]
      |
 [ supporting defence ]
```

Placing a mine directly in front of an objective may allow the attacker to clear it before reaching the scoring position.

Placing it **behind or beside the objective** can make the scoring position itself dangerous.

The attacker may then have to choose between:

- clearing the deployable and moving past the objective;
- interacting with the objective while exposed to the deployable;
- Dodging instead of interacting or attacking;
- approaching from a worse route;
- spending extra Orders repositioning after scoring.

The objective becomes a trap rather than merely a point you are guarding.

## 4. Forcing Dodge is already value

Do not evaluate a mine only by whether it inflicts damage.

A mine has already produced value if it makes the opponent:

- spend an Order Discovering it;
- shoot it instead of advancing;
- Dodge instead of attacking;
- take a longer route;
- send a disposable model first;
- expose an Engineer or Deactivator carrier;
- delay a push until another troop arrives;
- stop short of an objective;
- abandon the route entirely.

Forcing a high-value attack piece to spend an Order Dodging can be better than killing a cheap model.

## 5. Layer threats that require different responses

Overlapping two identical threats is useful, but the strongest defence usually combines **different kinds of problems**.

Examples:

### Mine + Repeater + Hacker

The enemy enters a space where:

- the Mine encourages Dodge;
- the Hacker encourages Reset or another anti-hacking response;
- the mission may require interacting instead;

There is no single ideal response.

### Mine + Armed Turret

The attacker may need to choose between:

- shooting the Turret and accepting the Mine;
- Dodging and letting the Turret attack;
- retreating;
- spending Orders finding a different angle.

### Perimeter weapon + Repeater

- Boost pressure encourages Dodge.
- Hacking pressure encourages Reset.

### Mine + direct-template or close-range defender

The attacker can clear one threat only to remain exposed to another.

The general rule is:

> **Layer Dodge threats, Reset threats, gunfighting threats, and movement constraints rather than simply stacking copies of the same threat.**

## 6. Mines: close the attractive route

Mines are often best used to cover the route the opponent would otherwise prefer.

Good mine locations include:

- building corners;
- alleys;
- stairs;
- ladder exits;
- narrow gaps;
- objective approaches;
- rooftop access points;
- likely Super-Jump or Climb landing areas;
- retreat paths;
- backfield support access;
- the reverse side of an ARO piece;
- likely routes into your Deployment Zone.

Do not necessarily mine the route already covered by your strongest gun.

Mine the **easy alternative** so the enemy is pushed toward the gun.

## 7. Mines as Order-tax pieces

A mine can tax multiple Orders even without detonating.

Typical sequence:

1. Enemy approaches.
2. Enemy identifies or suspects a Mine.
3. Enemy stops outside the Trigger Area.
4. Enemy spends an Order Discovering, shooting, Dodging, or repositioning.
5. Enemy spends another Order resuming the attack.

If a cheap deployable turns a two-Order attack into a four-Order attack, it has done substantial work.

This matters especially in Turns 2 and 3 when Order budgets are lower and scoring deadlines are tighter.

## 8. Drop Bears: edit the table during your active turn

Do not think of Drop Bears only as grenades that become Mines.

Think of them as a way to **rewrite the terrain around an enemy position**.

Strong uses include throwing one:

- behind the target before attacking;
- across the target's retreat route;
- behind an objective;
- onto the far side of a corner;
- near an Engineer or Doctor;
- into a route another enemy model will need next turn;
- behind your own attacker before withdrawing.

A useful pattern is:

```text
Mine
  X
  |
enemy -------- your attacker
```

The target can still fight, but retreat and reinforcement now become more difficult.

Because a newly placed Drop Bear is infrastructure for later Orders rather than an immediate same-Order explosion, plan the **next activation** when placing it.

## 9. Mine Dispensers: ranged terrain modification

Mine Dispensers let a model seed dangerous areas without physically moving into them.

Treat them as strategic weapons for:

- placing Mines behind enemies;
- blocking retreat routes;
- defending future objectives;
- controlling upper levels or inaccessible corners;
- preparing the midfield before withdrawing;
- creating a screen behind an advancing attack piece.

A fast Mine Dispenser platform can attack one part of the table while preparing another part for the Reactive Turn.

The most important question is often not:

> What can I hit with this Mine Dispenser?

but:

> Where can I place a future problem the enemy cannot conveniently clear?

## 10. Perimeter weapons: project the device forward

Do not use Perimeter weapons only as personal bodyguards for their carrier.

Because the device itself can often be placed forward within the allowed deployment area, use it to project the carrier's control into:

- doorways;
- corridors;
- objective approaches;
- corners;
- building exits;
- likely DZ-intrusion paths.

Instead of:

```text
Perimeter
    |
carrier
```

think:

```text
carrier -------- perimeter ===== threatened route
```

The device becomes the forward edge of your defence.

## 11. Perimeter geometry matters

A Perimeter weapon that technically covers a large Zone but cannot physically reach the target because of walls, sealed rooms, or awkward vertical geometry may control much less space than it appears to.

Position Perimeter devices where terrain funnels enemies into valid paths.

Particularly strong locations:

- doors;
- stairwells;
- narrow alleys;
- open ground immediately after a corner;
- objective rooms with limited exits.

## 12. Repeaters are virtual minefields

Repeaters control space without attacking by themselves.

Their real value is:

> **Repeater × hackers × enemy Hackable models × route importance.**

A Repeater backed by no meaningful hackers is a weak deterrent.

A Repeater backed by strong Oblivion, Immobilization, or Killer Hacking coverage can make an entire route hostile to HI, TAGs, REMs, and enemy hackers.

Use Repeaters to cover:

- objectives;
- the cover a TAG wants to occupy;
- narrow central passages;
- stair and elevator exits;
- rooftop access;
- likely retreat positions;
- movement corridors toward your DZ.

A Repeater that the enemy never enters may still have succeeded if it forced them to reroute.

## 13. Deployable Repeaters: put hacking where movement ends

Do not automatically place a Deployable Repeater as far forward as possible.

Place it where enemy Hackable units are likely to **finish movement**.

Good spots include:

- immediately behind objectives;
- behind midfield cover;
- around corners where TAGs or HI need to stop;
- beside narrow passages;
- near the far side of a building entrance;
- near likely end-of-turn defensive positions.

The best Deployable Repeater is one that makes the enemy's desirable final position tactically unacceptable.

## 14. Repeaters become stronger when combined with physical threats

A Repeater by itself often has straightforward counterplay.

A Repeater covered by a Mine or Perimeter weapon creates a much harder problem.

Example:

```text
corner
  |
Mine ---- Repeater zone ---- objective
                      |
                    Hacker
```

The enemy cannot simply walk an Engineer forward and solve the network without also interacting with the physical defence.

## 15. Armed Turrets are speed bumps, not anchors

Treat Armed Turrets as disposable autonomous ARO pieces.

Their job is usually to:

- force one additional clearance Order;
- punish careless movement;
- protect a short crossing;
- cover an objective;
- defend a backfield corner;
- discourage a fast DZ raid;
- make an attacker reveal from Marker State at an inconvenient time.

Do not expect them to function like a durable Total Reaction REM.

They are usually better when they control **small, important spaces** rather than long open fire lanes.

## 16. Restrict a Turret's exposure

A 360° reactive piece does not need panoramic visibility.

Place the Turret so terrain exposes it only to the area you want it to defend.

Good:

```text
████████ building
█ turret X ---> short crossing
████████
```

Weak:

```text
X -------------------------------- open table
```

The second placement allows the opponent's best gunfighter to engage the Turret from an ideal range.

The first forces the attacker to enter the local defensive network before dealing with it.

## 17. Current Armed Turret reload rule

Armed Turrets are **Non-Reloadable**.

Do not plan around Baggage replenishing them.

Use them as one-use positional assets and place them where their single deployment will produce the highest Order tax.

## 18. Disco Balls: temporary geometry

Disco Balls should be thought of as temporary terrain rather than damage tools.

Their purpose is to remove or alter Lines of Fire so your army can:

- cross open lanes;
- reach an objective;
- retreat safely;
- isolate one defender from supporting AROs;
- split an enemy ARO network;
- create a safe scoring corridor;
- protect a Turn 3 objective push.

The question is not:

> What can the Disco Ball kill?

It is:

> Which enemy Lines of Fire can I temporarily delete?

A well-placed Disco Ball can turn a defended objective into a local fight instead of a whole-table fight.

## 19. Build deployable depth

Do not stack every deployable in one small area.

A stronger defence often looks like:

```text
ENEMY
  ↓
Mine

       Repeater

              Perimeter

                     Turret

                            DZ
```

Each layer asks a new question.

This produces **graceful degradation**:

- clearing the Mine does not clear the Repeater;
- clearing the Repeater does not remove the Perimeter threat;
- crossing the Perimeter area still exposes the attacker to the Turret or other AROs.

The enemy cannot solve the entire defence with one successful gunfight.

## 20. Protect retreat routes before attacking

Deployables are excellent tools for preparing the exit of an active-turn attack piece.

A useful sequence is:

1. advance;
2. deploy Mine or Repeater;
3. attack;
4. retreat behind the new control piece.

Now the opponent's counterattack must first cross the space you prepared.

This is especially strong on Turn 1, where the goal of an attack run is often not simply to kill models but to finish in a position that remains difficult to punish.

## 21. Make pursuit inefficient

Without a deployable:

```text
Move → Move → Shoot
```

With a mine covering the pursuit route:

```text
Move → clear / Discover / Dodge / reroute → Move → Shoot
```

That extra Order may be the difference between your attacker surviving and dying.

A cheap deployable can therefore act as an indirect defensive buff for an expensive attack piece.

## 22. Reload and Baggage interaction

Baggage can replenish compatible Disposable weapons and equipment according to the current Reload rules.

This can make a Baggage REM into a logistics hub for troops carrying:

- Mines;
- Drop Bears;
- Deployable Repeaters;
- Pitchers;
- other reloadable Disposable equipment or weapons.

Important distinctions:

- Reload replenishes the **carrier's expended Disposable uses**.
- It does not duplicate or refill pieces already sitting on the table.
- Non-Reloadable equipment such as current Armed Turrets cannot be replenished this way.

This creates a useful recurring pattern:

```text
deploy → retreat → reload → redeploy
```

## 23. Keep Baggage close enough to matter

A theoretical reload is worthless if the carrier spends most of a turn walking back to the Deployment Zone.

A Baggage piece supporting a deployable-heavy force should often sit:

- safe from easy attack;
- one movement layer behind the active fighting line;
- near routes used by your deployable carriers.

A mobile Baggage REM can gradually advance behind the army and keep disposable infrastructure relevant throughout the game.

## 24. Deactivator counterplay

Deactivator is one of the cleanest answers to enemy deployables.

Therefore, do not merely protect the deployable itself.

Protect the **approach the Deactivator user must take**.

Force the Engineer or other Deactivator carrier to cross:

- another Mine;
- a Repeater network;
- a Perimeter weapon;
- an ARO lane;
- a direct-template threat;
- an exposed objective area.

If the enemy spends several Orders moving an Engineer forward to remove a cheap deployable, you may already have gained value.

## 25. Use deployables to bait valuable support models

An Engineer moving forward to clear a Mine, Turret, Repeater, or other device may expose a much more strategically important model than the deployable itself.

Possible sequence:

1. enemy sees cheap deployable;
2. enemy sends Engineer or Deactivator carrier;
3. several Orders are spent approaching;
4. support model becomes exposed;
5. you attack the Engineer;
6. enemy TAG, REM network, or other machinery loses support.

The deployable has shaped the target priority of the opponent.

## 26. Expect sacrificial clearing pieces

Opponents will often use:

- cheap Warbands;
- Peripheral models;
- cheap REMs;
- expendable skirmishers;
- disposable troops;

as mine sweepers or perimeter triggers.

Do not build a defence where one cheap sacrificial model removes your entire area-control plan.

Instead, create layers so that sacrificing one model only clears **one layer**.

Example:

```text
Mine → Repeater → hidden ARO → DZ defence
```

A cheap model may clear the Mine, but the valuable attacker behind it still must enter the hacking network and hidden defensive layer.

## 27. Make the opponent choose what to sacrifice first

A good deployable network creates sequencing pressure.

The opponent must decide:

- send a Warband first and lose it to the Mine;
- send the Engineer first and expose support;
- send the TAG first and enter the Repeater;
- send a Camo marker and spend Orders revealing or maneuvering;
- abandon the route entirely.

The more specialised answers the opponent needs, the more Orders your defence consumes.

## 28. Marker ambiguity multiplies Mine value

A Mine marker is more dangerous when it exists among genuine Camouflage markers.

The opponent may not know whether a marker represents:

- a Mine;
- a specialist;
- an attack piece;
- a hidden defensive unit waiting to reveal.

That uncertainty creates additional Order tax and slows aggressive movement.

Use Camo and Mine markers together to make the opponent solve **information problems** before solving the physical defence.

## 29. Threatening space versus attacking

This is the central deployable concept.

### Actually attacking

You activate a weapon, roll dice, and try to remove something.

Value depends on the immediate exchange.

### Threatening space

You create an area the opponent cannot use efficiently.

Value comes from:

- diverted routes;
- delayed attacks;
- extra Orders;
- forced Dodge or Reset;
- sacrificial clearing models;
- exposed Engineers;
- abandoned objectives;
- awkward ending positions;
- prevented pursuit;
- preserved friendly models.

A deployable that controls space for two turns without triggering can be more valuable than one that explodes immediately.

## 30. Using deployables to consume Orders without detonating

The ideal deployable often creates a **credible threat that the opponent must respect but cannot efficiently remove**.

Examples:

- a Mine behind an objective that causes the enemy to approach from the other side;
- a Repeater covering the best TAG position;
- a Perimeter weapon covering a doorway the enemy chooses not to enter;
- a Turret controlling a short crossing that makes the opponent climb onto another level;
- a Disco Ball enabling you to bypass several AROs without needing to kill them.

In all of these cases, the deployable's value comes from movement and Order distortion rather than casualties.

## 31. Common mistakes

### Placing deployables beside the model being defended

This frequently lets the opponent solve both with the same attack angle.

**Better:** cover the route or destination.

### Stacking identical threats

Two or three Mines are useful, but they all invite the same answer.

**Better:** mix Dodge, Reset, shooting, and movement pressure.

### Exposing Turrets to long-range gunfighters

This turns an Order-tax piece into an easy target.

**Better:** restrict LoF to locally important crossings.

### Putting all defence on the front edge

Once the first layer dies, the table opens.

**Better:** build depth.

### Deploying only for the current turn

Area control is often strongest when it shapes the opponent's **next turn**.

**Better:** think about pursuit, scoring routes, and likely end positions.

### Ignoring Deactivator access

A well-placed device with an easy, safe Deactivator approach is much weaker than it appears.

**Better:** cover the clearing route.

### Assuming every Disposable tool can be reloaded

Some are Non-Reloadable.

**Better:** check the profile and current rules before building Baggage plans around it.

## 32. Practical placement checklist

Before placing a deployable, ask:

1. **What exact space am I trying to control?**
2. **Why does the enemy want to enter that space?**
3. **Can they remove this deployable safely from outside that space?**
4. **Can I hide the deployable while exposing its threat area?**
5. **Does another threat punish the obvious counter?**
6. **Am I covering the destination rather than merely the current enemy position?**
7. **Can the opponent clear everything with one sacrificial model?**
8. **Does this protect an objective, attacker retreat, or future scoring route?**
9. **If they ignore it, have I still gained something?**
10. **If they use a Deactivator, what exposes the Deactivator user?**
11. **Is this reloadable, and is Baggage close enough for reload to matter?**
12. **Would placing this one layer farther back create more Order tax?**

## 33. Compact doctrine

1. **Control desirable destinations, not current enemy positions.**
2. **Cover corners and exits rather than standing beside friendly models.**
3. **Place deployables behind or beside objectives when that makes scoring itself dangerous.**
4. **Layer threats that require different responses.**
5. **Use Mines and Perimeter weapons to force Dodge.**
6. **Use Repeaters to make movement by Hackable troops strategically expensive.**
7. **Use Armed Turrets as local speed bumps, not long-range anchors.**
8. **Use Disco Balls to alter Line-of-Fire geometry rather than to seek damage.**
9. **Protect retreat and pursuit routes, not only the Deployment Zone.**
10. **Make Deactivator users expose themselves to clear your infrastructure.**
11. **Use Baggage to reload compatible Disposable tools, but remember that Armed Turrets are Non-Reloadable.**
12. **Build defensive depth so one sacrificial model cannot clear the network.**
13. **Measure deployables in enemy Orders consumed and routes denied, not just models killed.**
14. **A deployable that never detonates can still be one of the most valuable pieces on the table.**
